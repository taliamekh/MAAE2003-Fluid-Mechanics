Ch 1 — Fluid Properties & Pressure

Density (Continuum Definition)
\[ \rho = \lim_{\delta V \to \delta V^*} \frac{\delta m}{\delta V} \]

What it means: Density is the mass per unit volume, defined at the smallest volume element \(\delta V^*\) where molecular fluctuations average out and a stable macroscopic value emerges.

Origin: Derived from the continuum assumption — at volumes larger than \(\delta V^* \sim 10^{-9}\) mm³ (containing ~\(3 \times 10^7\) molecules of air at STP), density is a smooth function of position, enabling differential calculus.

When to use: Any problem requiring fluid density as a point property. Underlies every governing equation in the course — continuity, momentum, energy.

When NOT to use: Does not apply at molecular scales (e.g., rarefied gas dynamics where mean free path ≈ characteristic length). Also breaks down at macroscopic scales where spatial non-uniformity matters (use local density instead of bulk average).

Use cases: "Find the density of water at 20°C" → look up \(\rho = 998\) kg/m³. "Is the continuum assumption valid?" → check if the problem length scale ≫ mean free path.

💡 Insider Tips

  • 🔑 Every RTT problem starts the same way: draw the control volume, label every inlet and outlet, identify what crosses each surface, write assumptions (steady? incompressible? inviscid?). Then pick the right RTT form.
  • 🔑 Bernoulli has strict conditions: steady, incompressible, inviscid, along a streamline, no shaft work, no heat transfer. If ANY of these is violated, you can't use it — you need the full energy equation instead.
  • 🔑 Sign conventions kill: in linear momentum, forces and velocities have directions. Pick a coordinate system FIRST and be consistent. The reaction force on the fluid is opposite to the reaction on the structure.
  • 🔑 Venturi vs Orifice vs Stagnation Tube: know what each one measures and how the governing equation changes. Venturi/orifice give flow rate from pressure difference; stagnation tube gives velocity at a point.
  • 🔑 Continuity is your setup equation: almost every numerical problem needs \(\dot{m}_{\text{in}} = \dot{m}_{\text{out}}\) (steady) or \(\dot{m}_{\text{in}} - \dot{m}_{\text{out}} = \frac{dm_{\text{CV}}}{dt}\) (unsteady) first, before applying Bernoulli or momentum.
  • 🔑 MCQ strategy: read the equation sheet before starting. For each MCQ, identify which formula applies, check units, and eliminate wrong answers. Don't overthink — if two options differ by a sign, check your coordinate convention.
  • 🔑 For angular momentum: the moment arm matters. Take moments about a point that eliminates unknown forces (e.g., a pivot or where a reaction force acts).
  • 🔑 90 minutes goes fast. Do the questions you're confident in first. Come back to tough ones. Don't spend 15 minutes stuck on one problem.

🎯 Problem Type → Strategy

Problem Looks Like…Use…Key Formula
Find velocity or pressure at a point in a flow with known conditions elsewhere Bernoulli's Equation + Continuity \(\frac{p_1}{\rho g} + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} + z_1 = \frac{p_2}{\rho g} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} + z_2\)
Venturi meter / orifice meter — find flow rate from pressure difference Bernoulli + Continuity between throat and approach \(Q = A_2\sqrt{\frac{2(p_1 - p_2)}{\rho\!\left[\left(\frac{A_2}{A_1}\right)^{\!2} - 1\right]}}\) (or with manometer Δh)
Find stagnation pressure / velocity from a Pitot tube Stagnation tube equation \(p_0 = p + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2\) → \(V = \sqrt{2(p_0 - p)/\rho}\)
Tank draining / filling — find rate of mass change RTT — Mass (unsteady continuity) \(\frac{dm_{\text{CV}}}{dt} = \dot{m}_{\text{in}} - \dot{m}_{\text{out}}\)
Steady flow with multiple inlets/outlets — find an unknown velocity or area RTT — Mass (steady continuity) \(\sum \rho_i A_i V_i\big|_{\text{in}} = \sum \rho_j A_j V_j\big|_{\text{out}}\)
Find force on a pipe bend, nozzle, plate, or vane RTT — Linear Momentum \(\sum \vec{F} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\int_{\text{CV}} \rho\vec{V}\,dV + \int_{\text{CS}} \rho\vec{V}(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\,dA\)
Force on a sluice gate or hydraulic jump Bernoulli (2 points) + Momentum (CV around gate) Combine Bernoulli for velocities, then momentum for force. Hydrostatic pressure terms on both sides.
Find torque/moment on a sprinkler, turbine, or rotating device RTT — Angular Momentum \(\sum \vec{M} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\int_{\text{CV}} \vec{r}\times\rho\vec{V}\,dV + \int_{\text{CS}} \vec{r}\times\rho\vec{V}(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\,dA\)
"What type of flow line is this?" / "When do they coincide?" Definitions Pathline = particle trajectory. Streamline = tangent to velocity field at an instant. Streakline = locus of particles from one point. All coincide in steady flow.

⚠ Common Traps

Using Bernoulli When Conditions Aren't Met
Bernoulli requires: steady, incompressible, inviscid, along a streamline, no shaft work. If there's a pump, friction, or unsteady flow — use the energy equation instead. Always state your assumptions explicitly.
Wrong Sign on Momentum Flux
The CS integral uses \(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n}\): positive for outflow, negative for inflow. The velocity vector \(\vec{V}\) keeps its own direction — don't flip it. Set up a clear coordinate system first.
Forgetting Pressure Forces on CV
In momentum problems, gauge pressure acts on every CV surface open to the atmosphere = 0 gauge, but pipes and nozzles have non-zero pressure. Don't forget the \(p \cdot A\) force terms.
Mixing Up ṁ and Q
\(\dot{m} = \rho Q = \rho A V\). If the fluid is incompressible and density is uniform, Q is conserved. But ṁ is ALWAYS conserved (even for compressible). Use ṁ for momentum; Q for Bernoulli.
Ignoring Gauge vs Absolute Pressure
Bernoulli and momentum equations work with either — but you must be consistent. If the problem gives manometer readings, those give gauge pressures. Atmospheric pressure cancels if all openings are at \(p_{\text{atm}}\).
Wrong Moment Arm in Angular Momentum
The moment is \(\vec{r} \times \vec{F}\). Make sure \(\vec{r}\) is measured from your chosen moment center to the point of application. Taking moments about a clever point (e.g., where unknown forces act) simplifies the problem.

Ch 1 — Fluid Properties & Pressure

Establishes the foundational definitions and assumptions that underpin every equation in the course — what a fluid is, how we treat it mathematically, and the three branches we will study.

Classification of Matter
What:
Matter classified as solid, liquid, gas, or plasma based on molecular bonding and response to shear stress.
Why:
The defining property of a fluid is that it cannot resist shear stress — it deforms continuously. Solids resist shear by deforming to equilibrium.
How:
Liquids have constant volume/density and form a free surface. Gases expand to fill their container and have no definite volume. Both are fluids.
Trap:
Don't confuse "cannot resist shear" with "has no viscosity." Fluids still resist shear through viscous effects — they just never reach static equilibrium under shear.
The Continuum Assumption
What:
Treat fluid properties as smooth, continuous functions of position rather than tracking individual molecules.
Why:
Enables the use of differential calculus — partial derivatives, integrals, and governing PDEs — for engineering-scale analysis.
How:
\(\rho = \lim_{\delta V \to \delta V^*} \frac{\delta m}{\delta V}\) where \(\delta V^* \sim 10^{-9}\) mm³ gives a stable local density.
Trap:
Fails in rarefied gas flows (high altitude, vacuum systems) where the mean free path approaches the problem's characteristic length.
Branches of Fluid Mechanics
What:
Three branches — hydrostatics (fluids at rest), kinematics (geometry of motion), and fluid dynamics (forces causing acceleration).
Why:
Structures the entire course: Ch 1–2 cover statics, Ch 4 covers kinematics, Ch 5–6 cover dynamics via the Reynolds Transport Theorem.

Classification of matter → shear stress concept reappears in viscosity (Ch 1.2) and Newton's law of viscosity

Continuum assumption → justifies every differential equation in Ch 3–6

Density definition → used directly in hydrostatics (Ch 2), Bernoulli (Ch 3), and RTT (Ch 5)

Ch 1 — Fluid Properties & Pressure

Density (Continuum Definition)
\[ \rho = \lim_{\delta V \to \delta V^*} \frac{\delta m}{\delta V} \]

What it means: Density is the mass per unit volume, defined at the smallest volume element \(\delta V^*\) where molecular fluctuations average out and a stable macroscopic value emerges.

Origin: Derived from the continuum assumption — at volumes larger than \(\delta V^* \sim 10^{-9}\) mm³ (containing ~\(3 \times 10^7\) molecules of air at STP), density is a smooth function of position, enabling differential calculus.

When to use: Any problem requiring fluid density as a point property. Underlies every governing equation in the course — continuity, momentum, energy.

When NOT to use: Does not apply at molecular scales (e.g., rarefied gas dynamics where mean free path ≈ characteristic length). Also breaks down at macroscopic scales where spatial non-uniformity matters (use local density instead of bulk average).

Use cases: "Find the density of water at 20°C" → look up \(\rho = 998\) kg/m³. "Is the continuum assumption valid?" → check if the problem length scale ≫ mean free path.

\[ \rho = \lim_{\delta V \to \delta V^*} \frac{\delta m}{\delta V} \]

Mass per unit volume at the continuum scale. Valid when problem length scale ≫ mean free path (~\(10^{-9}\) mm³ threshold).

\[ \rho = \lim_{\delta V \to \delta V^*} \frac{\delta m}{\delta V} \]
Density #2
\[ \rho = \frac{m}{V} \]

What it means: Mass of fluid per unit volume — the most fundamental intensive property.

Origin: Direct definition. For incompressible fluids \(\rho = \text{const}\); for gases, \(\rho\) varies with \(p\) and \(T\) via the ideal gas law.

When to use: Every governing equation — continuity, momentum, energy, Bernoulli. Unit conversion problems. Buoyancy calculations.

When NOT to use: Don't confuse with specific weight (\(\gamma = \rho g\)) or specific gravity (\(SG = \rho/\rho_W\)).

\[ \rho = \frac{m}{V} \]

Mass per unit volume. SI: kg/m³. BG: slugs/ft³.

\[ \rho = \frac{m}{V} \]
Specific Weight #3
\[ \gamma = \rho g \]

What it means: Weight of fluid per unit volume — density scaled by gravitational acceleration.

When to use: Hydrostatic pressure problems (\(p = \gamma h\)), buoyancy, manometers. Often more convenient than \(\rho\) in BG units.

When NOT to use: Don't use interchangeably with density — \(\gamma\) includes gravity.

\[ \gamma = \rho g \]

Weight per unit volume. SI: N/m³. BG: lbf/ft³.

\[ \gamma = \rho g \]
Specific Gravity #4
\[ SG = \frac{\rho}{\rho_W} = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma_W} \]

What it means: Dimensionless ratio of a fluid's density to water's density at 4°C (\(\rho_W = 1000\;\text{kg/m}^3\)).

When to use: Quick comparison with water. Buoyancy (SG > 1 → sinks). Manometer problems involving multiple fluids.

When NOT to use: Never use SG in place of \(\rho\) in equations — multiply by \(\rho_W\) first.

\[ SG = \frac{\rho}{\rho_W} \]

Dimensionless density ratio. Reference: water at 4°C, 101.3 kPa.

\[ SG = \frac{\rho}{\rho_W} \]
Newton's Law of Viscosity #5
\[ \tau = \mu \frac{du}{dy} \]

What it means: Shear stress in a fluid is proportional to the velocity gradient. The proportionality constant \(\mu\) is the dynamic viscosity.

Origin: Derived from the strain rate of a fluid element. The no-slip condition requires \(u = 0\) at a stationary wall and \(u = V\) at a moving surface.

When to use: Finding shear force on plates/surfaces. Determining viscosity from experiment. Pipe flow wall shear stress.

When NOT to use: Only valid for Newtonian fluids (constant \(\mu\)). Non-Newtonian fluids require modified constitutive relations.

\[ \tau = \mu \frac{du}{dy} \]

Shear stress = viscosity × velocity gradient. Newtonian fluids only.

\[ \tau = \mu \frac{du}{dy} \]
Kinematic Viscosity #6
\[ \nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho} \]

What it means: Dynamic viscosity normalized by density. Dimensions of diffusivity (m²/s) with no force involved.

When to use: Reynolds number calculation: \(Re = VL/\nu\). Comparing fluids of different densities. Navier-Stokes equation.

\[ \nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho} \]

Viscosity normalized by density. SI: m²/s. Used in Reynolds number.

\[ \nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho} \]
Droplet / Bubble Pressure #7
\[ \Delta p_{\text{droplet}} = \frac{2\Upsilon}{R} \qquad \Delta p_{\text{bubble}} = \frac{4\Upsilon}{R} \]

What it means: Surface tension creates internal pressure in curved liquid surfaces. Bubbles have 2× the pressure of droplets because they have two surfaces.

When to use: Finding pressure inside droplets, bubbles, or curved menisci.

When NOT to use: Not valid for non-spherical geometries without modification. Gravity neglected.

\[ \Delta p = \frac{2\Upsilon}{R} \;\text{(droplet)} \qquad \frac{4\Upsilon}{R} \;\text{(bubble)} \]

Pressure excess inside curved surfaces due to surface tension.

\[ \Delta p = \frac{2\Upsilon}{R} \;\text{(drop)},\; \frac{4\Upsilon}{R} \;\text{(bubble)} \]
Capillary Height #8
\[ h = \frac{2\Upsilon \cos\theta}{\gamma R} \]

What it means: Height a liquid rises (or falls) in a narrow tube due to surface tension. Positive for wetting liquids (\(\theta < 90°\)), negative for non-wetting (\(\theta > 90°\)).

When to use: Capillary tube problems. Correcting pressure measurements in small-diameter tubes.

When NOT to use: Only valid for circular tubes. Requires known contact angle and surface tension.

\[ h = \frac{2\Upsilon \cos\theta}{\gamma R} \]

Capillary rise in a tube. Positive = wetting (rises), negative = non-wetting (falls).

\[ h = \frac{2\Upsilon \cos\theta}{\gamma R} \]
Ideal Gas Law #9
\[ p = \rho R T \]

What it means: Relates absolute pressure, density, and absolute temperature for an ideal gas through the specific gas constant \(R\).

When to use: Finding gas density at given conditions. Compressible flow density changes. Atmosphere problems.

When NOT to use: Fails at very high pressures or very low temperatures where gas behavior deviates from ideal (real gas effects).

\[ p = \rho R T \]

Ideal gas equation of state. Use absolute pressure and temperature.

\[ p = \rho R T \]
Hydrostatic Pressure Gradient #10
\[\frac{dp}{dz} = -\gamma = -\rho g\]

What it means: In a static fluid, pressure only changes in the vertical direction. It increases with depth (going down) and decreases with altitude (going up).

Origin: Force balance on an infinitesimal control volume of static fluid — horizontal forces cancel, vertical forces give \(dp = -\gamma\,dz\).

When to use: Any hydrostatic problem. Foundation for manometry, pressure-depth calculations, atmospheric pressure.

When NOT to use: Moving fluids (use Bernoulli/momentum instead). Also not valid if \(\gamma\) varies spatially in a non-trivial way without integration.

\[\frac{dp}{dz} = -\gamma = -\rho g\]

Pressure changes only vertically: increases with depth, decreases with altitude.

\[\frac{dp}{dz} = -\gamma = -\rho g\]
Pressure–Depth (Incompressible) #11
\[p_2 - p_1 = -\gamma(z_2 - z_1) \qquad\text{or}\qquad z_1 + \frac{p_1}{\gamma} = z_2 + \frac{p_2}{\gamma}\]

What it means: For an incompressible fluid (constant \(\gamma\)), the pressure difference between two points depends only on their elevation difference.

When to use: Most liquid problems (water, oil, mercury). Manometry. Any problem asking "find pressure at depth h."

When NOT to use: Gases over large altitude changes (density varies — use tropospheric or isothermal formulas instead).

\[p_2 - p_1 = -\gamma(z_2 - z_1)\]

Pressure-depth for constant-density fluids. Going down increases pressure.

\[p_2 - p_1 = -\gamma(z_2 - z_1)\]
Gage & Vacuum Pressure #12
\[p_{\text{gage}} = p_{\text{abs}} - p_{\text{atm}} \qquad p_{\text{vacuum}} = p_{\text{atm}} - p_{\text{abs}}\]

What it means: Gage pressure is how far above atmospheric; vacuum pressure is how far below.

When to use: Converting between gage and absolute. Most gauges read gage pressure. Use absolute for gas law and thermodynamic calculations.

When NOT to use: Never use gage pressure in the ideal gas law or tropospheric formula — those require absolute pressure.

\[p_{\text{gage}} = p_{\text{abs}} - p_{\text{atm}}\]

Gage = above atm; vacuum = below atm. Use absolute for gas law.

\[p_{\text{gage}} = p_{\text{abs}} - p_{\text{atm}}\]
Pressure Head #13
\[h = \frac{p_{\text{gage}}}{\gamma}\]

What it means: Express any pressure as an equivalent liquid column height. Useful for intuitive understanding and manometry.

When to use: Interpreting manometer readings. Comparing pressures in different fluids. "Express pressure in meters of water / mm of mercury."

When NOT to use: Compressible fluids — the concept breaks down when \(\gamma\) varies with altitude.

\[h = p_{\text{gage}} / \gamma\]

Height of liquid column that produces gage pressure \(p\).

\[h = p_{\text{gage}} / \gamma\]
Troposphere Pressure #14
\[p = p_0\!\left(1 - \frac{Bz}{T_0}\right)^{g/(RB)}\]

What it means: Atmospheric pressure in the troposphere (0–11 km), where temperature decreases linearly with altitude at lapse rate \(B\).

Constants: \(T_0 = 288.16\) K, \(B = 0.00650\) K/m, \(g/(RB) \approx 5.26\) for air.

When to use: "Find pressure at altitude z" in the troposphere. Standard atmosphere calculations.

When NOT to use: Above 11 km (use isothermal formula). Liquids (use incompressible formula).

\[p = p_0(1 - Bz/T_0)^{5.26}\]

Troposphere (0–11 km) with linear temperature lapse. \(B = 0.00650\) K/m.

\[p = p_0(1 - Bz/T_0)^{g/(RB)}\]
Isothermal Atmosphere #15
\[p = p_0\,e^{-gz/(RT_0)}\]

What it means: Pressure in an isothermal (constant temperature) atmosphere — exponential decay with altitude.

When to use: Stratosphere (11–20 km). Also a quick-and-dirty approximation for the troposphere (~4% error at 5 km).

When NOT to use: Troposphere for accurate results — the temperature lapse makes ~4% difference at 5 km.

\[p = p_0\,e^{-gz/(RT)}\]

Exponential decay for constant-T atmosphere. ~4% error in troposphere.

\[p = p_0\,e^{-gz/(RT)}\]
Hydraulic Force Multiplication #16
\[F_2 = F_1\,\frac{A_2}{A_1}\]

What it means: Pascal's Law: equal pressure throughout a confined fluid means a small force on a small piston can support a large force on a large piston.

When to use: Hydraulic jack/press/lift problems. Finding force ratios or mechanical advantage.

When NOT to use: Open systems or compressible fluids. Also assumes pistons are at the same elevation (otherwise add \(\gamma\Delta h\) correction).

\[F_2 = F_1(A_2/A_1)\]

Pascal's Law: force multiplication via area ratio. Mechanical advantage = \(A_2/A_1\).

\[F_2 = F_1(A_2/A_1)\]
General Manometer Rule #17
\[p_n - p_1 = \sum_{\text{down}} \gamma_i\,|h_i| - \sum_{\text{up}} \gamma_i\,|h_i|\]

What it means: Walk through a manometer from known to unknown pressure. Going down adds \(\gamma h\), going up subtracts \(\gamma h\). Jump across at same level in same fluid.

When to use: ANY manometer problem — single-fluid, multi-fluid, U-tube, inclined, differential.

When NOT to use: Moving fluids in the manometer tube (rare). The fluid in the manometer must be static.

\[p_n - p_1 = \sum_{\downarrow}\gamma_i|h_i| - \sum_{\uparrow}\gamma_i|h_i|\]

Down = add \(\gamma h\), up = subtract \(\gamma h\). Jump across at same level.

\[p_n - p_1 = \sum_{\downarrow}\gamma_i|h_i| - \sum_{\uparrow}\gamma_i|h_i|\]
Differential Manometer #18
\[p_a - p_b = (\rho_2 - \rho_1)\,g\,h\]

What it means: Pressure drop across a flow device measured by a U-tube. Only the manometer reading \(h\) and the density difference matter — the connecting leg height cancels.

When to use: Measuring pressure drop across valves, orifices, venturi meters, or any flow restriction.

When NOT to use: Don't forget to subtract \(\rho_1\)! The formula uses the DIFFERENCE in densities, not just the gauge fluid density.

\[p_a - p_b = (\rho_2 - \rho_1)gh\]

Differential U-tube: density DIFFERENCE times \(gh\). Don't forget \(\rho_1\)!

\[\Delta p = (\rho_2 - \rho_1)gh\]

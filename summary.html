Ch 1 — Fluid Properties & Pressure

Establishes the foundational definitions and assumptions that underpin every equation in the course — what a fluid is, how we treat it mathematically, and the three branches we will study.

Classification of Matter
What:
Matter classified as solid, liquid, gas, or plasma based on molecular bonding and response to shear stress.
Why:
The defining property of a fluid is that it cannot resist shear stress — it deforms continuously. Solids resist shear by deforming to equilibrium.
How:
Liquids have constant volume/density and form a free surface. Gases expand to fill their container and have no definite volume. Both are fluids.
Trap:
Don't confuse "cannot resist shear" with "has no viscosity." Fluids still resist shear through viscous effects — they just never reach static equilibrium under shear.
The Continuum Assumption
What:
Treat fluid properties as smooth, continuous functions of position rather than tracking individual molecules.
Why:
Enables the use of differential calculus — partial derivatives, integrals, and governing PDEs — for engineering-scale analysis.
How:
\(\rho = \lim_{\delta V \to \delta V^*} \frac{\delta m}{\delta V}\) where \(\delta V^* \sim 10^{-9}\) mm³ gives a stable local density.
Trap:
Fails in rarefied gas flows (high altitude, vacuum systems) where the mean free path approaches the problem's characteristic length.
Branches of Fluid Mechanics
What:
Three branches — hydrostatics (fluids at rest), kinematics (geometry of motion), and fluid dynamics (forces causing acceleration).
Why:
Structures the entire course: Ch 1–2 cover statics, Ch 4 covers kinematics, Ch 5–6 cover dynamics via the Reynolds Transport Theorem.

Classification of matter → shear stress concept reappears in viscosity (Ch 1.2) and Newton's law of viscosity

Continuum assumption → justifies every differential equation in Ch 3–6

Density definition → used directly in hydrostatics (Ch 2), Bernoulli (Ch 3), and RTT (Ch 5)

Units & Dimensional Analysis
What:
Four primary dimensions {M, L, T, Θ} from which all fluid mechanics quantities derive. Two consistent unit systems: SI and BG.
Why:
Dimensional homogeneity is required for every valid equation — if terms don't match dimensions, the equation is wrong.
How:
Check: every term in an equation must reduce to the same primary dimension combination. Convert: 1 slug = 14.5939 kg, 1 ft = 0.3048 m.
Trap:
Never mix SI and BG in the same calculation. Some "constants" carry hidden dimensions (e.g., valve flow coefficient \(C_V\)).
Density, Specific Weight & Specific Gravity
What:
Three related ways to characterize a fluid's "heaviness": \(\rho = m/V\), \(\gamma = \rho g\), \(SG = \rho/\rho_W\).
How:
Need density? → \(\rho = m/V\). Need weight per volume? → \(\gamma = \rho g\). Need dimensionless comparison? → \(SG = \rho / 1000\).
Trap:
Don't confuse \(\rho\) with \(\gamma\) — they differ by a factor of \(g\). SG is dimensionless but NOT a substitute for \(\rho\) in equations.
Viscosity
What:
Resistance to fluid deformation under shear. Dynamic viscosity \(\mu\) relates shear stress to velocity gradient; kinematic viscosity \(\nu = \mu/\rho\).
How:
Newton's law: \(\tau = \mu \, du/dy\). Finding \(\mu\) experimentally: measure \(\tau\) and \(du/dy\), then divide.
Trap:
Liquid viscosity ↓ with T; gas viscosity ↑ with T — opposite trends. Don't confuse dynamic (\(\mu\)) with kinematic (\(\nu\)).
Surface Tension & Capillarity
What:
Surface tension \(\Upsilon\) creates pressure differences across curved surfaces and drives capillary rise/fall in narrow tubes.
How:
Droplet: \(\Delta p = 2\Upsilon/R\). Bubble: \(\Delta p = 4\Upsilon/R\). Capillary: \(h = 2\Upsilon\cos\theta / \gamma R\).
Trap:
Bubble has 2× droplet pressure (two surfaces). Capillary height is negative for non-wetting fluids (\(\theta > 90°\)).
Ideal Gas Law
What:
\(p = \rho R T\) — equation of state for ideal gases relating pressure, density, and absolute temperature.
How:
Need gas density? → \(\rho = p/(RT)\). Constant T compression? → \(p_1/\rho_1 = p_2/\rho_2\).
Trap:
Must use absolute pressure and temperature (Pa and K, not gauge and °C). \(R\) is gas-specific, not universal.

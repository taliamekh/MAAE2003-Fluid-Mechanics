1.3 — Hydrostatics & Manometers

Many fluid problems do not involve motion at all. Think of water tanks, reservoirs, dams, and hydraulic systems — the fluid sits at rest, yet pressure forces can be enormous. When the fluid velocity is everywhere zero, we say the fluid is in a hydrostatic condition. This lecture covers how pressure distributes itself in a stationary fluid and how we measure it.

Hydrostatics and Pressure

Recall from Lecture 1.2 that pressure is defined as the normal force per unit area: \(p = dF/dA\). In a moving fluid, both normal forces (pressure) and shear forces (viscosity) act on fluid elements. In a static fluid there is no relative motion between layers — no velocity gradient — so by Newton’s law of viscosity (\(\tau = \mu\,du/dy\)), shear stress is identically zero.

★ Key Insight

In a static fluid, the only surface force is pressure (normal force). All shear stresses vanish because there is no fluid motion.

Characteristic of Pressure

To understand how pressure behaves at a point, consider a small triangular (wedge-shaped) fluid element in static equilibrium. The element has width \(b\) into the page, with faces experiencing pressures \(p_x\), \(p_z\), and \(p_n\) (on the inclined face at angle \(\theta\)).

Wedge-shaped fluid element: force balance proves pressure is equal in all directions at a point (Slide 4)

Derivation: Pressure at a Point

Step 1 — x-direction:

\[\sum F_x = 0 \implies p_x\,b\,\Delta z - p_n\,b\,\Delta s\,\sin\theta = 0\]

From the geometry, \(\Delta s\,\sin\theta = \Delta z\), so:

\[p_x = p_n \tag{1}\]

Step 2 — z-direction:

\[\sum F_z = 0 \implies p_z\,b\,\Delta x - p_n\,b\,\Delta s\,\cos\theta - \tfrac{1}{2}\rho g\,b\,\Delta x\,\Delta z = 0\]

Using \(\Delta s\,\cos\theta = \Delta x\):

\[p_z = p_n + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho g\,\Delta z \tag{2}\]

Step 3 — Shrink to a point: Since \(\theta\) is arbitrary and \(\Delta z \to 0\), the weight term vanishes:

\[\boxed{p_x = p_z = p_n = p} \tag{3}\]

Pressure Is a Scalar

Pressure at a point in a static fluid is the same in all directions — it is a scalar, not a vector. It is a thermodynamic property like temperature and density. A body in contact with a static fluid experiences a normal force at each point due to pressure.

Pascal’s Law

A pressure change occurring anywhere in a confined incompressible fluid is transmitted throughout the fluid such that the same change occurs everywhere. This is the foundation of all hydraulic machinery.

Static Pressure Variation

Consider a small cubic control volume of static fluid. Applying force balance in each coordinate direction reveals the fundamental pressure distribution law.

Force balance on an infinitesimal control volume in a static fluid (Slide 8)

Derivation: Hydrostatic Pressure Equation

Sum forces in the \(z\)-direction on the CV (\(\Delta A = \Delta x\,\Delta y\)):

\[\left(p - \frac{\partial p}{\partial z}\frac{\Delta z}{2}\right)\!\Delta A - \left(p + \frac{\partial p}{\partial z}\frac{\Delta z}{2}\right)\!\Delta A - \gamma(\Delta A\,\Delta z) = 0\]

Simplifying gives:

\[\frac{dp}{dz} = -\gamma \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{dp}{dx} = \frac{dp}{dy} = 0\]

Hydrostatic Pressure Gradient

\[\frac{dp}{dz} = -\gamma = -\rho g\]
\(p\) = pressure [Pa] \(z\) = vertical coordinate (positive upward) [m] \(\gamma\) = specific weight [N/m³] \(\rho\) = density [kg/m³] \(g\) = gravitational acceleration [m/s²]

★ Three Principles of Hydrostatic Pressure

1. No pressure change in the horizontal direction.
2. In the vertical direction, pressure changes proportionally to \(\rho g\,\Delta z\).
3. Pressure is equal in all directions at a point.

Incompressible Fluid: Pressure–Depth Relation

For an incompressible liquid (constant \(\gamma\)) with no significant gravity variation, integrate \(dp = -\gamma\,dz\) from point 1 to point 2:

Pressure–Depth Relation (Incompressible)

\[p_2 - p_1 = -\gamma(z_2 - z_1)\]

Equivalently:

\[z_1 + \frac{p_1}{\gamma} = z_2 + \frac{p_2}{\gamma}\]
\(p_1, p_2\) = pressures at points 1 and 2 [Pa] \(z_1, z_2\) = elevations [m] \(\gamma = \rho g\) = specific weight [N/m³]

In a continuously distributed uniform static fluid, pressure varies only with vertical distance and is independent of container shape. Pressure is the same at all points on a given horizontal plane in the same uniform fluid.

Points at the same depth in the same continuous fluid have equal pressure, but \(p_A = p_B = p_C \neq p_D\) since D is in mercury (Slide 10)

★ Exam Warning

Points at the same depth have equal pressure only if they are in the same continuous fluid. If a different fluid separates them, pressures are NOT equal even at the same elevation.

Absolute Pressure, Gage Pressure, and Vacuum

Relationships between absolute, gage, and vacuum pressure (Slide 13)

Pressure Reference Definitions

\[p_{\text{gage}} = p_{\text{absolute}} - p_{\text{atm}}\] \[p_{\text{vacuum}} = p_{\text{atm}} - p_{\text{absolute}}\]
\(p_{\text{absolute}}\) = pressure from absolute zero [Pa] \(p_{\text{atm}}\) ≈ 101,325 Pa at sea level \(p_{\text{gage}}\) = pressure above atmosphere \(p_{\text{vacuum}}\) = pressure below atmosphere

Absolute pressure is measured from absolute zero (perfect vacuum). Gage pressure is measured from local atmosphere — what most gauges read. Vacuum pressure applies when absolute pressure is below atmospheric.

Pressure Head

Since \(p_{\text{gage}} = \gamma h\) for an incompressible fluid, any pressure can be expressed as the height of a liquid column:

Pressure Head

\[h_{\text{liquid}} = \frac{p_{\text{gage}}}{\gamma}\]
\(h\) = height of liquid column [m] \(p_{\text{gage}}\) = gage pressure [Pa] \(\gamma\) = specific weight [N/m³]

Example: Pressure Head Comparison

To produce a gage pressure of 50 kPa:

Water (\(\gamma_w = 9810\) N/m³): \(h = 50\times10^3 / 9810 = 5.10\) m

Mercury (\(\gamma_{Hg} = 133{,}100\) N/m³): \(h = 50\times10^3 / 133{,}100 = 0.376\) m

Mercury needs a much shorter column because it is 13.6× denser — this is why mercury manometers are compact.

Hydrostatic Pressure in Oceans and Atmosphere

Pressure above and below the free surface (Slide 14)

Taking the free surface as \(z = 0\) with pressure \(p_a\): moving upward by height \(b\), \(p \approx p_a - b\gamma_{\text{air}}\). Moving downward by depth \(h\), \(p \approx p_a + h\gamma_{\text{water}}\). Be careful with signs.

Example 2.1 — Lake Pressure

Problem: Newfound Lake, max depth 60 m, atmospheric pressure 91 kPa. Find absolute pressure at maximum depth.

Show Solution

Given: \(h = 60\) m, \(p_a = 91\) kPa, \(\gamma_{\text{water}} = 9790\) N/m³

\[p_{\max} = p_a - \gamma z = 91{,}000 - (9790)(-60) = 678{,}400\text{ Pa}\] \[\boxed{p_{\max} \approx 678\text{ kPa}}\]

Tip: Always use pascals in the formula, convert to kPa at the end.

Example — Pipe AB Force on Cap

Problem: Water fills pipe AB. Absolute pressure at A = 400 kPa, atmospheric = 101 kPa, pipe inner diameter = 50 mm. B is 0.3 m above A. Find resultant force on cap at B.

Show Solution

Step 1: Pressure at B (B is higher, so \(z_A - z_B = -0.3\) m):

\[p_B = p_A + \rho_w g(z_A - z_B) = 400 + (1000)(9.81)(-0.3)/1000 = 397.06\text{ kPa}\]

Step 2: Net force on cap (inside: water at \(p_B\), outside: atmosphere):

\[F_B = (p_B - p_{\text{atm}})\pi r^2 = (397.06 - 101)\times10^3 \times \pi(0.025)^2\] \[\boxed{F_B = 581.3\text{ N}}\]

Compressible Fluid: Atmospheric Pressure

For gases, density varies with altitude — we cannot use \(p_2 - p_1 = -\gamma(z_2 - z_1)\). Instead, combine \(dp = -\gamma\,dz\) with the ideal gas law \(\gamma = pg/(RT)\):

Derivation: Compressible Pressure

\[\frac{dp}{p} = -\frac{g}{RT}\,dz\]

This separable ODE requires knowing how \(T\) varies with \(z\). Two cases: linear lapse rate (troposphere) and constant temperature (stratosphere).

Troposphere Pressure (0 ≤ z < 11 km)

Temperature: \(T = T_0 - Bz\), with \(T_0 = 288.16\) K, \(B = 0.00650\) K/m.

\[p = p_0\!\left(1 - \frac{Bz}{T_0}\right)^{\!g/(RB)}\]

where \(g/(RB) \approx 5.26\) for air.

\(p_0\) = sea-level pressure = 101,350 Pa \(T_0\) = 288.16 K \(B\) = 0.00650 K/m (lapse rate) \(R\) = 287 J/(kg·K) for air

Isothermal Atmosphere

\[p = p_0\,e^{-gz/(RT_0)}\]
Applies in stratosphere (11–20.1 km) at \(T \approx -56.5\)°C Also used as quick troposphere approximation (~4% error at 5 km)

★ Tip: Use Absolute p and T

In gas law calculations, pressure must be in Pascals (not kPa) and temperature in Kelvin (not °C). Mixing gage/Celsius is the most common error.

Hydraulic Machinery

Pascal’s Law in action: a small force on a small piston produces a large force on a large piston. This is the principle behind hydraulic jacks, brakes, presses, and lifts.

Hydraulic force multiplication (Slide 24)

Hydraulic Force Multiplication

\[\frac{F_1}{A_1} = \frac{F_2}{A_2} \implies F_2 = F_1\,\frac{A_2}{A_1}\] \[\text{Mechanical advantage} = \frac{F_2}{F_1} = \frac{A_2}{A_1}\]
\(F_1\) = applied force [N] \(F_2\) = output force [N] \(A_1, A_2\) = piston areas [m²]

Manometry

A manometer measures pressure using liquid columns. Since \(dp/dz = -\gamma\), measuring column height directly gives pressure difference.

Barometer

Barometer: \(p_{\text{atm}} = \gamma_{\text{liquid}}\,h\) when \(p_{\text{vap}} \approx 0\) (Slide 26)

Barometer Equation

\[p_{\text{atm}} = \gamma_{\text{liquid}}\,h \quad (\text{when } p_{\text{vap}} \approx 0)\]
\(h\) = liquid column height [m] \(\gamma_{\text{liquid}}\) = specific weight of manometer fluid [N/m³]

Multi-Fluid Manometer

Multi-fluid manometer: sum pressure changes through each layer (Slide 27)

General Manometer Rule

\[p_n - p_1 = \sum_{\text{down}} \gamma_i\,|h_i| \;-\; \sum_{\text{up}} \gamma_i\,|h_i|\]
"Down" = lower elevation → add \(\gamma h\) "Up" = higher elevation → subtract \(\gamma h\) Jump across at same level in same fluid

U-Tube and Inclined-Tube Manometers

Left: U-tube manometer. Right: inclined-tube — tilting amplifies readable length for small pressure differences (Slide 30)

Differential Manometer

Measures pressure difference across a flow device (valve, orifice). For a U-tube differential manometer:

Differential Manometer

\[p_a - p_b = (\rho_2 - \rho_1)\,g\,h\]
\(h\) = gauge fluid height difference [m] \(\rho_2\) = gauge fluid density (e.g. mercury) [kg/m³] \(\rho_1\) = working fluid density [kg/m³]

★ Common Student Error

In the differential manometer, note the density difference \((\rho_2 - \rho_1)\). Forgetting to subtract the working fluid density is a very common mistake and a serious error when both fluids are liquids.

Example 2.4 — Multi-Fluid Manometer

Problem: Gage B reads 87 kPa at point A in a water flow. Manometer has water (down 5 cm), mercury (up 7 cm), SAE 30 oil (up 6 cm). Find \(p_A\). Fluids at 20°C.

Show Solution

Properties: \(\gamma_w = 9790\) N/m³, \(\gamma_m = 133{,}100\) N/m³, \(\gamma_o = 8720\) N/m³

Method: Start at A, go "down" then "up," jumping across at the mercury meniscus:

\[p_A + \gamma_w(0.05) - \gamma_m(0.07) - \gamma_o(0.06) = p_B\] \[p_A + 490 - 9317 - 523 = 87{,}000\] \[\boxed{p_A \approx 96.4\text{ kPa}}\]

Note: The 5-figure intermediate result is unrealistic — data are known to ~3 significant figures.

★ Manometer Problem Strategy

1. Start at a point of known pressure.
2. Walk through: going down adds \(\gamma h\), going up subtracts \(\gamma h\).
3. At each fluid interface, "jump across" — pressures equal at same level in same fluid.
4. End at the unknown and solve.

📌 Assumed knowledge (you must already know this)

This is tested inside Midterm-2 problems but is not re-taught in Ch 3–5 — it carries over from Ch 1–2 and your prerequisites. If any line looks unfamiliar, fix it first.

Fluid properties & numbers to have memorised
Density of water\(\rho_w \approx 1000\ \text{kg/m}^3\) (\(1.94\ \text{slug/ft}^3\))
Density of air\(\rho_{air}\approx 1.2\ \text{kg/m}^3\) (\(0.00238\ \text{slug/ft}^3\))
Specific weight\(\gamma=\rho g\); for water \(\gamma_w\approx 9.81\ \text{kN/m}^3\) (\(62.4\ \text{lbf/ft}^3\))
Specific gravity\(SG=\rho/\rho_w\) → \(\rho=SG\cdot\rho_w\)
Gravity\(g=9.81\ \text{m/s}^2\) (\(32.2\ \text{ft/s}^2\))
Hydrostatics & manometry (carried in from Ch 1–2)
  • Pressure with depth: \(p=p_{atm}+\rho g h\). Most problems use gage pressure, so set \(p_{atm}=0\).
  • Manometer rule: walk from one open end to the other — add \(\rho g h\) going down, subtract going up, and switch \(\rho\) at every fluid interface.
  • Hydrostatic force on a submerged plane surface: \(F=\gamma\,\bar h\,A=\rho g\,y_c A\) (pressure at the centroid × area). This is the \(F_A\), \(F_B\) you need in sluice-gate momentum problems.
  • Vertical rectangle from the surface (width \(w\), depth \(h\)): \(F=\tfrac12\gamma h^2 w\), acting at \(h/3\) above the bottom.
Geometry, trig, vectors & calculus
  • Areas: circle \(A=\pi D^2/4=\pi R^2\); rectangle \(A=b\,h\).
  • Resolving a vector: \(V_x=V\cos\theta,\; V_y=V\sin\theta\). Pick a positive \(x\) and \(y\) direction before you start.
  • Cross product (moments): \(\vec r\times\vec V\) — in 2-D the magnitude is (moment arm) × (velocity), direction by the right-hand rule (CCW positive).
  • Area integrals: for a round pipe use the ring element \(dA=2\pi r\,dr\); between flat plates use \(dA=w\,dy\).
  • Unit conversion (be consistent!): gpm → ft³/s: ÷7.48 then ÷60; \(1\ \text{slug}=14.59\ \text{kg}\); inches → ft: ÷12.

📚 Core concepts

Ordered the way the ideas build, not by chapter number: flow basics (Ch 4) → Bernoulli (Ch 3) → control volumes (Ch 5). Just the working idea and the key equation — full derivations live in the chapter notes.

A · Flow Fundamentals (Ch 4 — Kinematics)

Describing the flow: the velocity field

Fluid dynamics adds motion to statics. We use the Eulerian view — watch fixed points in space and record the velocity there — rather than tracking individual particles (Lagrangian).

\[ \vec V=(u,v,w)=u(x,y,z,t)\,\vec i+v(x,y,z,t)\,\vec j+w(x,y,z,t)\,\vec k \]

A stagnation point is where the fluid is momentarily at rest: set every velocity component to zero (\(u=0,\,v=0\)) and solve for the location.

Lines of flow — and when they're the same
  • Streamline — tangent to the velocity everywhere at one instant (the "photo" of the flow direction).
  • Pathline — the actual track one particle follows over time (the "long-exposure" of one dot).
  • Streakline — the line joining all particles that passed through one point (dye trace).
★ The one fact they test
In steady flow, streamlines, pathlines and streaklines are all identical. That's the whole MCQ.
Classifying a flow

Steady vs unsteady asks about time: steady means nothing at a point changes with time, \(\partial/\partial t=0\). Uniform vs non-uniform asks about space: uniform means it doesn't change along the flow, \(\partial/\partial s=0\). They're independent questions — don't mix them up.

Rate of flow: Q, average velocity, ṁ
\[ Q=\int_A v_n\,dA=A\,\bar V \qquad \dot m=\rho\int_A v_n\,dA=\rho A\bar V=\rho Q \]
Q — volume flow rate (m³/s) v_n — velocity ⟂ to area (m/s) A — cross-section area (m²) V̄ — average velocity = Q/A (m/s) ṁ — mass flow rate (kg/s)

For a profile, integrate to get \(Q\), then \(\bar V=Q/A\). Handy result: any parabolic pipe profile gives \(\bar V=V_{max}/2\); a linear plate profile also gives \(\bar V=u_{max}/2\).

B · Bernoulli's Equation (Ch 3)

The equation — three ways to read it
\[ p_1+\tfrac12\rho V_1^2+\rho g z_1=p_2+\tfrac12\rho V_2^2+\rho g z_2 \]

Divide by \(\rho\) for energy per unit mass, or by \(\gamma=\rho g\) for the head (height) form — the version you'll use most:

\[ \frac{p}{\gamma}+\frac{V^2}{2g}+z=H=\text{constant} \]
p/γ — pressure head (m) V²/2g — velocity head (m) z — elevation head (m) H — total head, constant along a streamline (m)

EGL (energy grade line) plots the total head \(H\); HGL (hydraulic grade line) plots \(p/\gamma+z\) — i.e. EGL minus the velocity head. For ideal flow the EGL is horizontal.

★ The six conditions (when Bernoulli is allowed)

Bernoulli is a restricted equation. It holds only when the flow is: (1) steady, (2) incompressible, (3) inviscid (no friction), (4) along a single streamline, (5) no shaft work (no pump/turbine between the points), (6) no heat transfer. If friction or a pump matters, you need the energy equation instead (that's Ch 6 / final material).

Static, dynamic & stagnation pressure
\[ \underbrace{p}_{\text{static}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12\rho V^2}_{\text{dynamic}}=\underbrace{p_0}_{\text{stagnation}} \]

Bring a flow to rest and all its dynamic pressure becomes static — that's the stagnation pressure \(p_0\). This is the entire basis for the Pitot tube.

★ Two cancellations that make tank problems trivial
  • Big tank/reservoir surface: the surface drops so slowly that \(V_{surface}\approx 0\).
  • Open to atmosphere at both points: \(p_1=p_2=p_{atm}\) (gage \(=0\)), so the pressure terms cancel.

Both together give Torricelli: \(\;V=\sqrt{2gz}\) for a tank draining through a hole at depth \(z\).

Flow-measurement devices (all Bernoulli + continuity)
Pitot tubevelocity from a stagnation reading: \(V=\sqrt{2(p_0-p)/\rho}\)
Venturismooth contraction; \(Q=C_v A_2\sqrt{\dfrac{2(p_1-p_2)}{\rho\,[1-(A_2/A_1)^2]}}\)
Orificeplate with a hole; same form with discharge coeff. \(C_D\) and contraction \(C_c\) (\(\approx0.6\))
Bell-mouthrounded inlet (e.g. wind tunnel); Bernoulli from still air to the throat

Accuracy ↑ / cost ↑: orifice → Venturi (Venturi recovers pressure, orifice is cheap but lossy). The pressure difference is usually read off a manometer, so you'll trace a manometer to get \(p_1-p_2\) first.

C · Reynolds Transport Theorem (Ch 5)

The big idea: from a system to a control volume

Newton's laws apply to a fixed lump of mass (a system), but in flow problems it's easier to watch a fixed region of space (a control volume, CV). The RTT is the bridge that converts any conservation law into "storage inside the CV + net flux out through the control surface (CS)":

\[ \frac{dB_{sys}}{dt}=\underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\int_{CV}\beta\,\rho\,d\forall}_{\text{storage}}+\underbrace{\int_{CS}\beta\,\rho\,(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA}_{\text{net flux out}} \]

\(B\) is any extensive property; \(\beta=dB/dm\) is its per-unit-mass amount. Pick \(\beta\) and the same equation gives you mass, momentum, or angular momentum:

β = 1 → mass (continuity) β = V⃗ → linear momentum β = r⃗ × V⃗ → angular momentum
★ Sign convention — the #1 thing to get right
\(\vec V\cdot\hat n\) uses the outward normal: positive for an outlet, negative for an inlet, zero on a wall. In steady flow the storage term is zero.
Conservation of Mass — Continuity (β = 1)
\[ \frac{d}{dt}\!\int_{CV}\rho\,d\forall+\sum\dot m_{out}-\sum\dot m_{in}=0 \]

Steady: \(\;\sum\dot m_{out}=\sum\dot m_{in}\). Incompressible (same \(\rho\)): \(\;\sum Q_{out}=\sum Q_{in}\), and for one pipe \(\;A_1V_1=A_2V_2\) (velocity goes up where area goes down).

★ Mass ≠ volume
Equal volume flow rates do not mean equal mass flow rates when densities differ (air vs water): \(\dot m_{air}=\dot m_{water}\cdot\rho_{air}/\rho_{water}\).
Linear Momentum (β = V⃗)
\[ \sum\vec F_{ext}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\int_{CV}\vec V\rho\,d\forall+\int_{CS}\vec V\rho(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA \;\xrightarrow{\text{steady}}\; \sum\vec F=\sum_{out}\dot m\vec V-\sum_{in}\dot m\vec V \]

This is Newton's 2nd law for a CV: the net external force equals the momentum flux out minus in. Always split into \(x\) and \(y\) components.

  • Left side (the forces): draw a free-body diagram of the CV — \(\sum\vec F=\vec F_{pressure}+\vec F_{weight}+\vec F_{reaction}+\vec F_{shear}\). The reaction is usually what you solve for.
  • Moving CV (constant velocity, e.g. a cart or windshield): use the relative velocity \(\vec V_{f/b}=\vec V_{flow}-\vec V_{body}\) everywhere — both in \(\dot m\) and in the momentum term.
Angular Momentum (β = r⃗ × V⃗)
\[ \sum\vec M_O=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\int_{CV}(\vec r\times\vec V)\rho\,d\forall+\int_{CS}(\vec r\times\vec V)\rho(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA \;\xrightarrow{\text{steady}}\; \sum\vec M_O=\sum(\vec r\times\vec V)\,\dot m \]

Same structure, now with a moment arm. Take moments about a convenient point \(O\) (usually a hinge or support so an unknown reaction drops out). Each port contributes (moment arm) × (velocity) × \(\dot m\).

★ Conceptual MCQ
Pressure forces act perpendicular to a surface but they can still produce a net torque about an axis — don't assume they cancel.

🧩 Question types & quick-solve guides

The eight problems you'll actually see. Each card: how to recognise it, what tools you need, the steps, and the trap. Colours match the triage lanes above.

1 · Bernoulli between two points (incl. draining tank)

Recognise: two labelled points, a height or pressure difference, "find the velocity / pressure / how high it rises." Tools: Bernoulli (head form) + continuity.

Steps: ① pick the two points (free surface or atmosphere is best — terms vanish) → ② write the head-form equation → ③ kill what's zero (\(V_{surface}\approx0\), \(p_{atm}=0\)) → ④ if both \(V\)'s survive, get a relation from continuity \(A_1V_1=A_2V_2\) → ⑤ solve.

Trap: mixing absolute and gage pressure, or forgetting that "large tank" means \(V_{surface}=0\).

2 · Flow-measurement device (Pitot / Venturi / orifice)

Recognise: a named meter or a manometer across a contraction; "find Q" or "find V." Tools: device formula + manometer trace.

Steps: ① trace the manometer to get \(p_1-p_2\) → ② pick the right device formula → ③ plug in areas (\(A=\pi D^2/4\)) and \(\rho\) → ④ apply \(C_v\) or \(C_D\) if given. Pitot is the shortcut: \(V=\sqrt{2(p_0-p)/\rho}\).

Trap: using the wrong fluid's \(\rho\) in the dynamic term vs the manometer fluid's \(\rho\) in the trace.

3 · Kinematics — velocity field / stagnation / classify

Recognise: a given \(\vec V=(u,v)\); "stagnation point," "is it steady/uniform," "sketch a streamline." Tools: algebra + definitions.

Steps: stagnation → set \(u=0\) and \(v=0\), solve for \((x,y)\). Steady? → check if anything depends on \(t\). Uniform? → check if anything depends on position along \(s\).

Trap: calling a flow "uniform" because it's steady (or vice-versa) — they're different questions.

4 · Flow rate from a velocity profile (integrate)

Recognise: a profile like \(u(r)=V_{max}(1-r^2/R^2)\) or \(u(y)\); "find Q, \(\bar V\), or \(\dot m\)." Tools: \(Q=\int v_n\,dA\).

Steps: ① choose the element — pipe: \(dA=2\pi r\,dr\); plates: \(dA=w\,dy\) → ② \(Q=\int_0^R u(r)\,2\pi r\,dr\) → ③ \(\bar V=Q/A\) → ④ \(\dot m=\rho Q\). Shortcut: parabolic or linear profile ⇒ \(\bar V=V_{max}/2\).

Trap: forgetting the \(2\pi r\) (the area of each ring grows with \(r\)) — you can't just integrate \(u\,dr\).

5 · Continuity — many ports / mass vs volume / unsteady

Recognise: a branching duct, a tank filling, or "air and water" with different densities. Tools: continuity.

Steps: ① draw the CV, mark every inlet/outlet with its sign → ② steady & incompressible: \(\sum Q_{in}=\sum Q_{out}\) → ③ different fluids: balance \(\dot m=\rho Q\), not \(Q\) → ④ filling/draining: keep the storage term, \(\frac{d}{dt}\!\int\rho\,d\forall=\sum\dot m_{in}-\sum\dot m_{out}\), to get \(dh/dt\).

Trap: setting volume flows equal across an air/water interface — use mass.

6 · Force on a surface / vane / nozzle (incl. moving CV)

Recognise: a jet hitting/turning on a plate, vane, or nozzle; "find the force / thrust." Tools: linear momentum (+ continuity, + Bernoulli for speeds).

Steps: ① sketch the CV, choose +x/+y → ② get speeds (continuity; Bernoulli if a free jet, then \(p\) cancels so speed is unchanged) → ③ \(\dot m=\rho A V\) → ④ \(\sum F_x=\sum\dot m V_x|_{out}-\sum\dot m V_x|_{in}\), same for \(y\) → ⑤ solve for the reaction, then flip its sign to report the force on the vane.

Moving CV: replace every \(V\) with the relative \(V_{f/b}=V_{flow}-V_{body}\). Trap: sign of inlet flux (\(\vec V\cdot\hat n<0\)) and solving for force on fluid vs force on body.

7 · Sluice gate / open-channel force

Recognise: water under a gate, upstream depth \(h_1\) and downstream depth \(h_2\); "force on the gate." Tools: Bernoulli + continuity + hydrostatic forces + momentum.

Steps: ① Bernoulli between the two depths (free surfaces, \(p=0\)) → ② continuity \(A_1V_1=A_2V_2\) to get both velocities → ③ hydrostatic forces on each face \(F=\tfrac12\gamma h^2 w\) (this is the pre-known part) → ④ momentum: \(F_1-F_{gate}-F_2=\dot m(V_2-V_1)\) → ⑤ solve for \(F_{gate}\).

Trap: leaving out the hydrostatic pressure forces on the upstream/downstream faces — they dominate the balance.

8 · Moment / torque (elbow, sprinkler, fan-tipping)

Recognise: "moment about a point," "torque to hold," "will it tip about an edge?" Tools: angular momentum (often after linear momentum + Bernoulli).

Steps: ① pick the pivot \(O\) so an unknown reaction has zero arm → ② find port velocities (continuity/Bernoulli) and \(\dot m\) → ③ \(\sum M_O=\sum(\text{arm})\,V\,\dot m\), include moments from pressure and weight forces → ④ solve for the moment, or set "righting = overturning" for a tipping threshold.

Trap: using the wrong moment arm (perpendicular distance to the line of action), or dropping the pressure-force moment.

⚡ Concept traps & tips

  • Always draw the CV / FBD first. Label every port, every force, your axes, and the unknown reaction. Marks come from the diagram even if the algebra slips.
  • Continuity before Bernoulli, every time. Bernoulli alone has two unknown velocities; continuity gives the relation that closes it.
  • Sign convention is everything in Ch 5. Outward normal: outlet \(+\), inlet \(-\). Pick \(+x,+y\) and stick to it; flip only at the very end to report a physical direction.
  • Free jet ⇒ pressure cancels. A jet open to atmosphere on both sides has \(p_1=p_2\), so Bernoulli says the speed doesn't change along it (only direction does).
  • Moving control volume ⇒ relative velocity in both the flux and the \(\dot m\). Don't mix absolute and relative.
  • Mass conservation ≠ volume conservation across different densities — balance \(\dot m=\rho Q\).
  • Gage pressure by default. Set \(p_{atm}=0\) unless the question is explicitly about absolute pressure.
  • Check units before you trust a number. Stay in one system; \(\gamma\), \(\rho\), and \(g\) must match (SI or BG).

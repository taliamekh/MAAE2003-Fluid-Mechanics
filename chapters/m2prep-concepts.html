Hydrostatics & manometry (carried in from Ch 1–2)
  • Pressure grows with depth: \(p=p_{atm}+\rho g h\). Most problems use gage pressure, so set \(p_{atm}=0\).
  • Manometer rule: walk from one open end to the other — add \(\rho g h\) going down, subtract going up, and switch \(\rho\) at every fluid interface.
  • Force on a submerged plane surface: \(F=\gamma\,\bar h\,A=\rho g\,y_c A\) — pressure at the centroid × area.
  • Vertical rectangle from the surface (width \(w\), depth \(h\)): \(F=\tfrac12\gamma h^2 w\), acting at \(h/3\) above the bottom. This is the \(F_1,F_2\) you need in sluice-gate problems.
Geometry, trig, vectors & calculus

  • Resolve a vector: \(V_x=V\cos\theta,\; V_y=V\sin\theta\). Choose \(+x\) and \(+y\) before you start.

  • Area integrals: round pipe → ring element \(dA=2\pi r\,dr\); between flat plates → \(dA=w\,dy\).

A · Flow Fundamentals (Ch 4 — Kinematics)

The velocity field

We use the Eulerian view — watch fixed points in space and record the velocity there — not the Lagrangian view of tracking individual particles.

\[ \vec V=(u,v,w)=u(x,y,z,t)\,\vec i+v(x,y,z,t)\,\vec j+w(x,y,z,t)\,\vec k \]

Stagnation point = where the fluid is momentarily at rest: set every component to zero (\(u=0,\ v=0\)) and solve for the location.

Lines of flow
  • Streamline — tangent to the velocity everywhere at one instant (a "photo" of flow direction).
  • Pathline — the actual track one particle follows over time (a "long-exposure" of one dot).
  • Streakline — the line joining all particles that passed through one point (a dye trace).
★ The one fact they test
In steady flow, streamlines, pathlines and streaklines are all identical.
Classifying a flow — two independent questions
  • Steady vs unsteady is about time: steady means nothing at a point changes with time, \(\partial/\partial t=0\).
  • Uniform vs non-uniform is about space: uniform means nothing changes along the flow, \(\partial/\partial s=0\).

Don't mix them up — a flow can be steady but non-uniform, or unsteady but uniform.

Rate of flow: Q, average velocity, ṁ
\[ Q=\int_A v_n\,dA=A\,\bar V \qquad \dot m=\rho\int_A v_n\,dA=\rho A\bar V=\rho Q \]
Q — volume flow rate (m³/s) v_n — velocity ⟂ to the area (m/s) A — cross-section area (m²) V̄ — average velocity = Q/A (m/s) ṁ — mass flow rate (kg/s)

Handy result: a parabolic pipe profile gives \(\bar V=V_{max}/2\); a linear plate profile also gives \(\bar V=u_{max}/2\).

B · Bernoulli's Equation (Ch 3)

The equation — three ways to read it
\[ p_1+\tfrac12\rho V_1^2+\rho g z_1=p_2+\tfrac12\rho V_2^2+\rho g z_2 \]

Divide by \(\rho\) for energy per unit mass, or by \(\gamma=\rho g\) for the head (height) form — the one you'll use most:

\[ \frac{p}{\gamma}+\frac{V^2}{2g}+z=H=\text{constant} \]
p/γ — pressure head (m) V²/2g — velocity head (m) z — elevation head (m) H — total head, constant along a streamline (m)

EGL plots total head \(H\); HGL plots \(p/\gamma+z\) (EGL minus velocity head). For ideal flow the EGL is horizontal.

★ The six conditions — when Bernoulli is allowed

Bernoulli is a restricted equation. It holds only if the flow is:

  1. Steady
  2. Incompressible
  3. Inviscid (no friction)
  4. Along a single streamline
  5. No shaft work (no pump or turbine between the two points)
  6. No heat transfer

If friction or a pump matters, use the energy equation instead (Ch 6 / final material).

Static, dynamic & stagnation pressure
\[ \underbrace{p}_{\text{static}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12\rho V^2}_{\text{dynamic}}=\underbrace{p_0}_{\text{stagnation}} \]

Bring a flow to rest and its dynamic pressure becomes static — that's the stagnation pressure \(p_0\), the entire basis of the Pitot tube.

★ Two cancellations that make tank problems trivial
  • Big tank/reservoir surface: the surface drops so slowly that \(V_{surface}\approx 0\).
  • Open to atmosphere at both points: \(p_1=p_2=p_{atm}\) (gage \(=0\)), so the pressure terms cancel.

Both together give Torricelli: \(\,V=\sqrt{2gz}\) for a tank draining through a hole at depth \(z\).

Flow-measurement devices (all Bernoulli + continuity)
Pitot tubevelocity from a stagnation reading: \(V=\sqrt{2(p_0-p)/\rho}\)
Venturismooth contraction; \(Q=C_v A_2\sqrt{\dfrac{2(p_1-p_2)}{\rho\,[1-(A_2/A_1)^2]}}\)
Orificeplate with a hole; same form with discharge coeff. \(C_D\) and contraction \(C_c\) (\(\approx0.6\))
Bell-mouthrounded inlet (e.g. wind tunnel); Bernoulli from still air to the throat

The pressure difference is usually read off a manometer, so trace it to get \(p_1-p_2\) first.

C · Reynolds Transport Theorem (Ch 5)

The big idea: from a system to a control volume

Newton's laws apply to a fixed lump of mass (a system), but in flow problems it's easier to watch a fixed region of space (a control volume, CV). The RTT converts any conservation law into "storage inside the CV + net flux out through the control surface (CS)":

\[ \frac{dB_{sys}}{dt}=\underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\int_{CV}\beta\,\rho\,d\forall}_{\text{storage}}+\underbrace{\int_{CS}\beta\,\rho\,(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA}_{\text{net flux out}} \]

Pick \(\beta=dB/dm\) and the same equation gives you mass, momentum, or angular momentum:

β = 1 → mass (continuity) β = V⃗ → linear momentum β = r⃗ × V⃗ → angular momentum
★ Sign convention — the #1 thing to get right
\(\vec V\cdot\hat n\) uses the outward normal: positive for an outlet, negative for an inlet, zero on a wall. In steady flow the storage term is zero.
Conservation of Mass — Continuity (β = 1)
\[ \frac{d}{dt}\!\int_{CV}\rho\,d\forall+\sum\dot m_{out}-\sum\dot m_{in}=0 \]
  • Steady: \(\sum\dot m_{out}=\sum\dot m_{in}\).
  • Incompressible (same \(\rho\)): \(\sum Q_{out}=\sum Q_{in}\), and for one pipe \(A_1V_1=A_2V_2\) (velocity goes up where area goes down).
★ Mass ≠ volume
Equal volume flow rates do not mean equal mass flow rates when densities differ (air vs water): \(\dot m_{air}=\dot m_{water}\cdot\rho_{air}/\rho_{water}\).
Linear Momentum (β = V⃗)
\[ \sum\vec F=\sum_{out}\dot m\vec V-\sum_{in}\dot m\vec V \quad(\text{steady}) \]

Newton's 2nd law for a CV: net external force = momentum flux out − in. Always split into \(x\) and \(y\).

  • Left side (forces): draw an FBD of the CV — \(\sum\vec F=\vec F_{pressure}+\vec F_{weight}+\vec F_{reaction}+\vec F_{shear}\). The reaction is usually the unknown.
  • Moving CV (constant velocity — cart, windshield): use the relative velocity \(\vec V_{f/b}=\vec V_{flow}-\vec V_{body}\) everywhere, in both \(\dot m\) and the momentum term.
Angular Momentum (β = r⃗ × V⃗)
\[ \sum\vec M_O=\sum(\vec r\times\vec V)\,\dot m \quad(\text{steady}) \]

Same structure, now with a moment arm. Take moments about a convenient point \(O\) (a hinge or support, so an unknown reaction drops out). Each port contributes (moment arm) × (velocity) × \(\dot m\).

★ Conceptual MCQ
Pressure forces act perpendicular to a surface but they can still produce a net torque about an axis — don't assume they cancel.

🧩 Question types & quick-solve guides

The eight problems you'll actually see. Each card: how to recognise it, the tools, the steps (one per line), and the trap. Colours match the triage lanes.

1 · Bernoulli between two points (incl. draining tank)

Recognise: two labelled points, a height or pressure difference; "find the velocity / pressure / how high it rises."

Tools: Bernoulli (head form) + continuity.

1

Pick the two points

A free surface or a point open to atmosphere is best — those terms vanish.

2

Write the head-form equation

\(\frac{p_1}{\gamma}+\frac{V_1^2}{2g}+z_1=\frac{p_2}{\gamma}+\frac{V_2^2}{2g}+z_2\).

3

Kill what's zero

Large tank ⇒ \(V_{surface}\approx0\); open to air ⇒ \(p=0\) (gage); pick the datum so one \(z=0\).

4

If both velocities survive, add continuity

\(A_1V_1=A_2V_2\) gives the relation that closes the system.

5

Solve

Rearrange for the single remaining unknown.

⚠ Trap: mixing absolute and gage pressure, or forgetting that "large tank" means \(V_{surface}=0\).

2 · Flow-measurement device (Pitot / Venturi / orifice)

Recognise: a named meter, or a manometer across a contraction; "find Q" or "find V."

Tools: device formula + manometer trace.

1

Trace the manometer

Get the pressure difference \(p_1-p_2\) across the device.

2

Pick the right device formula

Pitot is the shortcut: \(V=\sqrt{2(p_0-p)/\rho}\). Venturi/orifice use the contraction formula.

3

Plug in geometry & fluid

Areas from \(A=\pi D^2/4\); use the flowing fluid's \(\rho\).

4

Apply the coefficient

Multiply by \(C_v\) or \(C_D\) if the problem gives one.

⚠ Trap: using the manometer fluid's \(\rho\) in the dynamic term — that's the flowing fluid's \(\rho\); the manometer fluid only sets \(p_1-p_2\).

3 · Kinematics — velocity field / stagnation / classify

Recognise: a given \(\vec V=(u,v)\); "stagnation point," "is it steady/uniform," "sketch a streamline."

Tools: algebra + definitions.

1

Stagnation point

Set \(u=0\) and \(v=0\); solve the pair for \((x,y)\).

2

Steady?

Check whether anything depends on \(t\). No \(t\) ⇒ steady.

3

Uniform?

Check whether anything depends on position along \(s\). No \(s\)-dependence ⇒ uniform.

⚠ Trap: calling a flow "uniform" because it's steady (or vice-versa) — they're different questions.

4 · Flow rate from a velocity profile (integrate)

Recognise: a profile like \(u(r)=V_{max}(1-r^2/R^2)\) or \(u(y)\); "find Q, \(\bar V\), or \(\dot m\)."

Tools: \(Q=\int v_n\,dA\).

1

Choose the area element

Pipe: \(dA=2\pi r\,dr\). Between flat plates: \(dA=w\,dy\).

2

Integrate for Q

\(Q=\int_0^R u(r)\,2\pi r\,dr\) (or the matching plate integral).

3

Average velocity

\(\bar V=Q/A\).

4

Mass flow

\(\dot m=\rho Q\). Shortcut: parabolic or linear profile ⇒ \(\bar V=V_{max}/2\).

⚠ Trap: forgetting the \(2\pi r\) — each ring's area grows with \(r\), so you can't integrate \(u\,dr\) alone.

5 · Continuity — many ports / mass vs volume / unsteady

Recognise: a branching duct, a tank filling, or "air and water" with different densities.

Tools: continuity.

1

Draw the CV, mark every port

Label each inlet/outlet with its sign (outlet +, inlet −).

2

Steady & incompressible

\(\sum Q_{in}=\sum Q_{out}\).

3

Different fluids

Balance mass, not volume: \(\dot m=\rho Q\).

4

Filling / draining

Keep the storage term: \(\frac{d}{dt}\!\int\rho\,d\forall=\sum\dot m_{in}-\sum\dot m_{out}\) to get \(dh/dt\).

⚠ Trap: setting volume flows equal across an air/water interface — use mass.

6 · Force on a surface / vane / nozzle (incl. moving CV)

Recognise: a jet hitting/turning on a plate, vane, or nozzle; "find the force / thrust."

Tools: linear momentum (+ continuity, + Bernoulli for speeds).

1

Sketch the CV, choose +x/+y

Cut the CV around the fluid touching the surface; fix your axes.

2

Get the speeds

Continuity for area changes; Bernoulli for a free jet (then \(p\) cancels, so speed is unchanged).

3

Mass flow

\(\dot m=\rho A V\).

4

Momentum, component by component

\(\sum F_x=\sum\dot m V_x|_{out}-\sum\dot m V_x|_{in}\); repeat for \(y\).

5

Solve, then flip the sign

Solve for the reaction on the fluid; the force on the vane is equal and opposite.

Moving CV: replace every \(V\) with the relative \(V_{f/b}=V_{flow}-V_{body}\).

⚠ Trap: the inlet flux sign (\(\vec V\cdot\hat n<0\)), and confusing force-on-fluid with force-on-body.

7 · Sluice gate / open-channel force

Recognise: water under a gate, upstream depth \(h_1\) and downstream depth \(h_2\); "force on the gate."

Tools: Bernoulli + continuity + hydrostatic forces + momentum.

1

Bernoulli between the two depths

Both are free surfaces (\(p=0\)); relates \(V_1\) and \(V_2\).

2

Continuity

\(A_1V_1=A_2V_2\) (per unit width, \(h_1V_1=h_2V_2\)) to get both velocities.

3

Hydrostatic force on each face

\(F=\tfrac12\gamma h^2 w\) on the upstream and downstream faces (the pre-known part).

4

Momentum balance

\(F_1-F_{gate}-F_2=\dot m(V_2-V_1)\).

5

Solve for \(F_{gate}\)

Everything else is now known.

⚠ Trap: leaving out the hydrostatic pressure forces on the faces — they dominate the balance.

8 · Moment / torque (elbow, sprinkler, fan-tipping)

Recognise: "moment about a point," "torque to hold," "will it tip about an edge?"

Tools: angular momentum (often after linear momentum + Bernoulli).

1

Pick the pivot \(O\)

Choose it so an unknown reaction has zero moment arm.

2

Find port velocities & ṁ

Continuity / Bernoulli for the speeds; \(\dot m=\rho A V\).

3

Sum moments

\(\sum M_O=\sum(\text{arm})\,V\,\dot m\), including moments from pressure and weight forces.

4

Solve / set the threshold

Solve for the moment, or set "righting = overturning" for a tipping condition.

⚠ Trap: wrong moment arm (use the perpendicular distance to the line of action), or dropping the pressure-force moment.

⚡ Concept traps & tips

  • Always draw the CV / FBD first. Label every port, force, axis, and the unknown reaction. Marks come from the diagram even if the algebra slips.
  • Continuity before Bernoulli, every time. Bernoulli alone has two unknown velocities; continuity closes it.
  • Sign convention is everything in Ch 5. Outward normal: outlet \(+\), inlet \(-\). Pick \(+x,+y\) and stick to it; flip only at the very end.
  • Free jet ⇒ pressure cancels. Open to atmosphere on both sides ⇒ \(p_1=p_2\), so Bernoulli says the speed doesn't change (only direction does).
  • Moving CV ⇒ relative velocity in both the flux and \(\dot m\). Never mix absolute and relative.
  • Mass conservation ≠ volume conservation across different densities — balance \(\dot m=\rho Q\).
  • Gage pressure by default. Set \(p_{atm}=0\) unless the question is explicitly about absolute pressure.
  • Check units before you trust a number. Stay in one system; \(\gamma,\rho,g\) must match (SI or BG).

A · Flow Fundamentals (Ch 4 — Kinematics)

Statics asked "what is the pressure in a fluid sitting still?" Kinematics is the first step into moving fluid: how do we even describe a flow before we apply any physics to it? Everything in Ch 3 and Ch 5 is built on this vocabulary, so it's worth getting solid.

Two ways to watch a fluid

Imagine a river. You could either:

  • Lagrangian — drop a leaf in and follow that one leaf, tracking its position and velocity as it travels. You're following a particle.
  • Eulerian — stand on a bridge and watch one fixed spot, recording the velocity of whatever water happens to be passing through that spot right now. You're watching a point in space.

Fluid mechanics almost always uses the Eulerian view, because we care about what's happening at fixed locations (at the pump, at the nozzle, at the wall) — not about chasing individual blobs of water. The whole control-volume method in Ch 5 is Eulerian.

The velocity field

The Eulerian description packages "the velocity at every point, at every instant" into one object — the velocity field. It has three components, and each can depend on where you are \((x,y,z)\) and on time \(t\):

\[ \vec V=(u,\,v,\,w)=u(x,y,z,t)\,\vec i+v(x,y,z,t)\,\vec j+w(x,y,z,t)\,\vec k \]
V⃗ — velocity vector at a point (m/s) u — x-component of velocity (m/s) v — y-component of velocity (m/s) w — z-component of velocity (m/s) x, y, z — position of the point (m) t — time (s) i⃗, j⃗, k⃗ — unit vectors along x, y, z

Reading a field is just plugging an \((x,y)\) into \(u\) and \(v\) to get the velocity there. Most exam fields are 2-D, so \(w=0\) and you only deal with \(u\) and \(v\).

Stagnation point — where the fluid stops

A stagnation point is a location where the fluid is momentarily at rest — every velocity component is zero there. To find it, set each component to zero and solve the system:

\[ u(x,y)=0 \quad\text{and}\quad v(x,y)=0 \]

Example: if \(\vec V=(x,\,-y)\), then \(u=x=0\) gives \(x=0\), and \(v=-y=0\) gives \(y=0\). The stagnation point is the origin \((0,0)\). Physically it's the spot on the nose of an object where the oncoming flow splits and the velocity drops to zero — exactly the point a Pitot tube sits at.

Three kinds of "line" through a flow

These sound similar and get confused constantly, so anchor each to a mental picture:

  • Streamline — a curve that is tangent to the velocity everywhere at one frozen instant. Think of a single photograph of the flow with little arrows: a streamline follows the arrows. Flow never crosses a streamline.
  • Pathline — the actual trajectory a single particle traces out over time. Think of a long-exposure photo of one glowing dot as it drifts through.
  • Streakline — the line formed by all the particles that have passed through one fixed point. Think of injecting dye at one spot and watching the ribbon it makes.
★ The fact they test
In steady flow (nothing changes with time), all three coincide — streamline = pathline = streakline. They only differ when the flow is unsteady.
Classifying a flow — two separate yes/no questions

Students lose easy marks by treating these as the same question. They are independent — one is about time, the other about space.

  • Steady vs unsteady — does the flow at a fixed point change with time? If \(\partial(\,\cdot\,)/\partial t=0\) for every quantity, it's steady. A tap left running at a fixed setting is steady; a tap being opened is unsteady.
  • Uniform vs non-uniform — does the flow change from place to place along the flow direction? If \(\partial(\,\cdot\,)/\partial s=0\), it's uniform. Flow in a long straight constant-diameter pipe is uniform; flow through a nozzle (speeding up as the pipe narrows) is non-uniform.

A flow can be any combination: steady & uniform, steady & non-uniform (the nozzle), unsteady & uniform, etc.

Flow rate: connecting velocity to "how much fluid"

Velocity tells you how fast the fluid moves; flow rate tells you how much gets delivered. Volume flow rate \(Q\) is the velocity component pointing through a cross-section, summed over the whole area:

\[ Q=\int_A v_n\,dA = A\,\bar V \qquad\qquad \dot m=\rho\int_A v_n\,dA=\rho A\bar V=\rho Q \]
Q — volume flow rate (m³/s) ṁ — mass flow rate (kg/s) v_n — velocity component ⟂ to the area (m/s) A — cross-sectional area (m²) dA — area element being integrated (m²) V̄ — area-average velocity, V̄ = Q/A (m/s) ρ — fluid density (kg/m³)

If the velocity is the same across the whole area, the integral is just \(Q=AV\). But often the velocity varies across the pipe — fastest in the centre, zero at the walls (the fluid sticks to the wall: the no-slip condition). Then you must integrate.

A typical pipe velocity profile: zero at both walls, maximum on the centreline. To get the flow rate you sum (integrate) all these little velocity contributions across the area.

For a round pipe, slice the area into thin rings of radius \(r\) and thickness \(dr\); each ring has area \(dA=2\pi r\,dr\). Then:

\[ Q=\int_0^R u(r)\,\underbrace{2\pi r\,dr}_{dA},\qquad \bar V=\frac{Q}{A}=\frac{Q}{\pi R^2} \]
★ Two shortcuts worth memorising
A parabolic profile in a pipe (laminar flow) gives \(\bar V=V_{max}/2\). A linear profile between flat plates also gives \(\bar V=u_{max}/2\). If you spot either shape, you can skip the integral.

B · Bernoulli's Equation (Ch 3 — Energy along a streamline)

Bernoulli is the single most-used equation in this course, but it's also the easiest to misuse. The idea is simple: as a parcel of fluid moves along a streamline, its energy is conserved — but that energy can trade between three forms. Where the fluid speeds up, something else (pressure or height) must give way. Get comfortable reading it as an energy-balance, not just a formula to plug into.

The equation — three forms of energy, conserved

Between two points 1 and 2 on the same streamline:

\[ \underbrace{p_1+\tfrac12\rho V_1^2+\rho g z_1}_{\text{total energy at point 1}}=\underbrace{p_2+\tfrac12\rho V_2^2+\rho g z_2}_{\text{total energy at point 2}} \]

Each term is an energy per unit volume (units of pressure, Pa):

  • \(p\) — pressure energy: the work the surrounding fluid can do by pushing.
  • \(\tfrac12\rho V^2\) — kinetic energy: energy of motion. Bigger when the fluid is fast.
  • \(\rho g z\) — potential energy: energy of height above your chosen datum.
p — static pressure at the point (Pa) ρ — fluid density (kg/m³) V — flow speed at the point (m/s) g — gravity, 9.81 m/s² z — height above the datum (m) subscripts 1, 2 — the two chosen points
The head form — the version you'll actually use

Divide every term by \(\gamma=\rho g\) (the specific weight). Now each term has units of metres — it becomes a height, which is why we call them "heads." This form is the friendliest for tank, pipe and nozzle problems:

\[ \frac{p}{\gamma}+\frac{V^2}{2g}+z=H=\text{constant along the streamline} \]
p/γ — pressure head (m) V²/2g — velocity head (m) z — elevation head (m) H — total head, constant for ideal flow (m) γ — specific weight, γ = ρg (N/m³) g — gravity, 9.81 m/s²

Why heads are nice: every term is a length you could literally measure with a ruler against the apparatus, and the three always add up to the same number \(H\). If one head grows, another must shrink by the same amount.

★ The six conditions — and why each one is needed

Bernoulli is a restricted equation: it's really the energy equation with every loss and source switched off. Each condition is what lets us drop one of those terms. Knowing the why tells you instantly when you're not allowed to use it.

  1. Steady — nothing at a point changes with time. Why: the derivation assumes the flow pattern is frozen; an accelerating-in-time flow has an extra unsteady term.
  2. Incompressible — \(\rho\) is constant. Why: we pulled \(\rho\) outside as a single number. Fails for high-speed gas where density changes.
  3. Inviscid (frictionless) — no viscosity. Why: friction turns mechanical energy into heat, which Bernoulli has no term for. This is the one that fails most often in real pipes.
  4. Along one streamline — both points lie on the same streamline. Why: the constant \(H\) can differ from one streamline to another (unless the flow is also irrotational).
  5. No shaft work — no pump or turbine between the points. Why: a pump adds energy and a turbine removes it; either breaks "energy stays constant."
  6. No heat transfer — no heating/cooling of the fluid. Why: same reason — it's an extra energy input/output the equation doesn't carry.

If friction or a pump does matter, switch to the full energy equation: \(\frac{p_1}{\gamma}+\frac{V_1^2}{2g}+z_1+h_{pump}=\frac{p_2}{\gamma}+\frac{V_2^2}{2g}+z_2+h_{turbine}+h_{loss}\) (Ch 6 / final material).

Static, dynamic & stagnation pressure

Take the constant-height version of Bernoulli (\(z_1=z_2\)) and bring a moving flow to a complete stop at point 2 (so \(V_2=0\)). All of its kinetic energy converts into pressure:

\[ \underbrace{p}_{\text{static}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12\rho V^2}_{\text{dynamic}}=\underbrace{p_0}_{\text{stagnation}} \]
p — static pressure (what a gauge moving with the flow reads) (Pa) ½ρV² — dynamic pressure (the "pressure of motion") (Pa) p₀ — stagnation (total) pressure, the flow brought to rest (Pa) ρ — fluid density (kg/m³) V — flow speed (m/s)

Three pressures to keep straight: static is what the fluid carries as it flows, dynamic is the extra you'd get by stopping it, and stagnation is their sum — the highest pressure in the flow. This is the entire working principle of the Pitot tube: it stops the flow and reads \(p_0\).

★ Two cancellations that make tank problems trivial

Almost every "tank draining" question relies on two simplifications. Spot them and the algebra collapses:

  • The tank surface barely moves. A wide reservoir surface drops so slowly compared to the jet that we take \(V_{surface}\approx0\). The surface velocity head is negligible.
  • Both points are open to the atmosphere. If the free surface and the exit jet both feel atmospheric pressure, then \(p_1=p_2=p_{atm}\). Working in gage pressure makes both terms \(0\), so they cancel.

With only the height and exit-velocity terms left, Bernoulli reduces to the classic Torricelli result for a tank draining through a hole a depth \(z\) below the surface:

\[ V=\sqrt{2gz} \]
V — exit velocity of the jet (m/s) g — gravity, 9.81 m/s² z — depth of the hole below the free surface (m)

Notice it's the same speed an object would reach falling freely through height \(z\) — the fluid trades height for speed exactly like a dropped ball.

Flow-measurement devices — all Bernoulli + continuity

Every flow meter in this course works the same way: force the fluid through a known area change, measure the pressure drop that the speed-up creates, and back out the flow rate. They differ only in shape and in how much energy they waste.

Pitot tube — stops the flow at its tip and reads stagnation pressure, giving velocity directly:

\[ V=\sqrt{\dfrac{2(p_0-p)}{\rho}} \]
V — flow velocity (m/s) p₀ — stagnation pressure at the tip (Pa) p — static pressure of the free stream (Pa) ρ — flowing-fluid density (kg/m³)

Venturi meter — a smooth contraction; combine Bernoulli with continuity \(A_1V_1=A_2V_2\) to get \(Q\) from the pressure drop:

\[ Q=C_v\,A_2\sqrt{\dfrac{2(p_1-p_2)}{\rho\,[\,1-(A_2/A_1)^2\,]}} \]
Q — volume flow rate (m³/s) C_v — velocity (discharge) coefficient, ≈ 0.98 for a Venturi A₁ — upstream (large) area (m²) A₂ — throat (small) area (m²) p₁ − p₂ — pressure drop across the contraction (Pa) ρ — flowing-fluid density (kg/m³)

Orifice plate — a plate with a hole; same formula form, but the jet contracts past the hole so it carries a smaller discharge coefficient \(C_D\) and a contraction coefficient \(C_c\approx0.6\). Cheap to make but wastes a lot of pressure.

Bell-mouth inlet — a rounded entrance (e.g. a wind-tunnel mouth); apply Bernoulli from the still air outside to the throat.

★ Accuracy vs cost, and the manometer step
A Venturi recovers most of its pressure (efficient but expensive); an orifice is cheap but lossy. In both, the pressure difference \(p_1-p_2\) is usually read off a manometer — so on an exam you'll trace the manometer first to get \(p_1-p_2\), then feed it into the device formula. Use the flowing fluid's \(\rho\) in the formula, not the manometer fluid's.
EGL and HGL — drawing the energy

Because the three heads always sum to \(H\), you can plot them as two lines running along the flow. They're a quick visual check that you've applied Bernoulli correctly:

  • EGL (Energy Grade Line) — plots the total head \(H=\dfrac{p}{\gamma}+\dfrac{V^2}{2g}+z\). For ideal (frictionless) flow it's a horizontal line, because \(H\) never changes. Real friction makes it slope gently downward; a pump steps it up, a turbine steps it down.
  • HGL (Hydraulic Grade Line) — plots only \(\dfrac{p}{\gamma}+z\) (the EGL minus the velocity head). It's the height water would rise to in a little open tube tapped into the pipe. The HGL sits below the EGL by exactly the velocity head \(\dfrac{V^2}{2g}\).

Reading them: where the pipe narrows, the fluid speeds up, the velocity head grows, and the HGL dips toward the EGL. Where it widens, the HGL rises back up.

C · Reynolds Transport Theorem (Ch 5 — Control-volume analysis)

This is the engine room of the second half of the course. The problem: Newton's laws and conservation of mass are written for a fixed chunk of matter (a "system"), but in fluids that chunk is constantly moving and deforming — impossible to track. The fix: watch a fixed region of space instead (a "control volume") and let fluid stream through it. The RTT is the formula that translates between the two viewpoints, and every conservation law in Ch 5 is just the RTT with a different quantity plugged in.

System vs control volume — the core switch
  • System — a fixed collection of fluid particles. Its mass is constant, but it moves and changes shape as it flows. This is what the physics laws are written for.
  • Control volume (CV) — a fixed (or moving) region of space we draw ourselves, like a box around a pump or a nozzle. Fluid flows in and out across its boundary.
  • Control surface (CS) — the boundary of the CV. All the "flow in / flow out" bookkeeping happens here.

The RTT says: the rate of change of any property for the system equals how fast it's piling up inside the CV plus the net rate it's carried out across the CS.

The master equation — one formula, three laws
\[ \frac{dB_{sys}}{dt}=\underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\int_{CV}\beta\,\rho\,d\forall}_{\text{rate stored inside CV}}+\underbrace{\int_{CS}\beta\,\rho\,(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA}_{\text{net rate carried out across CS}} \]
B — any extensive property (mass, momentum, …) β — the same property per unit mass, β = dB/dm ρ — fluid density (kg/m³) d∀ — volume element inside the CV (m³) dA — area element on the control surface (m²) V⃗ — fluid velocity at the surface (m/s) n̂ — outward unit normal to the surface V⃗·n̂ — velocity component leaving the CS (m/s) t — time (s) CV — control volume · CS — control surface

The cleverness is the choice of \(\beta\). Pick what property you want conserved and the very same equation becomes that law:

β = 1 → mass (continuity) β = V⃗ → linear momentum β = r⃗ × V⃗ → angular momentum
★ The sign convention — the #1 thing students get wrong

The flux term \(\int_{CS}\beta\,\rho\,(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA\) hinges entirely on the sign of \(\vec V\cdot\hat n\), and that sign comes from the outward normal — the arrow pointing out of the CV at each patch of surface:

  • Outlet: flow leaves, so \(\vec V\) and \(\hat n\) point the same way → \(\vec V\cdot\hat n>0\) (positive).
  • Inlet: flow enters, so \(\vec V\) points in but \(\hat n\) points out → \(\vec V\cdot\hat n<0\) (negative).
  • Solid wall: no flow crosses it, the velocity is tangent → \(\vec V\cdot\hat n=0\).
Same flow direction at both faces (left → right), but the outward normal flips, so the inlet flux is negative and the outlet flux is positive. On a solid wall the velocity is tangent to the surface, giving \(\vec V\cdot\hat n=0\) — walls drop out of the flux term entirely.

One more huge simplification: in steady flow nothing accumulates, so the storage term \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\int_{CV}(\cdots)=0\) and you're left with just "flux out = flux in." Most exam problems are steady.

Conservation of Mass — Continuity (β = 1)

Set \(\beta=1\) (mass per unit mass is just 1) and \(B=\) mass. Since mass can't be created, \(dB_{sys}/dt=0\):

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\!\int_{CV}\rho\,d\forall+\sum\dot m_{out}-\sum\dot m_{in}=0 \]
ρ — fluid density (kg/m³) d∀ — volume element inside the CV (m³) ṁ_out — mass flow rate leaving each outlet (kg/s) ṁ_in — mass flow rate entering each inlet (kg/s) ṁ = ρAV at each port (kg/s)

Reading it: (rate mass builds up inside) + (mass out) − (mass in) = 0. Two common simplifications:

  • Steady — the storage term vanishes, so \(\sum\dot m_{out}=\sum\dot m_{in}\): whatever mass goes in must come out.
  • Incompressible (constant \(\rho\)) — divide through by \(\rho\): \(\sum Q_{out}=\sum Q_{in}\). For a single pipe this is \(A_1V_1=A_2V_2\), so the fluid speeds up where the pipe narrows.
★ Mass ≠ volume
Equal volume flow rates are not equal mass flow rates when densities differ. Mixing air and water? You must balance \(\dot m=\rho Q\), not \(Q\). Only when \(\rho\) is the same everywhere can you balance volume.
Linear Momentum (β = V⃗) — Newton's 2nd law for a CV

Set \(\beta=\vec V\). Now \(B\) is momentum and \(dB_{sys}/dt=\sum\vec F\) (Newton's 2nd law). For steady flow the storage term drops and you get the workhorse momentum equation:

\[ \sum\vec F=\sum_{out}\dot m\,\vec V-\sum_{in}\dot m\,\vec V \]
ΣF⃗ — net external force on the fluid in the CV (N) ṁ — mass flow rate at each port (kg/s) V⃗ — flow velocity at each port (m/s, vector) subscripts out / in — sum over outlets and inlets

It's a vector equation — always split it into \(x\) and \(y\) and solve each separately. The two sides:

  • Right side (momentum flux): (mass rate) × (velocity) leaving minus entering. Watch component signs — a velocity pointing in −x is negative.
  • Left side (forces): draw a free-body diagram of the CV. \(\sum\vec F=\vec F_{pressure}+\vec F_{weight}+\vec F_{reaction}+\vec F_{shear}\). The reaction (the force holding the vane/nozzle/elbow in place) is almost always the unknown you're solving for.
A jet turned 90° by a vane. The fluid's momentum changes direction, so it pushes on the vane (orange). The reaction that holds the vane is equal and opposite. Teal = velocities, orange = force. Per port use \(\dot m=\rho A V\), and for a frictionless vane the speed is unchanged (\(V_2=V_1\)), only the direction turns.
★ Moving control volume (cart, moving vane, windshield)
If the CV moves at constant velocity, work in its frame: replace every velocity with the relative velocity \(\vec V_{f/b}=\vec V_{flow}-\vec V_{body}\), and use that relative velocity in both the \(\dot m\) (the rate the jet actually catches the moving surface) and the momentum term. Forgetting to use it in \(\dot m\) is the classic mistake.
Angular Momentum (β = r⃗ × V⃗) — for things that rotate or pivot

Set \(\beta=\vec r\times\vec V\) and the law becomes a moment (torque) balance about a point \(O\). Use it whenever a moment or a turning effect is asked for — sprinklers, pipe bends on a hinge, reaction at a support:

\[ \sum\vec M_O=\sum_{out}(\vec r\times\vec V)\,\dot m-\sum_{in}(\vec r\times\vec V)\,\dot m \]
ΣM⃗_O — net moment about point O (N·m) r⃗ — position vector from O to the port (m) V⃗ — flow velocity at the port (m/s) r⃗ × V⃗ — moment arm × velocity (the angular term) ṁ — mass flow rate at the port (kg/s) O — the point you take moments about

Same structure as linear momentum, but every term now carries a moment arm \(r\). The trick is choosing \(O\) cleverly — put it on a hinge or support so an unknown reaction force passes through it and drops out of the moment equation.

★ Conceptual MCQ they like
Pressure forces act perpendicular to a surface, but they can still produce a net torque about an axis — don't assume "perpendicular" means "no moment." Whether they cancel depends on the geometry.
A reliable recipe for any momentum problem

Almost every Ch 5 force question yields to the same sequence. Run it top to bottom:

1

Draw the control volume

Enclose the device (vane, nozzle, elbow) and cut the CS straight across every inlet and outlet pipe. Mark a clear \(x\)–\(y\) axis.

2

Get the velocities first

Use continuity (\(A_1V_1=A_2V_2\)) and, if pressures/heights are involved, Bernoulli — to find every port velocity before touching momentum.

3

Find each mass flow rate

\(\dot m=\rho A V\) at every port. For a moving CV, use the relative velocity here.

4

Draw the FBD of the CV

Add pressure forces on each cut face (\(F=pA\), gauge), weight if relevant, and the unknown reaction \(R\).

5

Write momentum in x, then y

\(\sum F_x=\sum\dot m V_x|_{out}-\sum\dot m V_x|_{in}\), and the same in \(y\). Mind every component sign.

6

Solve for the reaction

Two equations, the unknowns are \(R_x\) and \(R_y\). Combine for magnitude \(R=\sqrt{R_x^2+R_y^2}\) and direction if asked.

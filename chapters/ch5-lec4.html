5.4 — RTT: Energy

Ch 3.7 — Reynolds Transport Theorem applied to the conservation of energy: the steady-flow energy equation, the head equation (generalized Bernoulli), pump & turbine work, and the kinetic-energy correction factor

Energy as the Transported Quantity

We have now run the Reynolds Transport Theorem through mass (5.1), linear momentum (5.2), and angular momentum (5.3). The same machine handles the fourth and final system law: conservation of energy. The pattern is identical — pick the extensive property \(B\) and its intensive counterpart \(\beta = dB/dm\), then read off the storage-plus-flux form. For energy, \(B = E\) (total energy of the system) and \(\beta = dE/dm = e\), the energy per unit mass.

The System Energy Law (First Law of Thermodynamics)

Recall from Ch 5.1 the four governing laws written for a system (a fixed lump of mass). The energy law is the first law of thermodynamics: the rate of change of the system’s energy equals the rate of heat added to the system minus the rate of work done by the system.

\[\frac{dQ}{dt} - \frac{dW}{dt} = \frac{dE}{dt}\]
\(\dfrac{dQ}{dt}\) — rate of heat added to the system (W) \(\dfrac{dW}{dt}\) — rate of work done by the system (W) \(\dfrac{dE}{dt}\) — rate of change of the system’s total energy (W)

Watch the sign convention: heat in is positive, work out (done by the fluid on its surroundings) is positive. This is the classic thermodynamics convention — the same one you used in thermo.

Now recall the RTT from Ch 5.1, which converts a system rate of change into a control-volume storage term plus a control-surface flux term:

Reynolds Transport Theorem (recall, Ch 5.1)
\[\frac{d}{dt}\big(B_{\text{sys}}\big) = \frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\int_{\text{CV}} \beta\rho\,d\forall\right) + \int_{\text{CS}} \beta\rho\,(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\,dA\]

Setting \(\beta = e\) (energy per unit mass) and equating \(\frac{d}{dt}B_{\text{sys}}\) to the first law \(\frac{dQ}{dt}-\frac{dW}{dt}\):

\[\frac{dQ}{dt} - \frac{dW}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\int_{\text{CV}} e\rho\,d\forall\right) + \int_{\text{CS}} e\rho\,(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\,dA\]

The first integral is the energy stored inside the control volume; the second is the energy flux carried with the flow across the control surface.

Energy per Unit Mass

What goes into \(e\)? In general, the energy of a fluid particle is the sum of its internal, kinetic, potential, and “other” energies:

\[e = e_{\text{internal}} + e_{\text{kinetic}} + e_{\text{potential}} + e_{\text{other}}\]

The “other” bucket holds chemical, nuclear, electromagnetic energies — irrelevant for ordinary pipe and channel flow, so we drop it. The remaining three are:

\[e = \hat{u} + \tfrac{1}{2}V^2 + gz\]
\(\hat{u}\) — specific internal energy (J/kg) \(\tfrac{1}{2}V^2\) — specific kinetic energy (J/kg) \(gz\) — specific potential energy (J/kg) \(V\) — flow speed (m/s) \(z\) — elevation above datum (m)

Splitting the Heat and Work Terms

The raw \(\dot{Q}\) and \(\dot{W}\) terms are too coarse to be useful. Before the equation earns its keep, we break each one into physically meaningful pieces and then discard the negligible ones.

Heat \(\dot{Q}\) enters or leaves through three transport mechanisms — conduction, convection, and radiation. We keep \(\dot{Q}\) as a single lumped term (its net value is usually given or assumed).

Three Kinds of Work

The total rate of work done by the system splits into three distinct contributions:

\[\dot{W} = \dot{W}_{\text{shaft}} + \dot{W}_{\text{press}} + \dot{W}_{\text{viscous}}\]
\(\dot{W}_{\text{shaft}}\) — shaft work: pumps, fans, compressors, turbines \(\dot{W}_{\text{press}}\) — work done by pressure forces at the CS \(\dot{W}_{\text{viscous}}\) — work done by viscous (shear) stresses at the CS

Viscous work — usually negligible

Shear stress acting on the control surface does work at a rate equal to (viscous force) × (velocity parallel to that force). Element by element:

\[d\dot{W}_v = -(\tau\,dA)\,V_{\text{tangential}} = -\vec{\tau}\cdot\vec{V}\,dA \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot{W}_v = -\int_{\text{CS}} \vec{\tau}\cdot\vec{V}\,dA\]
★ Key Insight — Why Viscous Work Drops Out

Where do shear stresses act? At the solid walls, at the interface between the wall and the fluid. But by the no-slip condition (Ch 1.2), the fluid velocity at a wall is essentially zero, \(V \approx 0\). A force doing work on something that isn’t moving does no work. So over a control surface that follows solid boundaries, \(\dot{W}_v \approx 0\). We carry the symbol \(\dot{W}_v\) through the algebra for completeness, but in nearly every problem it vanishes — its only lasting trace is a contribution to the head loss term you’ll meet below.

Pressure work — the term that merges with the flux

Pressure pushes the fluid across the control surface and thereby does work. The rate is force times the velocity of the boundary it pushes through:

\[\frac{dW_{\text{press}}}{dt} = \int_{\text{CS}} \frac{dF\,ds}{dt} = \int_{\text{CS}} (p\,dA)\cdot V \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot{W}_p = \int_{\text{CS}} p\,(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\,dA\]
★ The Clever Move — Pressure Work Becomes Enthalpy

Look at \(\dot{W}_p = \int_{\text{CS}} p(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\,dA\). It is an integral over the same control surface, against the same factor \((\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\,dA\), as the energy flux term. They share a common factor — so we move \(\dot{W}_p\) to the right side and fold it into the flux integral. Dividing the pressure term by \(\rho\) to match the per-mass form, the energy flux integrand picks up a \(+\,p/\rho\):

\[e + \frac{p}{\rho} = \underbrace{\hat{u} + \frac{p}{\rho}}_{\hat{h}\ \text{(enthalpy)}} + \tfrac{1}{2}V^2 + gz\]

The grouping \(\hat{h} = \hat{u} + p/\rho\) is the specific enthalpy — the same enthalpy from thermodynamics. Pressure work has quietly turned internal energy into enthalpy in the flux term.

The General Energy Equation

Collecting everything — lumped heat \(\dot{Q}\), shaft work \(\dot{W}_s\), viscous work \(\dot{W}_v\), and the pressure work absorbed into the flux as enthalpy — gives the complete control-volume energy equation:

General Energy Equation (Integral Form)
\[\dot{Q} - \dot{W}_s - \dot{W}_v = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\left[\int_{\text{CV}}\!\left(\hat{u} + \tfrac{1}{2}V^2 + gz\right)\rho\,d\forall\right] + \int_{\text{CS}}\!\left(\hat{h} + \tfrac{1}{2}V^2 + gz\right)\rho\,(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\,dA\]
\(\dot{Q}\) — net heat added to the control volume (W) \(\dot{W}_s\) — shaft work done by the system (W) \(\dot{W}_v\) — viscous work (\(\approx 0\) at no-slip walls) \(\hat{u}\) — specific internal energy, used inside the CV (J/kg) \(\hat{h}=\hat{u}+p/\rho\) — specific enthalpy, used in the flux (J/kg) \(\rho\) — fluid density (kg/m³)
★ Same Storage-plus-Flux Structure as Mass & Momentum
LHSSources: heat in, minus shaft work and viscous work done by the CV
1st RHSStorage — rate of change of energy stored inside the CV (uses \(\hat{u}\), zero for steady flow)
2nd RHSFlux — net energy carried out through the CS (uses \(\hat{h}\), because pressure work merged in)

The one subtlety unique to energy: the storage term uses internal energy \(\hat{u}\) but the flux term uses enthalpy \(\hat{h}\). That asymmetry is the fingerprint of pressure work crossing the boundary.

One-Dimensional Ports & Steady Flow

Just as with mass and momentum (Ch 5.1–5.2), real problems have a handful of discrete inlets and outlets where the flow is approximately one-dimensional. The surface flux integral collapses into a sum over ports, each weighted by its mass flow rate \(\dot{m}\):

\[\int_{\text{CS}}\!\left(\hat{h}+\tfrac{1}{2}V^2+gz\right)\rho(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\,dA = \sum\left(\hat{h}+\tfrac{1}{2}V^2+gz\right)_{\text{out}}\dot{m}_{\text{out}} - \sum\left(\hat{h}+\tfrac{1}{2}V^2+gz\right)_{\text{in}}\dot{m}_{\text{in}}\]

For steady flow the storage term vanishes (nothing inside the CV changes with time), leaving a clean balance between sources and the net port flux:

Steady-Flow Energy Equation (Multiple Ports)
\[\dot{Q} - \dot{W}_s - \dot{W}_v = \sum\left(\hat{h}+\tfrac{1}{2}V^2+gz\right)_{\text{out}}\dot{m}_{\text{out}} - \sum\left(\hat{h}+\tfrac{1}{2}V^2+gz\right)_{\text{in}}\dot{m}_{\text{in}}\]

The most common case has a single inlet and a single outlet. Conservation of mass then forces \(\dot{m}_{\text{out}} = \dot{m}_{\text{in}} = \dot{m}\). Dividing through by \(\dot{m}\) converts every rate into a per-unit-mass quantity:

Per-Unit-Mass Quantities
\[q = \frac{\dot{Q}}{\dot{m}}, \qquad w_s = \frac{\dot{W}_s}{\dot{m}}, \qquad w_v = \frac{\dot{W}_v}{\dot{m}}\]
\(q\) — specific heat transfer (J/kg) \(w_s\) — specific shaft work (J/kg) \(w_v\) — specific viscous work (J/kg, \(\approx 0\))

Assigning the inlet as point 1 and the outlet as point 2, the single-stream steady-flow energy equation becomes:

Specific Energy Equation (“used by mechanical engineers”)
\[\hat{h}_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}V_1^2 + gz_1 = \left(\hat{h}_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}V_2^2 + gz_2\right) - q + w_s + w_v\]
\(\hat{h}_1,\hat{h}_2\) — specific enthalpy at inlet / outlet (J/kg) \(V_1,V_2\) — speed at inlet / outlet (m/s) \(z_1,z_2\) — elevation at inlet / outlet (m) \(q\) — specific heat added (J/kg) \(w_s\) — specific shaft work (J/kg)

Read it as: energy entering at 1 = energy leaving at 2, adjusted for heat added and work done along the way.

The Head Equation (Generalized Bernoulli)

Engineers prefer to measure energy not per unit mass but per unit weight — a quantity with units of length called head. Dividing the specific energy equation by \(g\) converts every term into metres of fluid. Then substituting the enthalpy definition \(\hat{h} = \hat{u} + p/\rho\) (and recalling \(\gamma = \rho g\)) gives:

\[\left(\frac{p_1}{\gamma} + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} + z_1\right) = \left(\frac{p_2}{\gamma} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} + z_2\right) + \underbrace{\frac{(\hat{u}_2 - \hat{u}_1) - q}{g} + \frac{w_v}{g}}_{\text{total head loss } h_{\text{loss}}} + \underbrace{\frac{w_s}{g}}_{\text{shaft head } h_s}\]
Head Loss and Shaft Head

Two new lumped quantities emerge from the algebra:

  • Total head loss \(h_{\text{loss}} = \dfrac{(\hat{u}_2-\hat{u}_1)-q}{g} + \dfrac{w_v}{g}\) — the irreversible losses. The first piece is the internal-energy loss (fluid heating up, minus any heat you removed); the second is the viscous-force loss. Friction always raises \(\hat{u}\), so \(h_{\text{loss}}\) is always positive — energy you can never get back.
  • Shaft head \(h_s = \dfrac{w_s}{g}\) — the head added or removed by machinery.

The shaft head is itself split into a pump contribution (adds head to the flow) and a turbine contribution (removes head). With the sign convention that a pump supplies head and a turbine extracts it:

\[h_s = -h_{\text{pump}} + h_{\text{turbine}}\]

Substituting yields the equation most mechanical and civil engineers actually use day to day:

Head Equation (the “engineering Bernoulli”)
\[\left(\frac{p_1}{\gamma} + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} + z_1\right) = \left(\frac{p_2}{\gamma} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} + z_2\right) + h_{\text{loss}} - h_{\text{pump}} + h_{\text{turbine}}\]
\(\dfrac{p}{\gamma}\) — pressure head (m) \(\dfrac{V^2}{2g}\) — velocity head (m) \(z\) — elevation head (m) \(h_{\text{loss}}\) — total head loss from friction (m) \(h_{\text{pump}}\) — head added by a pump (m) \(h_{\text{turbine}}\) — head removed by a turbine (m) \(\gamma=\rho g\) — specific weight (N/m³)
★ Exam Warning — “h” Means Head Here, Not Enthalpy

This equation reuses the letter \(h\), but here it stands for head (a length), not enthalpy \(\hat{h}\). Watch the context. Also memorise the sign pattern on the right side: + head loss, − pump, + turbine. A pump adds energy so it enters with a minus (it lowers the head the downstream side needs); a turbine extracts energy so it enters with a plus. Getting this sign backwards is the single most common mistake in pump/turbine problems.

★ Connection — This Is Bernoulli with Extra Terms

Strip away the loss, pump, and turbine terms (\(h_{\text{loss}}=h_{\text{pump}}=h_{\text{turbine}}=0\), i.e. ideal frictionless flow with no machinery) and the head equation collapses to exactly Bernoulli’s equation in head form from Ch 3: \(\frac{p_1}{\gamma}+\frac{V_1^2}{2g}+z_1 = \frac{p_2}{\gamma}+\frac{V_2^2}{2g}+z_2\). The head equation is the generalized Bernoulli equation — Bernoulli with friction and machines bolted on. Bernoulli was always the special, lossless case of this more honest statement.

Pump and Turbine Work

Pumps — raising the head

A pump pushes mechanical energy into the fluid, raising its pressure and therefore its head. From the shaft-head definition, the specific work delivered to the fluid is \(w_s = g\,h_{\text{pump}}\), so the total hydraulic power (useful power added to the fluid) is:

Pump Hydraulic Power
\[\dot{W}_f = \dot{m}\,w_s = \rho\,Q\,g\,h_{\text{pump}}\]
\(\dot{W}_f\) — useful power added to the fluid (W) \(\dot{m}=\rho Q\) — mass flow rate (kg/s) \(Q\) — volumetric flow rate (m³/s) \(h_{\text{pump}}\) — pump head (m) \(\rho\) — fluid density (kg/m³)

Real pumps are not perfect — the shaft consumes more power than reaches the fluid. The ratio is the pump efficiency:

Pump Efficiency
\[\eta_{\text{pump}} = \frac{\dot{W}_f}{\dot{W}_{\text{pump}}} = \frac{\text{useful power added to fluid}}{\text{consumed (shaft) power}}\]
\(\eta_{\text{pump}}\) — pump efficiency (\(0 < \eta < 1\)) \(\dot{W}_f\) — useful power delivered to fluid (W) \(\dot{W}_{\text{pump}}\) — power consumed at the shaft (W)

Turbines — extracting the head

A turbine does the reverse: it pulls mechanical work out of the fluid, lowering its head. The power extracted from the fluid is \(\dot{W}_f = \rho Q g\,h_{\text{turbine}}\), and the turbine efficiency compares the useful shaft power obtained to the power the fluid gives up:

Turbine Power & Efficiency
\[\dot{W}_f = \dot{m}\,w_s = \rho\,Q\,g\,h_{\text{turbine}}, \qquad \eta_{\text{turbine}} = \frac{\dot{W}_{\text{turbine}}}{\dot{W}_f} = \frac{\text{useful power on shaft}}{\text{total power received from fluid}}\]
\(\dot{W}_f\) — total power received from the fluid (W) \(h_{\text{turbine}}\) — turbine head extracted (m) \(\dot{W}_{\text{turbine}}\) — useful power delivered to the shaft (W) \(\eta_{\text{turbine}}\) — turbine efficiency (\(0 < \eta < 1\))

Notice the efficiency definition flips between pump and turbine. For a pump, the fluid is the output (useful power on top); for a turbine, the shaft is the output (useful power on top). In both cases the useful quantity goes in the numerator — just identify which side is “useful.”

Example 1 — Pump Power & Efficiency

Example 1: Ideal Pumping Power and Pump Efficiency

Problem: A pump delivers \(Q = 0.3\) m³/s of water. The suction (inlet) pipe has diameter \(D_1 = 0.25\) m, where the static pressure is \(p_1 = -25\) kPa (gage). The discharge (outlet) pipe has diameter \(D_2 = 0.2\) m, where the pressure is \(p_2 = 700\) kPa (gage). The discharge pipe is \(10\) m above the suction pipe. (a) Find the ideal pumping power. (b) Find the pump efficiency if \(310\) kW is required to drive it. Take \(\rho_{\text{water}} = 1000\) kg/m³.

Fig 5.4.1 — Pump control volume: suction at point 1 (low pressure, larger pipe), discharge at point 2 (high pressure, smaller pipe, 10 m higher).

Step 1 — Velocities from continuity (incompressible):

The flow rate fixes the velocity at each section through \(Q = A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2\), with \(A = \frac{\pi}{4}D^2\):

\[V_1 = \frac{Q}{\frac{\pi}{4}D_1^2} = \frac{0.3}{\frac{\pi}{4}(0.25)^2} = 6.11\ \text{m/s}\]
\[V_2 = \frac{Q}{\frac{\pi}{4}D_2^2} = \frac{0.3}{\frac{\pi}{4}(0.2)^2} = 9.55\ \text{m/s}\]

And the mass flow rate:

\[\dot{m} = \rho Q = \left(1000\ \tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3}\right)\!\left(0.3\ \tfrac{\text{m}^3}{\text{s}}\right) = 300\ \tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}\]

Step 2 — Pump head from the head equation:

This is an ideal pump, so neglect head loss (\(h_{\text{loss}}=0\)) and there is no turbine (\(h_{\text{turbine}}=0\)). The head equation between inlet (1) and outlet (2):

\[\frac{p_1}{\gamma} + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} + z_1 = \frac{p_2}{\gamma} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} + z_2 - h_{\text{pump}}\]

Solve for \(h_{\text{pump}}\) (with \(\gamma = \rho g = 1000 \times 9.81 = 9810\) N/m³ and \(z_2 - z_1 = 10\) m):

\[h_{\text{pump}} = \frac{p_2 - p_1}{\gamma} + \frac{V_2^2 - V_1^2}{2g} + (z_2 - z_1)\]
\[h_{\text{pump}} = \frac{700{,}000 - (-25{,}000)}{9810} + \frac{(9.55)^2 - (6.11)^2}{2(9.81)} + 10 = 73.9 + 2.7 + 10\]
\[\boxed{h_{\text{pump}} = 86.65\ \text{m}}\]

Step 3 (a) — Ideal pumping power:

The hydraulic (ideal) power is the power delivered to the fluid:

\[\dot{W}_f = \dot{m}\,g\,h_{\text{pump}} = \left(300\ \tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}\right)\!\left(9.81\ \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}\right)\!(86.65\ \text{m})\]
\[\boxed{\dot{W}_f = 255\ \text{kW} \quad (\text{ideal power})}\]

Step 4 (b) — Pump efficiency:

The shaft consumes 310 kW but only 255 kW reaches the fluid:

\[\eta_{\text{pump}} = \frac{\dot{W}_f}{\dot{W}_{\text{pump}}} = \frac{255\ \text{kW}}{310\ \text{kW}}\]
\[\boxed{\eta_{\text{pump}} = 0.82 \quad (82\%)}\]

Therefore: the pump must deliver \(h_{\text{pump}} = 86.65\) m of head, requiring an ideal hydraulic power of \(255\) kW; running at \(310\) kW, the pump is \(82\%\) efficient.

🧠 Thought process

This is a single-inlet / single-outlet steady-flow problem with a machine in the control volume — the head equation is the right tool. The two pressures and the elevation change make it tempting to reach for raw energy, but heads keep the bookkeeping in metres and the pump term explicit.

The chain is always the same: continuity first (get \(V_1, V_2, \dot{m}\)), then the head equation to isolate \(h_{\text{pump}}\), then power \(=\dot{m}g\,h_{\text{pump}}\), then efficiency. Note the negative inlet gage pressure is physical — the suction side is below atmospheric — and it correctly increases the head the pump must supply.

Kinetic Energy Correction Factor

The head equation we just used quietly assumed that the velocity head \(V^2/2g\) can be built from a single velocity \(V\). But real flow in a pipe is not uniform — it is faster in the centre and zero at the walls. So which \(V\) do we use, and is the average velocity good enough?

Fig 5.4.2 — Velocity varies across the pipe: \(u(r)\) is maximum on the centerline and zero at the walls. The average velocity alone does not capture the true kinetic energy.
Why the Average Velocity Falls Short

The average velocity \(V_{\text{av}}\) is defined to reproduce the correct mass flow — it is the uniform speed that carries the same \(\dot{m}\). But kinetic energy goes as \(V^2\) (in fact the flux goes as \(V^3\)), and the average of the cubes is not the cube of the average. Faster fluid in the core carries disproportionately more kinetic energy than \(V_{\text{av}}\) suggests. We patch this with a dimensionless kinetic energy correction factor \(\alpha\), defined so the true KE flux equals \(\alpha\) times the KE flux you would compute from \(V_{\text{av}}\):

\[\int\left(\tfrac{1}{2}V^2\right)\rho(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\,dA \equiv \alpha\left(\tfrac{1}{2}V_{\text{av}}^2\right)\dot{m}\]

where, for incompressible flow, the average velocity is \(V_{\text{av}} = \frac{1}{A}\int u\,dA\).

For parallel streamlines normal to the cross-section, writing the KE flux as \(\frac{1}{2}\rho\int u^3\,dA = \frac{1}{2}\rho\,\alpha\,V_{\text{av}}^3 A\) and solving gives an explicit integral for \(\alpha\):

Kinetic Energy Correction Factor
\[\alpha = \frac{1}{A}\int\left(\frac{u}{V_{\text{av}}}\right)^{3}dA\]
\(\alpha\) — KE correction factor (dimensionless, \(\geq 1\)) \(u\) — local axial velocity at radius \(r\) (m/s) \(V_{\text{av}}\) — average velocity over the cross-section (m/s) \(A\) — cross-sectional area (m²)

Inserting \(\alpha\) on each velocity head gives the complete incompressible steady-flow head equation:

Complete Head Equation (with KE Correction)
\[\left(\frac{p_1}{\gamma} + \frac{\alpha_1 V_1^2}{2g} + z_1\right) = \left(\frac{p_2}{\gamma} + \frac{\alpha_2 V_2^2}{2g} + z_2\right) + h_{\text{loss}} - h_{\text{pump}} + h_{\text{turbine}}\]
★ Typical Values of α

The correction factor depends entirely on the shape of the velocity profile:

  • \(\alpha \approx 2\) for fully developed laminar flow (parabolic profile — very non-uniform, so the correction is large)
  • \(1.04 < \alpha < 1.11\) for turbulent flow (much flatter profile — the average is nearly representative, so \(\alpha \approx 1\))

In most turbulent engineering flows \(\alpha\) is so close to 1 that it is dropped — which is exactly why Example 1 above could use plain \(V^2/2g\). But for laminar flow, ignoring \(\alpha\) underestimates the kinetic energy by roughly a factor of two, and it must be kept.

Recap — The Energy Toolkit

What You Now Have
  • General energy equation — RTT with \(\beta = e\); storage uses \(\hat{u}\), flux uses enthalpy \(\hat{h}\) because pressure work merged in.
  • Specific energy equation — steady, single-stream, per unit mass: \(\hat{h}_1+\tfrac{1}{2}V_1^2+gz_1 = (\hat{h}_2+\tfrac{1}{2}V_2^2+gz_2)-q+w_s+w_v\).
  • Head equation — per unit weight (metres of fluid): \(\frac{p}{\gamma}+\frac{V^2}{2g}+z\) at each section, plus \(+h_{\text{loss}}-h_{\text{pump}}+h_{\text{turbine}}\). This is Bernoulli generalized for friction and machines.
  • Pump: \(\dot{W}_f = \rho Q g\,h_{\text{pump}}\), \(\eta_{\text{pump}} = \dot{W}_f / \dot{W}_{\text{pump}}\) (fluid is the useful output).
  • Turbine: \(\dot{W}_f = \rho Q g\,h_{\text{turbine}}\), \(\eta_{\text{turbine}} = \dot{W}_{\text{turbine}} / \dot{W}_f\) (shaft is the useful output).
  • KE correction \(\alpha\): needed when the velocity profile is non-uniform; \(\approx 2\) laminar, \(\approx 1\) turbulent.

This closes the RTT chapter: mass, linear momentum, angular momentum, and now energy — the four control-volume conservation laws that underpin essentially all of applied fluid mechanics.

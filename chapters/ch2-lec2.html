2.2 — Hydrostatic Forces on Curved Surfaces

In Lecture 2.1 every surface was flat, so the pressure pushed in a single direction and the whole force could be found with one calculation: \(F_R = \gamma \bar{h} A\). Real structures are rarely that obliging. Aquarium tunnels, arch dams, tank ends, pipe walls and gate lips are all curved, and that one fact changes the whole calculation.

The physics has not changed — hydrostatic pressure still acts perpendicular to the surface at every point (a fluid at rest carries no shear, so the only stress left is pressure, and pressure always pushes normal to the surface). What changes is that on a curved surface the direction of that normal swings around as you move along the wall. Each little patch of the surface is pushed a slightly different way, so we can no longer just multiply one pressure by one area.

Hydrostatic pressure acts perpendicular to the surface at every point. On a curved wall the direction of that normal force changes continuously along the surface, so the load cannot be captured by a single pressure × area product.

The strategy: split the force into two simple problems

The trick is to stop fighting the curve and instead resolve the resultant force into a horizontal component \(F_H\) and a vertical component \(F_V\). Each component, taken on its own, turns out to be something we already know how to handle:

  • The horizontal part behaves exactly like a flat-plate problem from 2.1 — we just use the vertical projection of the curved surface.
  • The vertical part is simply the weight of the fluid sitting above the surface (real or imagined).

Once we have both components we glue them back together into a single resultant force \(F\) acting along a single line — the line of action. So the curved-surface problem becomes: replace the messy distributed load (left) with one equivalent force on one known line (right).

Actual condition vs. equivalent model. The distributed hydrostatic load on the curved surface is replaced by a single resultant force \(F\) acting along a definite line of action.

What we are solving for

To describe that single equivalent force completely we need its magnitude and its line of action (the angle it makes and a point it passes through). Those break down into four quantities we chase one at a time:

The four targets: horizontal force \(F_H\), vertical force \(F_V\), and the two centre-of-pressure coordinates \(x_{CP}\) (where \(F_V\) acts) and \(z_{CP}\) (where \(F_H\) acts). From these we assemble the resultant \(F\) and its angle \(\theta\).
Resultant Force (magnitude)

\[ F = \sqrt{F_H^{\,2} + F_V^{\,2}} \]

\(F\) — resultant hydrostatic force (N) \(F_H\) — horizontal component (N) \(F_V\) — vertical component (N)

When to use: Once both components are known, this Pythagorean combination gives the magnitude of the single equivalent force. \(F_H\) and \(F_V\) are perpendicular, so they add as legs of a right triangle.

When NOT to use: Do not add \(F_H\) and \(F_V\) arithmetically (\(F \neq F_H + F_V\)) — they act in different directions.

Line of Action (angle)

\[ \theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{F_V}{F_H}\right) \]

\(\theta\) — angle of the resultant below the horizontal \(F_V\) — vertical component (N) \(F_H\) — horizontal component (N)

When to use: Gives the direction of the resultant. The line of action passes through the point located by \(x_{CP}\) and \(z_{CP}\), at this angle.

★ Note: For a circular arc, the resultant always points through the centre of the circle — every pressure arrow is a radius, so they all aim at one point. A handy check.

The plan, then, is to find \(F_H\), \(F_V\), \(x_{CP}\) and \(z_{CP}\) one by one. We work them out from a free-body diagram of the wedge of fluid sitting on the curved surface.

Horizontal component \(F_H\)

Picture the fluid block that rests against the curved surface. In the horizontal direction the only fluid pressure that matters is whatever pushes sideways, and that is captured exactly by the vertical plane onto which the curved surface projects (the segment \(ac\) below). The curved surface and its flat vertical shadow enclose the same sideways push, so:

The horizontal force on a curved surface equals the hydrostatic force on its vertical projection — and that is just the flat-plate result from 2.1.

In the horizontal direction the problem collapses to a flat plate: the curved surface \(ab\) is replaced by its vertical projection \(ac\), and the triangular pressure prism over \(ac\) gives \(F_H\). \(p_{CG}\) and \(A_{ac}\) are the pressure at the projection’s centroid and its area.
Horizontal Component

\[ F_H = \int_a^c p\,dA = p_{CG}\,A_{ac} = (\gamma\, h_{CG} + p_a)\,A_{ac} \]

\(F_H\) — horizontal force (N) \(p_{CG}\) — pressure at the centroid of the projection (Pa) \(\gamma\) — specific weight of fluid (N/m³) \(h_{CG}\) — depth of projection centroid below free surface (m) \(p_a\) — surface (gauge) pressure (Pa) \(A_{ac}\) — area of the vertical projection (m²)

When to use: Always, for the sideways force on any curved surface. Project onto the vertical plane, find the centroid depth of that projection, and treat it as a flat plate.

When NOT to use: \(A_{ac}\) is the projected (flat) area, never the curved surface area. In an open tank \(p_a = 0\) (gauge); in a pressurised chamber keep the \(p_a\) term.

Where does \(F_H\) act? Since \(F_H\) is nothing more than a flat-plate force on the projection \(ac\), its line sits below the centroid of \(ac\) by exactly the centre-of-pressure offset from Lecture 2.1:

Vertical Location of \(F_H\) (\(z_{CP}\))

\[ z_{CP} = h_{CG} + \frac{I_{xx}}{h_{CG}\,A_{ac}} \]

\(z_{CP}\) — depth of \(F_H\)’s line below the free surface (m) \(h_{CG}\) — centroid depth of the projection (m) \(I_{xx}\) — 2nd moment of area of the projection about its own centroid (m⁴) \(A_{ac}\) — projected area (m²)

When to use: To locate the height at which the horizontal force acts. This is the same \(y_{cp}\) idea from 2.1 (\(y_{CP} = -I_{xx}/(h_{CG}A)\)), measured from the surface as \(z_{CP} = z_{CG} + y_{CP}\). \(I_{xx}\) belongs to the projection, not the curved surface.

★ Tip: The offset \(I_{xx}/(h_{CG}A)\) shrinks as depth grows — deep surfaces have \(F_H\) acting essentially at the centroid.

Vertical component \(F_V\)

Now hold the same fluid block in your mind and look at the vertical direction. The surface has to support the entire weight of fluid stacked directly above it, all the way up to the free surface. That weight, plus any push from the surface pressure on top, is the vertical force.

To compute it we slice the column above the curved surface into easy shapes (here a rectangle giving \(W_1\) and a quarter-circle giving \(W_2\)) and add their weights:

Free-body diagram of the fluid wedge. The vertical force equals the total weight of fluid above the surface — split into convenient sub-volumes (\(W_1 = \gamma\forall_{bcde}\), \(W_2 = \gamma\forall_{abc}\)) — plus the surface-pressure load \(W_{air} = p_a A_{de}\). The horizontal forces \(F_1\) on the dashed cut faces are internal and cancel.
Vertical Component

\[ F_V = W_1 + W_2 + W_{air} = \gamma\,\forall_{\text{above}} + p_a A_{de} \]

\(F_V\) — vertical force (N) \(\gamma\) — specific weight of fluid (N/m³) \(\forall_{\text{above}}\) — volume of fluid above the surface, up to the free surface (m³) \(p_a\) — surface (gauge) pressure (Pa) \(A_{de}\) — horizontal projected area under the surface load (m²)

When to use: For the vertical force on any curved surface. Break the column above the surface into simple volumes, weigh each, and add. In an open tank \(W_{air} = p_a A = 0\) (gauge).

When NOT to use: Do not use the curved surface area × some pressure — the vertical force is a weight of fluid, i.e. \(\gamma \times\) volume.

★ Key Subtlety: Real vs. Imaginary Fluid Above

The vertical force is always the weight of the column between the surface and the free surface — but that fluid is not always physically there.

Fluid sits on top of the surface (like the arc below): the column is real, its weight pushes the surface down, and \(F_V\) points down.

Fluid pushes up from underneath (like the hemisphere example): imagine the volume above the surface filled with the same fluid. The surrounding fluid supports exactly that imaginary weight, so \(F_V\) equals \(\gamma\) times that volume but points up. This is the same buoyancy logic we will formalise in 2.3.

Where does \(F_V\) act? The vertical force acts through the combined centroid of the fluid volume above the surface. We find its horizontal position by balancing moments of the sub-weights about a convenient axis (\(\sum M_o = 0\)):

Horizontal Location of \(F_V\) (\(x_{CP}\))

\[ x_{CP} = \frac{W_1 x_1 + W_2 x_2 + W_{air} x_{air}}{F_V} \]

\(x_{CP}\) — horizontal position of \(F_V\)’s line (m) \(W_i\) — weight of each sub-volume (N) \(x_i\) — centroid arm of each sub-volume from the axis (m) \(F_V\) — total vertical force (N)

When to use: To locate the line of the vertical force. It is a weighted average of the sub-volume centroids — heavier pieces pull the line toward themselves. The horizontal forces create no moment, so they drop out.

★ Tip: The centroid of a quarter circle of radius \(r\) sits \(\dfrac{4r}{3\pi}\) from each straight edge — memorise this; it appears constantly.

With \(F_H\), \(F_V\), \(x_{CP}\) and \(z_{CP}\) in hand, the resultant \(F = \sqrt{F_H^2 + F_V^2}\) acts at angle \(\theta = \tan^{-1}(F_V/F_H)\) through the point those coordinates define. Time to put it to work.

Example 1 — Hydrostatic Force on a Circular Arc

Surface \(AB\) is a circular arc of radius \(2\text{ m}\) and width \(1\text{ m}\) into the page. The distance \(EB\) is \(4\text{ m}\). The fluid above \(AB\) is water, and atmospheric pressure acts on the free surface and on the underside of \(AB\). Find (a) the magnitude of the hydrostatic force on \(AB\), and (b) its line of action. Take \(\gamma_{\text{water}} = 9810\text{ N/m}^3\) at 10°C.

Example 1 setup. The quarter-circle arc \(AB\) (centre \(C\)) supports the water column above it. \(W_1\) is the rectangular block \(BCDE\); \(W_2\) is the quarter-circle wedge \(ABC\).
▸ Show full solution

Thought process

This is a curved-surface problem, so I will find \(F_H\) and \(F_V\) separately and recombine. The water sits above the arc, so \(F_V\) is the real weight of the column above it (pointing down), split into the rectangle \(BCDE\) and the quarter-circle \(ABC\). The tank is open, so \(p_a = 0\) gauge and there is no \(W_{air}\) term.

\(F_H\) is the flat-plate force on the vertical projection of the arc, which is the segment \(AC\) (height \(= r = 2\text{ m}\)). For the line of action I balance moments for \(x_{CP}\) and use the centre-of-pressure offset for \(z_{CP}\).

Given: \(r = 2\text{ m}\), width \(w = 1\text{ m}\), \(EB = 4\text{ m}\), \(\gamma = 9.81\text{ kN/m}^3\), open tank (\(p_a = 0\)).

Find: (a) resultant \(F\); (b) line of action (\(\theta\), and \(x_{CP}\), \(z_{CP}\)).

Geometry. The arc centre \(C\) lies at depth \(EB = 4\text{ m}\) (level with \(B\)); point \(A\) is a further \(r = 2\text{ m}\) below \(C\), at depth \(6\text{ m}\). The vertical projection \(AC\) therefore spans depths 4 m to 6 m, so its centroid is at \(h_{CG} = 5\text{ m}\), and \(A_{AC} = (2\text{ m})(1\text{ m}) = 2\text{ m}^2\).

1

Vertical force — weight of the column above

Rectangle \(BCDE\) (\(4\text{ m}\) tall \(\times\) \(2\text{ m}\) wide \(\times\) \(1\text{ m}\) deep):

\[ W_1 = \gamma\,\forall_{BCDE} = (9.81\text{ kN/m}^3)(4\text{ m})(2\text{ m})(1\text{ m}) = 78.5\text{ kN} \]

Quarter-circle wedge \(ABC\) (area \(\tfrac14\pi r^2\), depth \(1\text{ m}\)):

\[ W_2 = \gamma\,\forall_{ABC} = (9.81\text{ kN/m}^3)\big(\tfrac14\pi (2\text{ m})^2\big)(1\text{ m}) = 30.8\text{ kN} \]

\[ F_V = W_1 + W_2 = 78.5 + 30.8 = 109.3\text{ kN} \quad(\downarrow) \]

2

Horizontal force — flat plate on projection \(AC\)

\[ F_H = \gamma\, h_{CG}\, A_{AC} = (9.81\text{ kN/m}^3)(5\text{ m})(2\text{ m})(1\text{ m}) = 98.1\text{ kN} \quad(\rightarrow) \]

3

Resultant magnitude

\[ F = \sqrt{F_H^2 + F_V^2} = \sqrt{(98.1)^2 + (109.3)^2} = \sqrt{9624 + 11946} = 146.9\text{ kN} \]

4

Line of action — angle

\[ \tan\theta = \frac{F_V}{F_H} = \frac{109.3}{98.1} = 1.11 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta = 48^\circ \]

5

Line of action — where \(F_V\) acts (\(x_{CP}\))

Arms from the vertical line \(CD\): rectangle centroid \(x_1 = 1\text{ m}\); quarter-circle centroid \(x_2 = \dfrac{4r}{3\pi} = \dfrac{4(2)}{3\pi} = 0.849\text{ m}\).

\[ x_{CP} = \frac{W_1 x_1 + W_2 x_2}{F_V} = \frac{(78.5)(1) + (30.8)(0.849)}{109.3} = 0.957\text{ m} \]

6

Line of action — where \(F_H\) acts (\(z_{CP}\))

For the projection \(AC\): \(I_{xx} = \dfrac{w\,h^3}{12} = \dfrac{(1)(2)^3}{12} = 0.667\text{ m}^4\).

\[ z_{CP} = h_{CG} + \frac{I_{xx}}{h_{CG}\,A_{AC}} = 5 + \frac{0.667}{(5)(2)} = 5.067\text{ m below the surface} \]

(i.e. \(5.067 - 4 = 1.067\text{ m}\) below \(C\), matching the figure.)

Result: a resultant of \(146.9\text{ kN}\) at \(48^\circ\) below the horizontal, passing \(0.957\text{ m}\) horizontally from line \(CD\) and \(1.067\text{ m}\) below \(C\). (Being a circular arc, this line also passes through the centre \(C\).)

Answer

(a) \(F = 146.9\text{ kN}\).   (b) acting at \(\theta = 48^\circ\) below horizontal, through the point \(x_{CP} = 0.957\text{ m}\) (from \(CD\)) and \(z_{CP} = 5.067\text{ m}\) deep.

Used: Vertical Component (#35), Horizontal Component (#34), Resultant Force (#32), Line-of-Action Angle (#33), \(x_{CP}\) (#36), \(z_{CP}\) (#37) — all on the Equation Sheet.

Example 2 — Force on a Hemisphere in a Pressurised Chamber (3-D)

Find the hydrostatic force in all three directions on the hemispherical surface shown. Water is enclosed in a chamber whose surface pressure is \(p_a = 35\text{ kPa}\). The hemisphere has radius \(R = 0.9\text{ m}\) and its centre is \(3\text{ m}\) below the water surface.

Example 2 setup. The hemisphere bulges into the chamber; water pushes on its curved face. The surface pressure \(p_a = 35\text{ kPa}\) acts on the free surface above.
▸ Show full solution

Thought process

Same decomposition, now in 3-D. The horizontal force uses the vertical projection of the hemisphere — a full circle of radius \(R\) — and because the chamber is pressurised I add \(p_a\) to the centroid pressure. The vertical force is the buoyancy-style weight of the displaced volume: water pushes up from below, so I imagine the hemisphere volume filled with water and take its weight (the uniform \(p_a\) contributes nothing vertically — on a hemisphere its vertical effects cancel by symmetry). The lateral direction is symmetric, so that force is zero.

Given: \(R = 0.9\text{ m}\), centre depth \(h_{CG} = 3\text{ m}\), \(p_a = 35\text{ kPa}\), \(\gamma = 9.81\text{ kN/m}^3\).

Find: \(F_x\) (horizontal), \(F_z\) (vertical), \(F_y\) (lateral).

1

Horizontal direction \(F_x\) — flat plate on the circular projection

Projected area \(A_{AC} = \pi R^2 = \pi(0.9\text{ m})^2\). Centroid pressure includes the chamber pressure:

\[ F_x = p_{CG}\,A_{AC} = (\gamma\, h_{CG} + p_a)\,A_{AC} \]

\[ = \big((9.81\text{ kN/m}^3)(3\text{ m}) + 35\text{ kPa}\big)\big(\pi (0.9\text{ m})^2\big) = 163.9\text{ kN} \]

2

Vertical direction \(F_z\) — weight of displaced volume

By the buoyancy lemma, the vertical force equals the weight of water that would fill the hemisphere (volume \(\tfrac23\pi r^3\)); the surrounding water carries it, pushing the surface up:

\[ F_z = \gamma\,\forall_{\text{hemisphere}} = \gamma\left(\tfrac23\pi r^3\right) = (9.81\text{ kN/m}^3)\left(\tfrac23\pi (0.9\text{ m})^3\right) = 15.0\text{ kN} \quad(\uparrow) \]

The uniform surface pressure \(p_a\) adds no vertical force — its vertical contributions over the hemisphere cancel.

3

Lateral direction \(F_y\) — symmetry

The hemisphere is symmetric about the vertical plane through its axis, so the lateral pushes cancel exactly:

\[ F_y = 0 \]

Answer

\(F_x = 163.9\text{ kN}\) (horizontal),   \(F_z = 15.0\text{ kN}\) (upward),   \(F_y = 0\) (lateral).

Used: Horizontal Component with surface pressure (#34), Vertical Component / displaced-volume weight (#35). Buoyancy lemma is developed further in 2.3.

★ Exam Checklist & Common Traps

The recipe: (1) \(F_H\) = flat-plate force on the vertical projection; (2) \(F_V\) = weight of fluid above the surface (real or imaginary); (3) \(z_{CP}\) from the centre-of-pressure offset, \(x_{CP}\) from a moment balance; (4) combine: \(F = \sqrt{F_H^2 + F_V^2}\), \(\theta = \tan^{-1}(F_V/F_H)\).

  1. Use the projected area for \(F_H\), never the curved area; \(A_{de}\)/volume for \(F_V\), never a pressure × curved area.
  2. Decide the direction of \(F_V\) by asking whether fluid is physically above (down) or below (up, imaginary column).
  3. In a pressurised chamber keep the \(p_a\) term in \(F_H\); for \(F_V\) on a symmetric surface uniform \(p_a\) cancels.
  4. \(I_{xx}\) and the centroid in \(z_{CP}\) belong to the projection, not the curved surface.
  5. For a circular arc, the resultant passes through the centre of the circle — a free sanity check on your line of action.

    Ch 1 — Fluid Properties & Pressure

    Density #1not on exam sheet
    \[ \htmlData{t=ρ — density (kg/m³)}{\color{#2E7D32}{\rho}} = \frac{\htmlData{t=m — mass (kg)}{\color{#E65100}{m}}}{\htmlData{t=V — volume (m³)}{\color{#E65100}{V}}} \]

    The mass of fluid per unit volume — the most fundamental intensive property. For incompressible fluids ρ = const; for gases, ρ varies with p and T via the ideal gas law.

    Where it comes from: Direct definition. Continuum limit: ρ = lim(δm/δV) as δV → δV* ≈ 10⁻⁹ mm³.

    ✓ Use when
    asked for mass per volume, need ρ for any governing equation (continuity, momentum, energy), unit conversion.
    ✗ Not for
    weight per volume — that's γ = ρg. Don't confuse with SG (dimensionless ratio).
    \[ \rho = \frac{m}{V} \]

    Mass per unit volume. SI: kg/m³. BG: slugs/ft³.

    \[ \rho = m / V \]
    Specific Weight #2
    \[ \htmlData{t=γ — specific weight (N/m³)}{\color{#2E7D32}{\gamma}} = \htmlData{t=ρ — density (kg/m³)}{\color{#2E7D32}{\rho}} \, \htmlData{t=g — gravitational acceleration (9.81 m/s²)}{\color{#2E7D32}{g}} \]

    Weight of fluid per unit volume — density scaled by gravitational acceleration.

    ✓ Use when
    hydrostatic pressure (p = γh), buoyancy, manometers. Often more convenient than ρ in BG units.
    ✗ Not for
    interchangeable with density — γ includes gravity. γ_water = 9810 N/m³, not 1000.
    \[ \gamma = \rho g \]

    Weight per unit volume. SI: N/m³. BG: lbf/ft³.

    \[ \gamma = \rho g \]
    Specific Gravity #3
    \[ \htmlData{t=SG — specific gravity (dimensionless)}{\color{#2E7D32}{SG}} = \frac{\htmlData{t=ρ — density of fluid}{\color{#2E7D32}{\rho}}}{\htmlData{t=ρ_W — density of water at 4°C (1000 kg/m³)}{\color{#1565C0}{\rho_W}}} \]

    Dimensionless ratio of a fluid's density to water's density at 4°C (ρ_W = 1000 kg/m³).

    ✓ Use when
    quick comparison with water, buoyancy (SG > 1 → sinks), manometer problems with multiple fluids.
    ✗ Not for
    plugging directly into equations — always convert: ρ = SG × ρ_W first.
    \[ SG = \rho / \rho_W \]

    Dimensionless density ratio. Reference: water at 4°C.

    \[ SG = \rho / \rho_W \]
    Newton's Law of Viscosity #4
    \[ \htmlData{t=τ — shear stress (Pa)}{\color{#D32F2F}{\tau}} = \htmlData{t=μ — dynamic viscosity (Pa·s)}{\color{#2E7D32}{\mu}} \frac{d\htmlData{t=u — velocity (m/s)}{\color{#E65100}{u}}}{d\htmlData{t=y — distance from wall (m)}{\color{#1565C0}{y}}} \]

    Shear stress in a fluid is proportional to the velocity gradient. The constant μ is the dynamic viscosity. The no-slip condition requires u = 0 at a stationary wall.

    Where it comes from: Strain rate of a fluid element under applied shear.

    ✓ Use when
    finding shear force on plates/surfaces, determining viscosity from experiment, pipe flow wall shear.
    ✗ Not for
    non-Newtonian fluids (variable μ). Not for turbulent flow without modification.
    \[ \tau = \mu \, du/dy \]

    Shear stress = viscosity × velocity gradient. Newtonian fluids only.

    \[ \tau = \mu \, du/dy \]
    Kinematic Viscosity #5
    \[ \htmlData{t=ν — kinematic viscosity (m²/s)}{\color{#2E7D32}{\nu}} = \frac{\htmlData{t=μ — dynamic viscosity (Pa·s)}{\color{#2E7D32}{\mu}}}{\htmlData{t=ρ — density (kg/m³)}{\color{#2E7D32}{\rho}}} \]

    Dynamic viscosity normalized by density. Has dimensions of diffusivity (m²/s).

    ✓ Use when
    Reynolds number: Re = VL/ν. Comparing fluids of different densities. Navier-Stokes form.
    ✗ Not for
    finding shear stress directly — need μ for that (τ = μ du/dy).
    \[ \nu = \mu / \rho \]

    Viscosity normalized by density. SI: m²/s. Used in Reynolds number.

    \[ \nu = \mu / \rho \]
    Droplet / Bubble Pressure #6
    \[ \htmlData{t=Δp — pressure difference (Pa)}{\color{#D32F2F}{\Delta p}} = \frac{2\htmlData{t=Υ — surface tension (N/m)}{\color{#2E7D32}{\Upsilon}}}{\htmlData{t=R — radius (m)}{\color{#E65100}{R}}} \;\text{(droplet)} \qquad \frac{4\htmlData{t=Υ — surface tension (N/m)}{\color{#2E7D32}{\Upsilon}}}{\htmlData{t=R — radius (m)}{\color{#E65100}{R}}} \;\text{(bubble)} \]

    Surface tension creates internal pressure in curved surfaces. Bubbles have two surfaces → 2× the pressure of droplets.

    ✓ Use when
    finding pressure inside droplets, bubbles, or curved menisci.
    ✗ Not for
    non-spherical geometries. Gravity effects neglected.
    \[ \Delta p = 2\Upsilon/R \;\text{(drop)},\; 4\Upsilon/R \;\text{(bubble)} \]

    Pressure excess inside curved surfaces due to surface tension.

    \[ \Delta p = 2\Upsilon/R \;\text{(drop)},\; 4\Upsilon/R \;\text{(bubble)} \]
    Capillary Height #7
    \[ \htmlData{t=h — capillary rise height (m)}{\color{#1565C0}{h}} = \frac{2\htmlData{t=Υ — surface tension (N/m)}{\color{#2E7D32}{\Upsilon}} \htmlData{t=cos θ — contact angle factor}{\color{#7B1FA2}{\cos\theta}}}{\htmlData{t=γ — specific weight (N/m³)}{\color{#2E7D32}{\gamma}} \, \htmlData{t=R — tube radius (m)}{\color{#E65100}{R}}} \]

    Height a liquid rises (or falls) in a narrow tube. Positive for wetting liquids (θ < 90°), negative for non-wetting (θ > 90°).

    ✓ Use when
    capillary tube problems, correcting manometer readings in small-diameter tubes.
    ✗ Not for
    non-circular tubes. Requires known contact angle θ and surface tension Υ.
    \[ h = 2\Upsilon\cos\theta / (\gamma R) \]

    Capillary rise. Positive = wetting (rises), negative = non-wetting (falls).

    \[ h = 2\Upsilon\cos\theta / (\gamma R) \]
    Ideal Gas Law #8
    \[ \htmlData{t=p — absolute pressure (Pa)}{\color{#D32F2F}{p}} = \htmlData{t=ρ — density (kg/m³)}{\color{#2E7D32}{\rho}} \, \htmlData{t=R — specific gas constant (J/(kg·K))}{\color{#E65100}{R}} \, \htmlData{t=T — absolute temperature (K)}{\color{#E65100}{T}} \]

    Equation of state for ideal gases — relates absolute pressure, density, and absolute temperature through the specific gas constant R.

    ✓ Use when
    finding gas density at given conditions, compressible flow, atmosphere problems.
    ✗ Not for
    very high pressures or low temperatures (real gas effects). Must use absolute p and T — never gauge or °C.
    \[ p = \rho R T \]

    Ideal gas EOS. Use absolute pressure (Pa) and temperature (K).

    \[ p = \rho R T \]
    Absolute / Gage Pressure #9
    \[ \htmlData{t=p_abs — absolute pressure (Pa)}{\color{#D32F2F}{p_{\text{abs}}}} = \htmlData{t=p_gage — gage pressure (Pa)}{\color{#D32F2F}{p_{\text{gage}}}} + \htmlData{t=p_atm — atmospheric pressure (101.325 kPa)}{\color{#D32F2F}{p_{\text{atm}}}} \]

    Absolute pressure = total from perfect vacuum. Gage = amount above atmospheric. Vacuum = amount below atmospheric.

    ✓ Use when
    converting between gage and absolute readings, ideal gas law (needs absolute), any equation requiring absolute p.
    ✗ Not for
    gage pressure can be negative (vacuum). p_abs is never negative. Don't mix reference frames.
    \[ p_{\text{abs}} = p_{\text{gage}} + p_{\text{atm}} \]

    Absolute = gage + atmospheric. p_atm ≈ 101.325 kPa at sea level.

    \[ p_{\text{abs}} = p_{\text{gage}} + p_{\text{atm}} \]
    Hydrostatic Pressure #10
    \[ \htmlData{t=p — pressure at depth h (Pa)}{\color{#D32F2F}{p}} = \htmlData{t=p_a — pressure at free surface (Pa)}{\color{#D32F2F}{p_a}} + \htmlData{t=γ — specific weight (N/m³)}{\color{#2E7D32}{\gamma}} \, \htmlData{t=h — depth below free surface (m)}{\color{#1565C0}{h}} \]

    Pressure increases linearly with depth in a static, incompressible fluid. Equivalent forms: p = p_a + ρgh.

    Where it comes from: Integrating dp/dz = −γ with z pointing up.

    ✓ Use when
    finding pressure at any depth, manometer analysis, pressure on submerged surfaces.
    ✗ Not for
    compressible fluids (γ varies with depth — use exponential atmosphere). Not for moving fluids.
    \[ p = p_a + \gamma h \]

    Pressure at depth h below free surface. Linear in incompressible fluid.

    \[ p = p_a + \gamma h \]
    Manometer Equation #11
    \[ \htmlData{t=p_A — pressure at point A}{\color{#D32F2F}{p_A}} - \htmlData{t=p_B — pressure at point B}{\color{#D32F2F}{p_B}} = \sum \htmlData{t=γ_i — specific weight of fluid i}{\color{#2E7D32}{\gamma_i}} \, \htmlData{t=Δh_i — height change in fluid i (m)}{\color{#1565C0}{\Delta h_i}} \]

    Walk from point A to point B through the manometer: go down in a fluid → add γΔh; go up → subtract γΔh. Sum all pressure jumps.

    ✓ Use when
    finding pressure difference between two points via U-tube or multi-fluid manometer.
    ✗ Not for
    dynamic flows. Watch signs — going down adds pressure, going up subtracts.
    \[ p_A - p_B = \sum \gamma_i \Delta h_i \]

    Walk through manometer: +γΔh going down, −γΔh going up.

    \[ p_A - p_B = \sum \gamma_i \Delta h_i \]

    Ch 2 — Hydrostatic Forces & Buoyancy

    Resultant Hydrostatic Force #12
    \[ \htmlData{t=F_R — resultant hydrostatic force (N)}{\color{#D32F2F}{F_R}} = \htmlData{t=γ — specific weight of fluid (N/m³)}{\color{#2E7D32}{\gamma}} \, \htmlData{t=h̄ — vertical depth to centroid (m)}{\color{#1565C0}{\bar{h}}} \, \htmlData{t=A — area of submerged surface (m²)}{\color{#1565C0}{A}} \]

    The total hydrostatic force on a submerged plane surface equals the pressure at the centroid times the area. Works for inclined and vertical surfaces.

    Where it comes from: Integrating p·dA over the surface, with p = γh varying linearly.

    ✓ Use when
    finding total force on a gate, dam face, tank wall, or any flat submerged surface.
    ✗ Not for
    curved surfaces (project onto planes first). h̄ is vertical depth to centroid, not distance along surface.
    \[ F_R = \gamma \bar{h} A \]

    Resultant force = pressure at centroid × area.

    \[ F_R = \gamma \bar{h} A \]
    Center of Pressure #13
    \[ \htmlData{t=y_cp — distance from surface to center of pressure along incline}{\color{#1565C0}{y_{cp}}} = \htmlData{t=ȳ — distance from surface to centroid along incline}{\color{#1565C0}{\bar{y}}} + \frac{\htmlData{t=I_xx — second moment of area about centroidal axis (m⁴)}{\color{#00838F}{I_{xx}}}}{\htmlData{t=ȳ — distance from surface to centroid}{\color{#1565C0}{\bar{y}}} \, \htmlData{t=A — surface area (m²)}{\color{#1565C0}{A}}} \]

    The center of pressure is always below the centroid (the extra term I_xx/(ȳA) is always positive). This offset is where F_R acts for moment equilibrium.

    ✓ Use when
    finding where the resultant force acts — needed for moment/equilibrium analysis on gates, hinges, stops.
    ✗ Not for
    ȳ is measured along the inclined surface from the free-surface line, not vertically. Use the correct I_xx for the shape.
    \[ y_{cp} = \bar{y} + I_{xx}/(\bar{y} A) \]

    Center of pressure = centroid + offset. Always below centroid.

    \[ y_{cp} = \bar{y} + I_{xx}/(\bar{y} A) \]

    Ch 1 — Fluid Properties & Pressure

    Density #1
    \[ \textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho} = \frac{\textcolor{#E65100}{m}}{\textcolor{#E65100}{V}} \]

    The mass of fluid per unit volume — the most fundamental intensive property.

    ✓ Use when
    asked for mass per volume, need ρ for continuity / momentum / energy equations.
    ✗ Not for
    weight per volume — that is γ = ρg. Do not confuse with SG (dimensionless).

    Mass per unit volume. SI: kg/m³. BG: slugs/ft³.

    Specific Weight #2
    \[ \textcolor{#2E7D32}{\gamma} = \textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho} \, \textcolor{#2E7D32}{g} \]

    Weight of fluid per unit volume — density scaled by gravity.

    ✓ Use when
    hydrostatic pressure (p = γh), buoyancy, manometers.
    ✗ Not for
    interchangeable with density — γ includes gravity. γ_water = 9810 N/m³.

    Weight per unit volume. SI: N/m³. BG: lbf/ft³.

    Specific Gravity #3
    \[ \textcolor{#2E7D32}{SG} = \frac{\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}}{\textcolor{#1565C0}{\rho_W}} \]

    Dimensionless ratio of a fluid's density to water's at 4°C.

    ✓ Use when
    quick comparison with water, buoyancy (SG > 1 sinks), manometer multi-fluid problems.
    ✗ Not for
    plugging directly into equations — convert: ρ = SG × ρ_W first.

    Dimensionless density ratio. Reference: water at 4°C.

    Newton's Law of Viscosity #4
    \[ \textcolor{#D32F2F}{\tau} = \textcolor{#2E7D32}{\mu} \frac{d\textcolor{#E65100}{u}}{d\textcolor{#1565C0}{y}} \]

    Shear stress is proportional to velocity gradient. The constant μ is the dynamic viscosity.

    ✓ Use when
    finding shear force on plates, determining viscosity from experiment, pipe flow wall shear.
    ✗ Not for
    non-Newtonian fluids (variable μ). Not for turbulent flow.

    Shear stress = viscosity × velocity gradient. Newtonian fluids only.

    Kinematic Viscosity #5
    \[ \textcolor{#2E7D32}{\nu} = \frac{\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\mu}}{\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}} \]

    Dynamic viscosity normalized by density. Has dimensions of diffusivity (m²/s).

    ✓ Use when
    Reynolds number: Re = VL/ν. Comparing fluids of different densities.
    ✗ Not for
    finding shear stress — need μ for that (τ = μ du/dy).

    Viscosity normalized by density. Used in Reynolds number.

    Droplet / Bubble Pressure #6
    \[ \textcolor{#D32F2F}{\Delta p} = \frac{2\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\Upsilon}}{\textcolor{#E65100}{R}} \;\text{(droplet)} \qquad \frac{4\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\Upsilon}}{\textcolor{#E65100}{R}} \;\text{(bubble)} \]

    Surface tension creates internal pressure. Bubbles have two surfaces → 2× droplet pressure.

    ✓ Use when
    pressure inside droplets, bubbles, or curved menisci.
    ✗ Not for
    non-spherical geometries. Gravity effects neglected.

    Pressure excess inside curved surfaces from surface tension.

    Capillary Height #7
    \[ \textcolor{#1565C0}{h} = \frac{2\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\Upsilon} \textcolor{#7B1FA2}{\cos\theta}}{\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\gamma} \, \textcolor{#E65100}{R}} \]

    Height a liquid rises or falls in a narrow tube. Positive for wetting (θ < 90°), negative for non-wetting.

    ✓ Use when
    capillary tube problems, correcting manometer readings in small tubes.
    ✗ Not for
    non-circular tubes. Requires known θ and Υ.

    Capillary rise. Positive = wetting, negative = non-wetting.

    Ideal Gas Law #8
    \[ \textcolor{#D32F2F}{p} = \textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho} \, \textcolor{#E65100}{R} \, \textcolor{#E65100}{T} \]

    Equation of state for ideal gases — relates absolute pressure, density, and absolute temperature.

    ✓ Use when
    gas density at given conditions, compressible flow, atmosphere problems.
    ✗ Not for
    very high p or low T (real gas). Must use absolute p and T — never gauge or °C.

    Ideal gas EOS. Use absolute pressure (Pa) and temperature (K).

    Absolute / Gage Pressure #9
    \[ \textcolor{#D32F2F}{p_{\text{abs}}} = \textcolor{#D32F2F}{p_{\text{gage}}} + \textcolor{#D32F2F}{p_{\text{atm}}} \]

    Absolute = total from vacuum. Gage = above atmospheric. Vacuum = below atmospheric.

    ✓ Use when
    converting gage ↔ absolute, ideal gas law (needs absolute p).
    ✗ Not for
    p_gage can be negative (vacuum). p_abs is never negative.

    Absolute = gage + atmospheric. p_atm ≈ 101.325 kPa.

    Hydrostatic Pressure #10
    \[ \textcolor{#D32F2F}{p} = \textcolor{#D32F2F}{p_a} + \textcolor{#2E7D32}{\gamma} \, \textcolor{#1565C0}{h} \]

    Pressure increases linearly with depth in a static incompressible fluid.

    ✓ Use when
    pressure at depth, manometer analysis, pressure on submerged surfaces.
    ✗ Not for
    compressible fluids (γ varies). Not for moving fluids.

    Pressure at depth h. Linear in incompressible fluid.

    Manometer Equation #11
    \[ \textcolor{#D32F2F}{p_A} - \textcolor{#D32F2F}{p_B} = \sum \textcolor{#2E7D32}{\gamma_i} \, \textcolor{#1565C0}{\Delta h_i} \]

    Walk A→B through manometer: go down → add γΔh, go up → subtract γΔh.

    ✓ Use when
    pressure difference via U-tube or multi-fluid manometer.
    ✗ Not for
    dynamic flows. Down adds pressure, up subtracts.

    Walk through: +γΔh down, −γΔh up.

    Ch 2 — Hydrostatic Forces & Buoyancy

    Resultant Hydrostatic Force #12
    \[ \textcolor{#D32F2F}{F_R} = \textcolor{#2E7D32}{\gamma} \, \textcolor{#1565C0}{\bar{h}} \, \textcolor{#1565C0}{A} \]

    Total force on a submerged plane surface = pressure at centroid × area.

    ✓ Use when
    total force on a gate, dam, tank wall, or any flat submerged surface.
    ✗ Not for
    curved surfaces (project onto planes). \(\bar{h}\) is vertical depth, not along surface.

    Resultant force = pressure at centroid × area.

    Center of Pressure #13
    \[ \textcolor{#1565C0}{y_{cp}} = \textcolor{#1565C0}{\bar{y}} + \frac{\textcolor{#00838F}{I_{xx}}}{\textcolor{#1565C0}{\bar{y}} \, \textcolor{#1565C0}{A}} \]

    Center of pressure is always below the centroid. Offset I_xx/(ȳA) is always positive.

    ✓ Use when
    finding where F_R acts — needed for moment/equilibrium on gates, hinges.
    ✗ Not for
    \(\bar{y}\) is along the incline from free-surface line, not vertical. Use centroidal I_xx.

    Center of pressure = centroid + offset. Always below centroid.

    Ch 3 — Bernoulli’s Equation

    Bernoulli Equation (energy form) #14
    \[ \frac{\textcolor{#D32F2F}{P}}{\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}} + \frac{\textcolor{#E65100}{V}^2}{2} + \textcolor{#2E7D32}{g}\,\textcolor{#1565C0}{z} = \text{const} \]

    Mechanical energy per unit mass is conserved along a streamline for steady, incompressible, frictionless flow. Each term has units of J/kg.

    ✓ Use when
    relating pressure, speed and height between two points on the same streamline — tanks, nozzles, ideal flow.
    ✗ Not for
    viscous/frictional flow, pumps or turbines (shaft work), unsteady or compressible flow, or jumping across streamlines.

    P/ρ + V²/2 + gz is constant along a streamline (steady, inviscid, incompressible).

    Bernoulli Equation (head form) #15
    \[ \frac{\textcolor{#D32F2F}{P}}{\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}\,\textcolor{#2E7D32}{g}} + \frac{\textcolor{#E65100}{V}^2}{2\textcolor{#2E7D32}{g}} + \textcolor{#1565C0}{z} = \textcolor{#1565C0}{H} \]

    The energy form divided by g — every term now has units of metres (head). P/(ρg) is the pressure head, V²/(2g) the velocity head, z the elevation head. Note ρg = γ, the specific weight.

    ✓ Use when
    working with the EGL/HGL, pump or turbine head, or manometer heights where metres are the natural unit.
    ✗ Not for
    same limits as the energy form — no friction, no shaft work, one streamline only.

    Pressure head + velocity head + elevation head = total head H (constant along a streamline).

    Steady-Flow Euler Equation #16
    \[ d\textcolor{#D32F2F}{P} + \textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}\,\textcolor{#E65100}{V}\,d\textcolor{#E65100}{V} + \textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}\,\textcolor{#2E7D32}{g}\,d\textcolor{#1565C0}{z} = 0 \]

    The differential form of Bernoulli before integration — Newton’s second law along a streamline for an inviscid fluid. Integrating along the streamline yields the Bernoulli equation.

    ✓ Use when
    deriving Bernoulli, or reasoning about the pressure gradient along a streamline.
    ✗ Not for
    a plug-and-play formula — it is the starting differential relation, not an algebraic result.

    dP + ρV·dV + ρg·dz = 0 — integrate to recover Bernoulli.

    Stagnation Pressure #17
    \[ \textcolor{#D32F2F}{P_0} = \textcolor{#D32F2F}{P} + \tfrac{1}{2}\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}\,\textcolor{#E65100}{V}^2 \]

    Pressure at a point where the flow is brought isentropically to rest: static pressure P plus dynamic pressure ½ρV². This is the basis of Pitot-tube velocity measurement.

    ✓ Use when
    relating total, static and dynamic pressure; finding speed from a Pitot or stagnation tube.
    ✗ Not for
    high-speed compressible flow (needs a compressibility correction) — the dynamic term assumes incompressible flow.

    P₀ = P + ½ρV². Stagnation = static + dynamic pressure.

    Continuity (incompressible) #18
    \[ \textcolor{#E65100}{Q} = \textcolor{#1565C0}{A_1}\,\textcolor{#E65100}{V_1} = \textcolor{#1565C0}{A_2}\,\textcolor{#E65100}{V_2} \]

    For steady incompressible flow the volume flow rate Q is constant, so a smaller area forces a faster velocity. Paired with Bernoulli for every flow-measurement device.

    ✓ Use when
    relating velocities at two pipe sections, or computing Q from area and speed.
    ✗ Not for
    compressible flow (use mass flow ρAV) or unsteady flow with storage.

    Q = A₁V₁ = A₂V₂. Area down → velocity up.

    Pitot / Stagnation Tube Velocity #19
    \[ \textcolor{#E65100}{V_1} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2\left(\textcolor{#D32F2F}{P_0} - \textcolor{#D32F2F}{P_1}\right)}{\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}}} \]

    The stagnation relation rearranged for speed — from the difference between stagnation and static pressure. A Pitot-static tube senses both pressures at once.

    ✓ Use when
    finding flow speed from a Pitot or Pitot-static probe reading.
    ✗ Not for
    compressible flow without correction, or a misaligned (yawed) probe.

    V = √(2(P₀ − P₁)/ρ). Speed from stagnation minus static pressure.

    Venturi Meter Flow Rate #20
    \[ \textcolor{#E65100}{Q} = C_v\,\textcolor{#1565C0}{A_2}\sqrt{\dfrac{2\left(\textcolor{#D32F2F}{P_1} - \textcolor{#D32F2F}{P_2}\right)}{\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}\left(1 - (\textcolor{#1565C0}{A_2}/\textcolor{#1565C0}{A_1})^2\right)}} \]

    Bernoulli plus continuity across a smooth contraction. The velocity coefficient C_v (≈ 0.98) corrects for small friction losses; the gentle shape gives nearly full pressure recovery.

    ✓ Use when
    metering pipe flow with a Venturi; take C_v from calibration or charts.
    ✗ Not for
    very low Reynolds number (C_v drops), or using the pipe area — A₂ is the throat.

    Q = C_v A₂ √(2(P₁−P₂) / [ρ(1−(A₂/A₁)²)]). C_v ≈ 0.98.

    Orifice Meter Flow Rate #21
    \[ \textcolor{#E65100}{Q} = C_D\,\textcolor{#1565C0}{A_0}\sqrt{\dfrac{2\left(\textcolor{#D32F2F}{P_1} - \textcolor{#D32F2F}{P_2}\right)}{\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}\left(1 - C_c^2(\textcolor{#1565C0}{A_0}/\textcolor{#1565C0}{A_1})^2\right)}} \]

    Same principle as the Venturi but with a sharp-edged plate. The jet necks down to a vena contracta (C_c ≈ 0.6), and the discharge coefficient C_D = C_c·C_v bundles contraction and friction. Cheaper than a Venturi but with a larger permanent pressure loss; a bell-mouth inlet has C_c = 1.

    ✓ Use when
    metering pipe flow with an orifice plate; take C_D from standards or calibration.
    ✗ Not for
    cases needing low pressure loss (use a Venturi), or forgetting contraction — the area is the hole A₀, the jet is smaller.

    Q = C_D A₀ √(2(P₁−P₂) / [ρ(1−C_c²(A₀/A₁)²)]). C_c ≈ 0.6, C_D = C_c·C_v.

    Ch 4 — Introduction to Fluid Dynamics

    Velocity Field #22
    \[ \textcolor{#E65100}{\vec{V}} = \textcolor{#E65100}{u}\,\vec{i} + \textcolor{#E65100}{v}\,\vec{j} + \textcolor{#E65100}{w}\,\vec{k} \]

    The Eulerian velocity field — velocity as a function of position and time, carried by three scalar components.

    ✓ Use when
    describing flow at fixed points in space, or locating a stagnation point (set V⃗ = 0 and solve).
    ✗ Not for
    tracking a single particle over time — that is the Lagrangian description.

    Velocity as a field: components u, v, w at each point.

    Volume Flow Rate #23
    \[ \textcolor{#E65100}{Q} = \int_A \textcolor{#E65100}{v_n}\,dA = \textcolor{#E65100}{\bar{U}}\,\textcolor{#1565C0}{A} \]

    Volume per unit time through a section. Only the velocity normal to the area carries flow — integrate it, or use average velocity × area.

    ✓ Use when
    finding discharge through a pipe or duct from a velocity profile or a known Ū.
    ✗ Not for
    mass flow of a compressible fluid — multiply by ρ (see #25). Tangential velocity adds nothing.

    Q = ∫vₙ dA = Ū·A. Normal velocity only.

    Average Velocity #24
    \[ \textcolor{#E65100}{\bar{U}} = \dfrac{\textcolor{#E65100}{Q}}{\textcolor{#1565C0}{A}} \]

    The single uniform velocity that carries the same discharge Q through the area — collapses a velocity profile to one number.

    ✓ Use when
    reducing a profile to a representative speed; Reynolds number, head loss, continuity.
    ✗ Not for
    the peak/centerline velocity — for laminar pipe flow Ū = V_max/2, not V_max.

    Ū = Q/A. Profile collapsed to one speed.

    Mass Flow Rate #25
    \[ \textcolor{#E65100}{\dot{m}} = \textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}\,\textcolor{#E65100}{Q} = \textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}\,\textcolor{#E65100}{\bar{U}}\,\textcolor{#1565C0}{A} \]

    Mass per unit time = density × volume flow. The form that conserves across a control volume even when area or velocity changes.

    ✓ Use when
    conservation of mass / continuity, compressible flow, mixing and energy balances.
    ✗ Not for
    incompressible volume continuity — there Q is conserved; ṁ simply adds the ρ factor.

    ṁ = ρQ = ρŪA. Mass per unit time.

    Ch 5 — Reynolds Transport Theorem

    Linear Momentum Equation (General) #26
    \[ \sum \textcolor{#E65100}{\vec{F}_{\text{ext}}} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\int_{\text{CV}} \textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{V}}\,\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}\;d\forall \;+\; \int_{\text{CS}} \textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{V}}\,\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}\,(\textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{V}}\!\cdot\!\textcolor{#9C27B0}{\hat{n}})\;dA \]

    The RTT applied to linear momentum. Left side = external forces. First integral = rate of momentum change stored inside the CV (zero for steady flow). Second integral = net momentum flux through the control surface.

    ✓ Use when
    finding forces on ducts, nozzles, vanes, gates, or any surface interacting with fluid; works for steady or unsteady, uniform or non-uniform flow.
    ✗ Not for
    non-inertial (accelerating) CVs — use the modified form with apparent forces. Also not for angular momentum — that needs the moment-of-momentum equation.

    ΣF⃗ = ∂/∂t ∫ V⃗ρ dV + ∫ V⃗ρ(V⃗·n̂) dA. General form; drop storage term for steady flow.

    Steady Ideal Flow Momentum #27
    \[ \sum \textcolor{#E65100}{\vec{F}_{\text{ext}}} = \sum_{\text{ports}} \textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{V}}\,\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}\,(\textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{V}}\!\cdot\!\textcolor{#9C27B0}{\hat{n}})\,\textcolor{#D32F2F}{A} \]

    Steady + incompressible + inviscid (ideal) flow with uniform velocity at each port. The integral collapses to a sum over discrete inlets and outlets. V⃗·n̂ is negative at inlets, positive at outlets.

    ✓ Use when
    flow is steady, ideal, and uniform at each port — most MAAE 2300 problems; multiple inlets/outlets handled naturally.
    ✗ Not for
    unsteady filling/emptying problems or non-uniform velocity profiles — go back to the integral form #26.

    ΣF⃗ = Σ V⃗ρ(V⃗·n̂)A. Discrete-port form; V⃗·n̂ < 0 at inlets.

    Simplified Momentum (1-in / 1-out) #28
    \[ \sum \textcolor{#E65100}{\vec{F}_{\text{ext}}} = \textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{v}_{\text{out}}}\,\textcolor{#D32F2F}{\dot{m}_{\text{out}}} - \textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{v}_{\text{in}}}\,\textcolor{#D32F2F}{\dot{m}_{\text{in}}} \]

    The simplest form: steady, uniform, non-moving CV with exactly one inlet and one outlet. Each velocity carries its own directional sign from geometry. Prone to sign errors for angled flows.

    ✓ Use when
    simple one-inlet/one-outlet problems with a stationary CV (e.g. pipe bend, nozzle reaction).
    ✗ Not for
    multiple outlets/inlets (use #27), moving CVs (use #29), or problems where sign confusion is likely — the full form is safer.

    ΣF⃗ = v⃗_out·ṁ_out − v⃗_in·ṁ_in. One inlet, one outlet, stationary CV.

    Moving CV Momentum #29
    \[ \sum \textcolor{#E65100}{\vec{F}_{\text{ext}}} = \sum \textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{V}_{f/b}}\;\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}\,(\textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{V}_{f/b}}\!\cdot\!\hat{n})\,\textcolor{#D32F2F}{A} \quad\text{where}\quad \textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{V}_{f/b}} = \textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{V}_{\text{flow}}} - \textcolor{#9C27B0}{\vec{V}_b} \]

    When the CV moves at constant velocity V⃗_b, replace every velocity with the relative velocity V⃗_f/b. The forces remain in the absolute frame. The relative velocity determines the mass flow rate crossing the CS.

    ✓ Use when
    the CV is attached to a moving object (truck, boat, turbine blade) at constant speed.
    ✗ Not for
    accelerating CVs — those need the non-inertial modification with an extra −a_CV ∫ρ dV term.

    ΣF⃗ = Σ V⃗_f/b ρ(V⃗_f/b·n̂)A. V⃗_f/b = V⃗_flow − V⃗_b. Relative velocity everywhere.

    Angular Momentum Equation (General) #30
    \[ \sum \textcolor{#E65100}{\vec{M}_{\text{ext}}} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\int_{\text{CV}} (\textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{r}} \times \textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{V}})\,\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}\;d\forall \;+\; \int_{\text{CS}} (\textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{r}} \times \textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{V}})\,\textcolor{#2E7D32}{\rho}\,(\textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{V}}\!\cdot\!\textcolor{#9C27B0}{\hat{n}})\;dA \]

    The RTT applied to angular momentum (moment-of-momentum). Left side = external moments about point O. First integral = rate of angular momentum change stored inside the CV (zero for steady flow). Second integral = net angular momentum flux through the CS. Identical structure to linear momentum (#26) with V⃗ replaced by r⃗ × V⃗.

    ✓ Use when
    finding torques or moments at pipe elbows, turbomachinery, rotating sprinklers, or any support where a moment reaction exists.
    ✗ Not for
    finding forces alone — use linear momentum (#26–#29) for that. Angular momentum gives moments/torques, not forces.

    ΣM⃗ = ∂/∂t ∫(r⃗×V⃗)ρ dV + ∫(r⃗×V⃗)ρ(V⃗·n̂) dA. Moment form of RTT; drop storage for steady flow.

    Angular Momentum — Steady Ideal Flow #31
    \[ \sum \textcolor{#E65100}{\vec{M}_{\text{ext}}} = \sum_{\text{ports}} (\textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{r}} \times \textcolor{#1565C0}{\vec{V}})\,\textcolor{#D32F2F}{\dot{m}} \]

    Steady ideal flow with discrete ports. The moment about O equals the sum of (r⃗ × V⃗)ṁ over all inlets and outlets. Only ports whose velocity has a nonzero moment arm about O contribute. Choose O at the support point so unknown reaction forces have zero moment arm.

    ✓ Use when
    finding moment at a pipe elbow, turbine shaft torque, or tipping analysis — steady, ideal, discrete ports.
    ✗ Not for
    unsteady problems (use #30 with storage term) or distributed inflows where discrete-port assumption fails.

    ΣM⃗ = Σ(r⃗ × V⃗)ṁ. Discrete-port moment form; choose O at the support to eliminate unknown forces.

5.1 — Reynolds Transport Theorem: Conservation of Mass

System → control volume • Extensive vs intensive properties • The RTT • Continuity equation • Worked examples: reservoir & jet engine

The Big Picture: Why We Need the RTT

Every fundamental law of mechanics — conservation of mass, Newton’s second law, conservation of energy — is written for a fixed lump of matter. In fluid mechanics we call that fixed lump a system: a fixed collection of atoms that we follow as it moves and deforms. This is the Lagrangian viewpoint, and it makes the laws easy to state:

The System Laws (Lagrangian form)
\[ \underbrace{\frac{dm}{dt} = 0}_{\text{mass}} \qquad \underbrace{\vec F = \frac{d}{dt}(m\vec V)}_{\text{linear momentum}} \qquad \underbrace{\vec M = \frac{d}{dt}\!\int (\vec r \times \vec V)\,dm}_{\text{angular momentum}} \qquad \underbrace{\dot Q - \dot W = \frac{dE}{dt}}_{\text{energy}} \]

Each law tracks a property of one fixed mass as it travels through space. Exact, no averaging — but to use them we’d have to follow every fluid particle individually. For a flowing fluid that is hopeless.

What we actually care about in engineering is what happens at a fixed location in space — the thrust at a nozzle, the flow through a pump, the rise of water behind a dam. That is the Eulerian viewpoint: we draw a fixed region called a control volume (CV) and watch fluid stream in and out of it. The problem is that the laws above are written for systems, not for control volumes.

★ The Core Idea

The Reynolds Transport Theorem (RTT) is the bridge between the two viewpoints. It rewrites the rate of change of any system property as something we can evaluate entirely on a fixed control volume. Once we have it, we get continuity, the momentum equation, and the energy equation just by choosing which property to track.

System vs. Control Volume

Two Ways to Define Your “Stuff”

• A system (closed system) is a collection of matter with a fixed set of atoms. Mass is constant; the boundary moves and deforms with the fluid. Equations apply to individual masses and quantities are exact.

• A control volume (open system) is a selected region in space that fluid may flow into and out of. It can be fixed, move, or deform. Equations apply to a region and quantities are averaged over it. The CV is enclosed by a control surface (CS) — part real wall (pipe, nozzle), part imaginary (an inlet/outlet plane).

The classic mental image is a deodorant can. Treat the can plus the spray it ejects as a system (fixed mass, but it spreads everywhere — awkward to track). Or draw the CV as just the can itself: mass leaves through the nozzle, and we only track what crosses that surface. The CV is far more practical.

Control volumes come in three flavours, chosen to suit the problem:

Fixed CV

Locked in space. Use for nozzle/duct stress, pipe junctions. (e.g. nozzle reaction force)

Moving CV

Travels at constant velocity \(\vec V_s\). Use for drag on a moving vehicle. (e.g. ship/aircraft drag)

Deforming CV

Boundary changes shape/size. Use for transient filling/emptying. (e.g. inflating balloon)

Recall: Extensive (B) vs. Intensive (β) Properties

To write one master equation that works for mass, momentum, and energy, we need generic symbols. Recall from §1.2 the distinction between extensive and intensive properties:

Generic Property and Its Per-Unit-Mass Value
\[ \beta = \frac{dB}{dm} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad B = \int_{\text{sys}} \beta\,\rho\,d\forall \]
\(B\) — any extensive property (scales with mass): total mass, momentum, or energy \(\beta = dB/dm\) — the matching intensive property (the amount of \(B\) per unit mass) \(\rho\) — fluid density [kg/m³] \(d\forall\) — differential volume element [m³]

The whole power of the RTT is that we derive it once for the generic pair \((B,\beta)\), then simply plug in:

Conservation law Extensive \(B\) Intensive \(\beta = dB/dm\)
Mass (this lecture)\(m\)\(\beta = m/m = 1\)
Linear momentum\(m\vec V\)\(\beta = \vec V\)
Energy\(E\)\(\beta = e = E/m\)

Recall: Flow Rate & Flux Through a Surface

Before deriving the theorem we need to count how much fluid crosses a surface per second. Consider a small patch of area \(dA\) with an outward unit normal \(\hat n\), through which fluid moves at velocity \(\vec V\). In a time \(dt\) the fluid sweeps out a slanted slab. Only the velocity component along the normal moves fluid across the patch:

Figure 5.1.1 — Fluid crossing an area patch \(dA\). In time \(dt\) it sweeps the slanted slab of volume \(d\forall = (\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA\,dt = V\,dA\cos\theta\,dt\). Only the normal component \(V\cos\theta\) carries fluid across.
Volume & Mass Flow Rate Through a Surface
\[ Q = \int_{S} (\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA = \int_{S} V_n\,dA \qquad\qquad \dot m = \int_{S} \rho\,(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA \]
\(Q\) — volume flow rate through the surface [m³/s] \(\dot m\) — mass flow rate through the surface [kg/s] \(V_n = \vec V\cdot\hat n = V\cos\theta\) — velocity component normal to the surface [m/s] \(\hat n\) — outward unit normal of the control surface
★ Sign Convention — Memorize This

Because \(\hat n\) always points out of the control surface:

• \(\vec V\cdot\hat n > 0\) → fluid leaves → outflow (mass/volume rates are positive)
• \(\vec V\cdot\hat n < 0\) → fluid enters → inflow (rates are negative)
• \(\vec V\cdot\hat n = 0\) → flow is parallel to the surface (a solid wall, where \(\vec V = 0\) anyway).

For a flow with a non-uniform velocity profile (like the parabolic profile in a pipe), it is convenient to replace the profile with a single average velocity \(\bar V\) that carries the same flow rate:

Average Velocity & 1-D Flow Rates
\[ \bar V = \frac{1}{A}\int_A u(r)\,dA \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad Q = \bar V A \qquad \dot m = \rho\,\bar V A = \rho Q \]
\(\bar V\) — cross-sectional average velocity [m/s] \(u(r)\) — local (pointwise) velocity across the section [m/s] \(A\) — cross-sectional area [m²] \(\rho\) — density [kg/m³]

Net Flow Through a Control Surface

Now wrap a control surface around a piece of pipe. Following the convention, each face gets an area vector \(\vec A_i\) (magnitude = area, direction = outward normal). The net rate at which any quantity leaves the CV is simply flow out minus flow in, summed over every face.

Figure 5.1.2 — 1-D flow through a control surface. Area vectors \(\vec A_i\) point out. No flow crosses the walls (\(\vec A_3,\vec A_4\)). Inlet: \(\vec V\cdot\hat n < 0\); outlet: \(\vec V\cdot\hat n > 0\).
Building the Net-Outflow Integral

Net rate of volume outflow = flow out − flow in. With \(\theta_i\) the angle between \(\vec V_i\) and \(\vec A_i\):

\[ Q = v_2 A_2\cos\theta_2 - v_1 A_1\cos\theta_1 = \vec V_2\cdot\vec A_2 - \vec V_1\cdot\vec A_1 \]

Generalizing to every face of the control surface, the discrete sum becomes a surface integral:

\[ Q = \sum_{CS}\vec V_i\cdot\vec A_i = \int_{CS}\vec V\cdot d\vec A \]

Weighting by density gives the net mass outflow, and weighting by \(\beta\) gives the net outflow of any property \(B\):

\[ \dot m = \int_{CS}\rho\,\vec V\cdot d\vec A \qquad\qquad \dot B_{\text{out,net}} = \int_{CS}\beta\,\rho\,\vec V\cdot d\vec A \]

Derivation of the Reynolds Transport Theorem

Here is the key construction. Take a fixed control volume and let a moving system coincide with it at time \(t\). An instant \(dt\) later, the system has drifted downstream: it has vacated a sliver at the back of the CV (where new fluid enters, \(\Delta M_{IN}\)) and pushed a sliver out the front (where fluid leaves, \(\Delta M_{OUT}\)).

Figure 5.1.3 — A fixed control volume (teal dashed) and a moving system (orange). At \(t=0\) they coincide. At \(t=\Delta t\) the system has shifted: it leaves \(\Delta M_{IN}\) behind (new fluid entering the CV) and pushes \(\Delta M_{OUT}\) out the front.
Step-by-Step Derivation

Step 1 — rate of change of a system property is, by definition, the limit of its change over \(\Delta t\):

\[ \frac{dB_{\text{sys}}}{dt} = \lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{B_{t+\Delta t} - B_{t}}{\Delta t} \]

Step 2 — relate system content to CV content. At \(t\) the system fills the CV exactly:

\[ B_{t} = B_{CV,\,t} \]

At \(t+\Delta t\) the system equals the CV content, minus the inflow sliver it has not yet reoccupied, plus the outflow sliver it now occupies outside the CV:

\[ B_{t+\Delta t} = B_{CV,\,t+\Delta t} - \Delta B_{IN} + \Delta B_{OUT} \]

Step 3 — substitute and split the limit:

\[ \frac{dB_{\text{sys}}}{dt} = \lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{B_{CV,\,t+\Delta t} - B_{CV,\,t}}{\Delta t} \;+\; \lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\Delta B_{OUT} - \Delta B_{IN}}{\Delta t} \]

Step 4 — identify each limit physically. The first is the rate of change of \(B\) stored inside the CV; the second is the net rate \(B\) flows out across the control surface (the integral we built in Figure 5.1.2):

\[ \frac{dB_{\text{sys}}}{dt} = \underbrace{\frac{d}{dt}\!\int_{CV}\beta\,\rho\,d\forall}_{\text{rate stored in CV}} \;+\; \underbrace{\int_{CS}\beta\,\rho\,\vec V\cdot d\vec A}_{\text{net rate out through CS}} \]
Reynolds Transport Theorem (fixed CV)
\[ \boxed{\;\frac{dB_{\text{sys}}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\int_{CV}\beta\,\rho\,d\forall \;+\; \int_{CS}\beta\,\rho\,(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA\;} \]
\(dB_{\text{sys}}/dt\) — rate of change of the property for the moving system (what the physical law gives us) \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\!\int_{CV}\beta\rho\,d\forall\) — rate the property accumulates inside the fixed CV \(\int_{CS}\beta\rho(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA\) — net flux of the property out through the control surface \(\beta = dB/dm\) — intensive property; \(\rho\) — density; \(\hat n\) — outward normal
★ Read It In Words

System change = what builds up inside the CV + what escapes through its surface. The left side is Lagrangian (a law we know); the entire right side is Eulerian (things we can measure at a fixed location). The changes of a moving system are now expressed solely in terms of a stationary control volume.

Steady-Flow Simplification

For steady flow with a CV of fixed geometry, nothing inside the CV changes with time, so the storage term vanishes \(\left(\dfrac{d}{dt}\!\int_{CV}\beta\rho\,d\forall = 0\right)\) and the RTT collapses to a pure surface integral:

RTT — Steady Flow
\[ \frac{dB_{\text{sys}}}{dt} = \int_{CS}\beta\,\rho\,(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA \qquad\text{(steady, fixed geometry)} \]

Conservation of Mass: The Continuity Equation

Now choose the property. Mass conservation says the mass of a system is constant, so \(B = m\) and the left side is zero. The intensive value is \(\beta = m/m = 1\). Substituting into the RTT gives the master continuity equation:

From RTT to Continuity

Set \(B = m\Rightarrow\beta = 1\), and use \(\dfrac{dm_{\text{sys}}}{dt} = 0\):

\[ 0 = \frac{dm_{\text{sys}}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\int_{CV}\rho\,d\forall + \int_{CS}\rho\,(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA \]

Rearranging shows the physical balance directly — the net mass flowing out equals the rate the CV is losing mass:

\[ \underbrace{\int_{CS}\rho\,(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA}_{\text{net mass outflow}} = \underbrace{-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{CV}\rho\,d\forall}_{\text{rate of mass decrease in CV}} \]
Continuity Equation (general, deforming CV)
\[ \boxed{\;\frac{d}{dt}\int_{CV}\rho\,d\forall + \int_{CS}\rho\,(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA = 0\;} \]
\(\dfrac{d}{dt}\!\int_{CV}\rho\,d\forall\) — rate of change of mass stored in the CV (local mass change) \(\int_{CS}\rho(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA\) — net convective mass flow out of the CS \(\rho\) — density [kg/m³]; \(d\forall\) — volume element [m³]; \(dA\) — area element [m²]

The general equation specializes through a ladder of assumptions. Each one knocks out a term or a factor:

Figure 5.1.4 — A branching duct: one inlet \(A\), two outlets \(B,C\). For steady flow, \(\sum\dot m_{\text{out}} = \sum\dot m_{\text{in}}\): whatever enters at \(A\) must leave through \(B\) and \(C\) combined.
Continuity — The Practical Forms

Steady flow (storage term = 0): mass in = mass out.

\[ \int_{CS}\rho\,(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA = \sum\dot m_{\text{out}} - \sum\dot m_{\text{in}} = 0 \]

Steady & uniform (1-D) — one average velocity per port:

\[ \sum (\rho A V)_{\text{out}} - \sum (\rho A V)_{\text{in}} = 0 \]

Incompressible, steady & uniform — \(\rho\) cancels, so volume flow is conserved:

\[ \sum (A V)_{\text{out}} = \sum (A V)_{\text{in}} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \sum Q_{\text{out}} = \sum Q_{\text{in}} \]
\(\dot m = \rho A V\) — mass flow per port [kg/s] \(Q = A V\) — volume flow per port [m³/s] \(A\) — port area; \(V\) — average normal velocity at the port

Control Volume Moving at Constant Velocity

If the control volume itself moves at a steady velocity \(\vec V_s\) (a CV riding along with a vehicle, say), the only change is that fluid crosses the control surface at its velocity relative to the CV, not its absolute velocity:

RTT — CV Moving at Constant Velocity
\[ \frac{d}{dt}B_{\text{sys}} = \frac{d}{dt}\int_{CV}\beta\,\rho\,d\forall + \int_{CS}\beta\,\rho\,(\vec V_r\cdot\hat n)\,dA, \qquad \vec V_r = \vec V - \vec V_s \]
\(\vec V_r\) — velocity of fluid relative to the control surface [m/s] \(\vec V\) — absolute fluid velocity (ground frame) [m/s] \(\vec V_s\) — absolute velocity of the control volume [m/s]
★ Recall: Material Derivative

The split into a storage term and a flux term mirrors the material derivative from kinematics: \(\dfrac{D}{Dt} = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial t} + \vec V\cdot\nabla\) (local rate of change + advective transport). In the RTT the local part lives in the CV integral and the advective part lives in the CS integral.

Worked Examples

Example 5.1 — Rise Rate of a Reservoir (unsteady mass balance)

Problem: A river discharges into a reservoir at \(Q_1 = 11{,}320\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}\). The outflow through the flow passages in the dam is \(Q_2 = 7{,}079.2\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}\). The free-surface area of the reservoir is \(A_3 = 110\ \text{km}^2\). How fast is the water level rising?

Figure 5.1.5 — Reservoir control volume. Inflow \(Q_1\) (river) and outflow \(Q_2\) (dam) are unequal, so the free surface rises at \(v_3\) over area \(A_3\).
Show Solution
Thought process

Inflow doesn’t equal outflow, so mass is accumulating — this is an unsteady continuity problem. The accumulation shows up as a rising free surface.

Water is incompressible, so I track volume. The slides treat the rising surface as a third “outflow” \(Q_3 = v_3 A_3\) through the top of a fixed CV; the balance \(Q_3 + Q_2 - Q_1 = 0\) then gives \(v_3\). (Equivalently, \(A_3\,dh/dt = Q_1 - Q_2\) — same result.)

Formulas needed: incompressible continuity \(\sum Q_{\text{out}} = \sum Q_{\text{in}}\), surface flux \(Q_3 = v_3 A_3\).

Given: \(Q_1 = 11{,}320\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}\), \(\;Q_2 = 7{,}079.2\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}\), \(\;A_3 = 110\ \text{km}^2\). \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) are unknown but not needed.

Step 1 — write the incompressible continuity balance on the reservoir CV. With the rising surface as outflow \(Q_3\):

\[ v_3 A_3 + \underbrace{v_2 A_2}_{Q_2} - \underbrace{v_1 A_1}_{Q_1} = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; v_3 A_3 = Q_1 - Q_2 \]

Step 2 — solve for the surface velocity:

\[ v_3 = \frac{Q_1 - Q_2}{A_3} = \frac{11{,}320 - 7{,}079.2}{A_3} = \frac{4{,}240.8\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}}{A_3} \]

Step 3 — convert the area to SI before dividing (this is where unit slips happen):

\[ A_3 = 110\ \text{km}^2 = 110\times 10^{6}\ \text{m}^2 = 1.10\times 10^{8}\ \text{m}^2 \] \[ v_3 = \frac{4{,}240.8}{1.10\times 10^{8}} = 3.86\times 10^{-5}\ \text{m/s} \]

Step 4 — express in convenient units (m/s is tiny — report cm/hr):

\[ v_3 = 3.86\times 10^{-5}\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\times 100\,\tfrac{\text{cm}}{\text{m}}\times 3600\,\tfrac{\text{s}}{\text{hr}} = \boxed{13.9\ \text{cm/hr}} \]

The reservoir surface rises about 14 cm every hour — slow, but over a day that is more than 3 m.

Example 5.2 — Jet-Engine Exit Velocity (steady, compressible)

Problem: During an engine test, air enters at \(\dot m = 40\ \text{kg/s}\) and is discharged at an absolute pressure of \(750\ \text{kPa}\) and temperature \(120^{\circ}\text{C}\). The exit diameter is \(0.3\ \text{m}\). Determine the exit velocity. Treat the exhaust as an ideal gas with \(R = 286.9\ \text{J/(kg}\!\cdot\!\text{K)}\).

Figure 5.1.6 — Steady flow through the engine: one inlet, one exit. Mass in = mass out, so \(\dot m = \rho_{\text{exit}}A_{\text{exit}}v_{\text{exit}}\).
Show Solution
Thought process

One inlet, one outlet, nothing accumulating → steady continuity: \(\dot m_{\text{in}} = \dot m_{\text{out}}\). The gas is compressible, so I cannot cancel \(\rho\) — I need the exit density from the ideal gas law first, then back out velocity from \(\dot m = \rho A V\).

Formulas needed: steady continuity \(\dot m_{\text{in}} = \dot m_{\text{out}}\), mass flow \(\dot m = \rho A V\), ideal gas \(\rho = p/(RT)\).

Step 1 — exit density from the ideal gas law. Convert temperature to absolute first:

\[ T = 120 + 273 = 393\ \text{K} \] \[ \rho_{\text{exit}} = \frac{p}{RT} = \frac{750{,}000\ \text{Pa}}{(286.9)(393)} = 6.65\ \text{kg/m}^3 \]

Step 2 — exit area from the diameter:

\[ A_{\text{exit}} = \frac{\pi D^2}{4} = \frac{\pi (0.3)^2}{4} = 0.0707\ \text{m}^2 \]

Step 3 — apply steady continuity \(\dot m_{\text{in}} = \dot m_{\text{exit}} = \rho_{\text{exit}}A_{\text{exit}}v_{\text{exit}}\) and solve for velocity:

\[ v_{\text{exit}} = \frac{\dot m}{\rho_{\text{exit}}A_{\text{exit}}} = \frac{40}{(6.65)(0.0707)} = \boxed{85.1\ \text{m/s}} \]
Watch the absolutes
The ideal gas law demands absolute pressure and Kelvin. Plugging in gauge pressure or °C is the #1 error here. Also use the specific gas constant for air (286.9), not the universal \(R_u\).
★ Problem-Solving Strategy — Continuity

Step 1 — draw the CV and mark every port with its outward normal \(\hat n\). Label inflows (\(\vec V\cdot\hat n < 0\)) and outflows (\(\vec V\cdot\hat n > 0\)).
Step 2 — steady or unsteady? If the CV mass is constant in time, drop the storage term. If something is filling/emptying (a rising tank), keep it.
Step 3 — compressible or incompressible? Liquids → cancel \(\rho\) and conserve volume \(Q\). Gases → keep \(\rho\), and get each \(\rho\) from the ideal gas law at that port’s \(p,T\).
Step 4 — uniform or profile? One number per port → use \(\dot m = \rho A V\). A profile → integrate \(\int\rho u\,dA\) or use \(\bar V\).
Step 5 — convert units (km² → m², kPa → Pa, °C → K) before substituting.

Lecture Recap

• The RTT converts a Lagrangian system law into an Eulerian control-volume statement: system change = storage in CV + net flux out of CS.
• Choose \((B,\beta)\) to get the law you want; \(B=m,\ \beta=1\) gives continuity.
• The outward-normal sign convention (\(\vec V\cdot\hat n\): + out, − in) drives every flux term.
• Steady flow kills the storage term; incompressibility cancels \(\rho\). Next lecture sets \(\beta = \vec V\) to get the linear-momentum equation.

4.1 — Introduction to Fluid Dynamics

Everything so far has dealt with fluids at rest — pressure distributions, hydrostatic forces, buoyancy. Now the fluid starts to move, and the central question changes from “what force does the fluid exert?” to “how do we describe the motion?” This lecture builds the vocabulary and the bookkeeping tools for the rest of the course: how to describe a flow field, how to picture it, how to classify it, and how to measure how much fluid is passing through a section.

Key Definitions

Fluid Statics

The study of fluids at rest. There is no relative motion between fluid particles, so no shear stresses act — pressure is the only stress. (This was Chapters 1–3.)

Kinematics

The study of the geometry of motion, without reference to the forces that cause it. The fundamental kinematic quantities describe four things a fluid element can do: translation, rotation, linear deformation (stretching), and angular deformation (shearing).

Fluid Dynamics

The study of fluids in motion, including the forces and energy involved. This is where the course is headed — continuity, momentum, and energy equations.

Fluid Kinematics

The study of the geometry of fluid motion — how fluids flow and how to describe that flow mathematically, before bringing forces into the picture.

Two Ways to Describe Fluid Motion

A flow contains an enormous number of fluid particles. There are two fundamentally different bookkeeping strategies for describing what they are doing.

Lagrangian Description

Pick a fluid particle and follow it. Keep track of the position and velocity of each individual particle as it moves — and do this simultaneously for every particle in the flow field. This is intuitive (it is just Newtonian particle mechanics applied to fluid blobs), but tracking billions of particles at once is hopeless for most engineering problems.

Eulerian Description

Forget individual particles. Instead, fix your attention on points in space (a region called the flow domain or control volume) and record what the fluid is doing as it passes through each point. Rather than following particles, we define field variables — functions of space and time — such as the pressure field \(p(x,y,z,t)\) and the velocity field \(\vec{V}(x,y,z,t)\), defined at every location and instant.

Two descriptions of the same flow. Lagrangian follows individual particles; Eulerian fixes points in space and records the field variables of whatever fluid is there.
The Velocity Field (Eulerian)

In Cartesian coordinates the velocity field has three scalar components, each a function of position and time:

\[ \vec{V} = (u,\,v,\,w) = u(x,y,z,t)\,\vec{i} + v(x,y,z,t)\,\vec{j} + w(x,y,z,t)\,\vec{k} \]

\(\vec{V}\) — velocity vector at a point (m/s) \(u,\,v,\,w\) — x, y, z velocity components (m/s) \(\vec{i},\,\vec{j},\,\vec{k}\) — unit vectors along x, y, z \(x,y,z\) — position; \(t\) — time

When to use: Any time you need the velocity at a location (not on a specific particle) — finding stagnation points, plugging into continuity/momentum/energy, evaluating flow rate across a section.

When NOT to use: If you genuinely need the history of one particle (e.g. a tracer's path), that is a Lagrangian question — integrate \(d\vec{x}/dt = \vec{V}\) along the particle instead.

★ Key Convention for This Course

We use the Eulerian description throughout MAAE 2300. It does not distinguish individual particles — we look only at the total effect within a control volume. This is what makes the control-volume governing equations (continuity, momentum, energy) possible.

Worked Example — Finding a Stagnation Point

Problem: A steady, incompressible, two-dimensional velocity field is given by

\[ \vec{V} = (u,v) = (0.5 + 0.8x)\,\vec{i} + (1.5 - 0.8y)\,\vec{j} \]

where x and y are in metres and velocity is in m/s. A stagnation point is a location where the velocity is zero. Determine whether any stagnation points exist and, if so, where.

Solution

Thought process: A vector is zero only if every component is zero. So set each scalar component to zero and solve the two equations independently — one gives x, the other gives y.

Assumptions: Flow is steady (no time dependence) and two-dimensional (no z-component, no variation with z), exactly as stated.

Step 1 — Set the x-component to zero:

\[ u = 0.5 + 0.8x = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x = -\frac{0.5}{0.8} = -0.625\ \text{m} \]

Step 2 — Set the y-component to zero:

\[ v = 1.5 - 0.8y = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; y = \frac{1.5}{0.8} = 1.875\ \text{m} \]

Step 3 — Combine. Both conditions are satisfied at exactly one point:

\[ \boxed{\text{One stagnation point at } (x,y) = (-0.625\ \text{m},\ 1.875\ \text{m})} \]

★ Tip: Because each component depends on only one coordinate here, the solution is unique. If a component were a function of both x and y, you would solve the two equations simultaneously and could get several stagnation points (or none).

Flow Visualization: Lines of Flow

Fluid mechanics is a highly visual subject. We use sketches and photographs to extract qualitative and quantitative information about a flow. There are four standard families of lines used to picture a flow: streamlines, pathlines, streaklines, and timelines. They are defined differently, and — importantly — they only coincide under special conditions.

Streamline

A line that is everywhere tangent to the velocity at a given instant. Key properties:

• The fluid velocity is tangent to the streamline at every point on it.
• Streamlines do not cross — if they did, the velocity at the crossing point would have two directions, which is impossible. There is a unique velocity at each point in space.
• Streamlines can change with time in an unsteady flow (they are an instantaneous snapshot).

Streamlines (teal) with the velocity vector (orange) tangent at each point. The arrows show flow direction; streamlines never intersect.
Streamtube

A bundle of streamlines that surrounds a region of flow. Because the bounding lines are streamlines, the velocity is tangent to the walls — so no fluid crosses the walls of a streamtube; flow is contained inside it like fluid in a conduit. In 2-D, a streamtube is formed by just two streamlines.

A streamtube: the two outer streamlines (navy) act like solid walls because no flow crosses them. Interior streamlines carry the flow through.
Pathline

The actual path traced by a single particle as it travels over a period of time \(0 \le t \le t_n\). It can be obtained experimentally: release one particle into the flow and take a long-exposure photograph. A pathline is a time history of one particle.

A pathline: one particle photographed at successive instants. The dashed curve is the route a single particle actually followed.
Streakline

The line connecting all particles that have passed through one fixed point, photographed at a single instant \(t = t_n\). Every particle on a streakline shares the same origin. Physically, a streakline is what you see when you continuously inject dye or smoke from a point in the flow — the visible streak of dye is the streakline.

A streakline: the locus, at one instant, of every particle that has passed through the injection point. This is the dye/smoke streak you see in a wind tunnel.
Timeline

A set of fluid particles that form a line at a given instant. Mark a row of adjacent particles at one moment, then watch how that row distorts as the flow carries it along — the deformation of a timeline reveals velocity gradients (shear) in the flow.

A timeline: a straight row of particles marked at \(t_0\) distorts into a curve at \(t_1\). The bending shows the velocity varies across the row (shear).
★ Key Result: Steady Flow

In general these four line families are different. But in steady flow, streamlines, pathlines, and streaklines all coincide. If the velocity field never changes in time, then the instantaneous tangent direction (streamline), the path one particle follows (pathline), and the dye streak (streakline) are the same curve. This is why dye and smoke can be used to reveal “the streamlines” of a steady flow — tools like Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) and CFD do this quantitatively.

In steady flow the three curves lie on top of one another (drawn slightly offset here only so all three are visible). They are physically the same line.

Classifying Flows: Steady, Unsteady, Uniform

Two independent yes/no questions classify a flow. Does it change with time? (steady vs unsteady) and does it change with location? (uniform vs non-uniform). These are separate ideas — do not conflate them.

Steady Flow

The velocity at a given point in space does not vary with time: \(\partial \vec{V}/\partial t = 0\) at every fixed point. The flow pattern is frozen even though individual particles keep moving through it.

Unsteady Flow

The velocity at a point changes with time. Unsteady flows may be nonperiodic (e.g. a valve being opened), periodic (e.g. pulsating pump flow), or truly random (turbulence).

Uniform Flow

The velocity at a given instant does not vary with location over the region of interest. (This is about spatial variation, not time — a flow can be steady but non-uniform, like flow speeding up through a nozzle.)

★ Don't Confuse These

Steady/unsteady is about time (\(\partial/\partial t\)); uniform/non-uniform is about space (\(\partial/\partial s\)). A nozzle flow at constant inlet conditions is steady (unchanging in time) but non-uniform (faster downstream). All four combinations are possible.

Rate of Flow

The quantity of fluid passing per unit time across a designated area is the flow rate. There are two versions: volume flow rate \(Q\) (how much volume per second) and mass flow rate \(\dot{m}\) (how much mass per second). These are the workhorses of the continuity equation coming up in Chapter 5.

Volume Flow Rate (uniform velocity)

If a fluid of velocity \(v\) crosses an area \(A\) at right angles, the volume that passes in time \(\Delta t\) is a slug of length \(\Delta x = v\,\Delta t\), so:

\[ Q = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta t} = \frac{A\,\Delta x}{\Delta t} = A\,v \qquad \left[\tfrac{\text{m}^3}{\text{s}}\right] \]

\(Q\) — volume flow rate (m³/s) \(\Delta V\) — volume passing in \(\Delta t\) (m³) \(A\) — cross-sectional area (m²) \(v\) — velocity normal to the area (m/s)

When to use: Velocity is uniform across the section and perpendicular to it — the standard “pipe carrying speed v through area A” estimate.

When NOT to use: If velocity varies over the area (any real pipe profile) or is not normal to the area, you must integrate — see the general form below.

In time \(\Delta t\), the fluid crossing area \(A\) fills a slug of length \(v\,\Delta t\). Dividing that volume by \(\Delta t\) gives \(Q = Av\).
Volume Flow Rate (general)

In general the velocity is not uniform and the area may be tilted. For an area with outward unit normal \(\vec{n}\), only the normal component of velocity \(v_n = \vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}\) carries fluid across it. Integrating the incremental flow \(dQ = \vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}\,dA\) over the whole area:

\[ Q = \int_A \vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}\;dA = \int_A v_n\;dA \]

\(Q\) — total volume flow rate (m³/s) \(\vec{v}\) — local velocity vector (m/s) \(\vec{n}\) — unit normal to the area \(v_n = \vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}\) — velocity component normal to dA (m/s) \(dA\) — incremental area element (m²)

When to use: Velocity varies across the section (e.g. a parabolic pipe profile) or the surface is not perpendicular to the flow — always the safe, general choice.

When NOT to use: Only the tangential component \(v_t\) exists at the surface — then nothing crosses it and \(Q=0\); do not include \(v_t\) in the integral.

Decomposing the velocity at a tilted area element. Only the normal component \(v_n=\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}\) (sand) transports fluid across \(dA\); the tangential component \(v_t\) (grey) slides along the surface and carries nothing across it.
Average Velocity

Real velocity profiles vary across a section, so we define a single average (or mean) velocity \(\bar{U}\) — the uniform velocity that would carry the same total volume flow rate through the area:

\[ \bar{U} = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{1}{A}\int_A v_n\;dA \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad Q = A\,\bar{U} \]

\(\bar{U}\) — average (mean) velocity over the section (m/s) \(Q\) — total volume flow rate (m³/s) \(A\) — cross-sectional area (m²)

When to use: Reducing a varying profile to one representative speed — lets you write the simple \(Q=A\bar{U}\) in continuity and energy equations.

When NOT to use: When the shape of the profile matters (e.g. kinetic-energy or momentum corrections); the average alone discards that information.

A pipe's actual profile peaks at \(V_{max}\) on the centreline. The average velocity \(\bar{U}\) (orange) is the flat profile that would push the same volume through — for the parabolic case, \(\bar{U}=V_{max}/2\).
Mass Flow Rate

Mass flow rate is the amount of mass crossing a section per unit time. Since mass = density × volume, the mass flow rate is the volume flow rate scaled by density:

\[ \dot{m} = \rho\int_A v_n\;dA = \rho A\bar{U} = \rho Q \qquad \left[\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{s}}\right] \]

\(\dot{m}\) — mass flow rate (kg/s) \(\rho\) — fluid density (kg/m³) \(Q\) — volume flow rate (m³/s) \(\bar{U}\) — average velocity (m/s); \(A\) — area (m²)

When to use: Mass must be conserved (continuity), or whenever density appears — compressible flow, mixing, momentum/energy fluxes.

When NOT to use: Don't assume \(\rho\) is constant in a compressible gas flow — keep it inside the integral if it varies across the section.

Worked Example 1 — Parabolic Pipe Profile

Problem: The velocity in a pipe of radius \(R\) at radial distance \(r\) from the centre is

\[ v(r) = V_{max}\!\left(1 - \frac{r^2}{R^2}\right) \]

where \(V_{max}\) is the centreline (maximum) velocity. Find the volume flow rate, average velocity, and mass flow rate. If \(V_{max}\) is held constant, by what factors do these change if the pipe diameter is doubled?

Integrate over thin annular rings of area \(dA = 2\pi r\,dr\) from the centre (\(r=0\)) out to the wall (\(r=R\)).
Solution

Thought process: The velocity depends only on \(r\), so split the cross-section into thin rings of constant velocity. A ring at radius \(r\) with thickness \(dr\) has area \(dA = 2\pi r\,dr\). Integrate \(v(r)\,dA\) from 0 to R.

Step 1 — Set up the volume flow rate integral:

\[ Q = \int_A v\,dA = \int_0^R V_{max}\!\left(1-\frac{r^2}{R^2}\right)2\pi r\,dr \]

Step 2 — Pull out constants and expand:

\[ Q = 2\pi V_{max}\int_0^R\!\left(r - \frac{r^3}{R^2}\right)dr \]

Step 3 — Integrate:

\[ Q = 2\pi V_{max}\left[\frac{r^2}{2} - \frac{r^4}{4R^2}\right]_0^R = 2\pi V_{max}\left(\frac{R^2}{2} - \frac{R^2}{4}\right) = 2\pi V_{max}\cdot\frac{R^2}{4} \]

\[ \boxed{Q = \frac{\pi R^2 V_{max}}{2}} \]

Step 4 — Average velocity (divide by area \(A = \pi R^2\)):

\[ \bar{U} = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{\pi R^2 V_{max}/2}{\pi R^2} \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{\bar{U} = \frac{V_{max}}{2}} \]

Step 5 — Mass flow rate (multiply \(Q\) by density):

\[ \dot{m} = \rho Q \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{\dot{m} = \rho V_{max}\frac{\pi R^2}{2}} \]

Step 6 — Doubling the diameter doubles the radius (\(R \to 2R\)). Both \(Q\) and \(\dot{m}\) scale with \(R^2\), so each grows by a factor of \(2^2 = 4\). The average velocity \(\bar{U}=V_{max}/2\) contains no \(R\), so it is unchanged.

\[ \boxed{Q \to 4Q, \quad \dot{m} \to 4\dot{m}, \quad \bar{U}\ \text{unchanged}} \]

★ Tip: For any laminar parabolic pipe profile, \(\bar{U}=V_{max}/2\) — memorise this ratio. It saves you the integral on an exam when only the average is needed.

Worked Example 2 — Laminar Pipe Flow (numerical)

Problem: Steady laminar flow in a pipe has the profile \(u(r) = 3(1 - 25r^2)\) m/s, with \(R = 0.2\) m. Find the volumetric flow rate and the average velocity.

Solution

Thought process: Same annular-ring approach as Example 1 (\(dA = 2\pi r\,dr\)), but now with concrete numbers. First sanity-check: at the wall \(r=R=0.2\), \(u = 3(1-25(0.04)) = 3(1-1) = 0\) — good, the no-slip condition holds.

Step 1 — Set up and simplify the integral:

\[ Q = \int_A u(r)\,dA = \int_0^{0.2} 3(1-25r^2)\,2\pi r\,dr = 6\pi\int_0^{0.2}\!\left(r - 25r^3\right)dr \]

Step 2 — Integrate:

\[ Q = 6\pi\left[\frac{r^2}{2} - \frac{25 r^4}{4}\right]_0^{0.2} \]

Step 3 — Evaluate at the limits. At \(r=0.2\): \(\tfrac{r^2}{2} = \tfrac{0.04}{2} = 0.02\) and \(\tfrac{25 r^4}{4} = \tfrac{25(0.0016)}{4} = 0.01\):

\[ Q = 6\pi\,(0.02 - 0.01) = 6\pi(0.01) = 0.06\pi \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{Q \approx 0.188\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}} \]

Step 4 — Average velocity (\(A = \pi R^2 = \pi(0.2)^2\)):

\[ \bar{U} = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{0.188}{\pi(0.2)^2} = \frac{0.188}{0.1257} \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{\bar{U} \approx 1.5\ \text{m/s}} \]

★ Check: Here \(V_{max}=u(0)=3\) m/s, and indeed \(\bar{U}\approx1.5 = V_{max}/2\) — consistent with the parabolic result from Example 1.

Worked Example 3 — Linear Profile Between Plates

Problem: For the linear velocity profile between two plates of width \(w\) and spacing \(b\) (with \(y\) measured from the centreline), the profile is \(u(y) = u_{max}\!\left(1 - \tfrac{2y}{b}\right)\). Determine the average velocity and volumetric discharge in terms of \(u_{max}\).

Velocity is maximum on the centreline and falls linearly to zero at each plate. By symmetry, integrate one half (\(0\) to \(b/2\)) and double.
Solution

Thought process: The flow is between flat plates of width \(w\), so a thin strip at height \(y\) has area \(dA = w\,dy\). The profile is symmetric about the centreline, so integrate from \(0\) to \(b/2\) and multiply by 2.

Step 1 — Set up the integral (factor of 2 for symmetry):

\[ Q = 2\int_0^{b/2} u(y)\,w\,dy = 2w\,u_{max}\int_0^{b/2}\!\left(1 - \frac{2y}{b}\right)dy \]

Step 2 — Integrate:

\[ Q = 2w\,u_{max}\left[\,y - \frac{y^2}{b}\,\right]_0^{b/2} = 2w\,u_{max}\left(\frac{b}{2} - \frac{(b/2)^2}{b}\right) \]

Step 3 — Simplify:

\[ Q = 2w\,u_{max}\left(\frac{b}{2} - \frac{b}{4}\right) = 2w\,u_{max}\cdot\frac{b}{4} \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{Q = \frac{w\,u_{max}\,b}{2}} \]

Step 4 — Average velocity (the flow area is \(A = wb\)):

\[ \bar{U} = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{w\,u_{max}\,b/2}{wb} \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{\bar{U} = \frac{u_{max}}{2}} \]

★ Pattern: A linear (triangular) profile gives \(\bar{U}=u_{max}/2\) — the same one-half ratio as the parabolic pipe. The “average is half the peak” result is common for these symmetric profiles, but always confirm it by integrating.

★ Where This Is Heading

This lecture set up the Eulerian / control-volume viewpoint and the two flow rates, \(Q\) and \(\dot{m}\). In Chapter 5 (Reynolds Transport Theorem) these flow rates become the “flux” terms that turn conservation laws (mass, momentum, energy) into usable control-volume equations — starting with conservation of mass, the continuity equation, which is essentially “\(\dot{m}_{\text{in}} = \dot{m}_{\text{out}}\)” for steady flow.

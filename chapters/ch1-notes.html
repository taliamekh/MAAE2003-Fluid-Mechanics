1.1 — Introduction

Fluid mechanics studies how fluids (liquids and gases) behave at rest and in motion. It is one of the most broadly applicable areas in engineering — spanning aerodynamics, marine propulsion, biomedical devices, renewable energy, sports engineering, and environmental science. In this course, we develop governing equations using the control volume approach and apply them to practical engineering problems.

Applications of Fluid Mechanics

The reach of fluid mechanics is enormous. A few representative domains from the lectures:

Transportation — Aviation (helicopter vortex ring states, aircraft lift/drag), marine (ship propeller vortices, hull drag), automotive (CFD streamline simulations for race cars).

Biomedical — Computational fluid dynamics for artificial heart pump design, lung airflow simulation, blood flow modeling through arteries.

Energy — Water flow in hydroelectric dams, airflow through wind turbines, combustion chamber flows.

Environment — Ocean current modeling, atmospheric pollution dispersion, weather prediction.

Sports — Aerodynamic optimization of golf clubs, drag reduction on bobsleds and cycling helmets.

The Concept of a Fluid: Classification of Matter

Matter exists in four states — solid, liquid, gas, and plasma. In this course, we focus on liquids and gases, which are collectively called fluids. The critical distinction between solids and fluids comes down to how they respond to shear stress.

Solid

Molecules are packed in a lattice or geometric structure with constant molecular spacing. Large cohesive forces hold molecules together, preventing relative movement (aside from slight vibration). When subjected to a load, a solid deforms but continues to support the load. A solid can resist shear stress by deforming.

Liquid

Molecules are not bound in a lattice — they are more mobile than in solids, but molecular spacing is still approximately constant. Liquids do not hold their shape; they flow and take the shape of their container, forming a horizontal free surface at the top. When confined, their intermolecular forces allow them to resist compressive forces, so liquids have constant volume and density. However, they cannot resist shear loading — they form waves instead.

Gas

Intermolecular forces are weak enough to allow molecules to travel freely until repelled by other molecules. Gases have no definite volume — they must be held in a container, otherwise they flow out to form an atmosphere. A gas completely fills any space it occupies. Gravitational effects are negligible compared to buoyancy forces. Gases are still classified as fluids because they flow when subjected to a force.

★ Key Distinction

A solid resists shear stress by deforming (it reaches equilibrium under load). A fluid cannot resist any shear stress — it deforms continuously under even the smallest applied shear, no matter how small. This is the defining difference.

A fourth state, plasma (ionized matter), exists and is critical for fusion energy research, but it is not covered in this course.

The Concept of Continuum

At the molecular level, matter is discrete — individual molecules bouncing around with gaps between them. But in engineering, we want to treat fluid properties like density as smooth, continuous functions so we can use calculus. The continuum assumption makes this possible.

Consider measuring density by counting molecules in a small volume:

If the sample volume is comparable to intermolecular spacing (very tiny), the number of molecules inside fluctuates wildly as they move in and out. The measured density is noisy and unstable — this is microscopic uncertainty.

If the sample volume is much larger than intermolecular spacing, the number of molecules entering roughly equals the number leaving at any instant. The measured density stabilizes to a constant value — the fluctuations average out.

Density (Continuum Definition)

\[ \rho = \lim_{\delta V \to \delta V^*} \frac{\delta m}{\delta V} \]

\(\rho\) — density (kg/m³) \(\delta m\) — mass in volume element \(\delta V\) — volume element \(\delta V^*\) — smallest volume for stable density

The critical threshold is \(\delta V^* \sim 10^{-9}\) mm³, which is extremely small compared to any engineering dimension. At standard atmospheric conditions, this volume of air contains roughly \(3 \times 10^7\) molecules — more than enough for a stable average.

★ Why Continuum Matters

Because \(\delta V^*\) is so small, density (and all other fluid properties) can be treated as smoothly varying functions of position. This is what lets us apply differential calculus to fluid mechanics — writing partial derivatives, integrals, and governing equations. Without the continuum assumption, we would need to track individual molecules (molecular dynamics), which is computationally impractical for engineering-scale problems.

If the volume is too large (macroscopic uncertainty), the density starts reflecting spatial variations in the fluid rather than a point value — think of a container where fluid is sloshing and spilling. The continuum definition uses the limit approaching \(\delta V^*\) to capture a true local density.

Branches of Fluid Mechanics

The field is divided into three branches, each of which we will study in this course:

Hydrostatics

The study of fluids at rest or moving with constant velocity (no acceleration). Covers pressure distribution, forces on submerged surfaces, and buoyancy. This is the focus of Ch 1–2 in our notes.

Kinematics

The study of the geometry of fluid motion — velocity fields, streamlines, pathlines, and streaklines — without considering the forces that cause the motion. Covered beginning in Ch 4.

Fluid Dynamics

The study of the forces that cause accelerated motion in fluids. This is where we develop and apply the momentum and energy equations using the Reynolds Transport Theorem (Ch 5–6).

Question 1a — Angular Momentum & Pressure Forces (5 marks)

Statement: Consider the validity of this statement: "Pressure forces do not contribute to changes in angular momentum since they are non-directional forces (acting normal to any surfaces) and should not be included when conserving the angular momentum of a control volume."
🧠 Thought process

The claim says pressure is "non-directional" — this is wrong. Pressure produces a force \(\vec{F} = p \cdot A \cdot \hat{n}\) that acts in a specific direction (normal to each surface). While the scalar pressure \(p\) is isotropic, the resulting force has a direction. If this force has a moment arm \(\vec{r}\) about an axis, it creates torque \(\vec{r} \times \vec{F} \neq 0\). Therefore pressure absolutely contributes to angular momentum changes.

✅ Answer: (b) The statement is invalid

Pressure forces are directional — they act normal to surfaces and can create torque about any axis when they have a nonzero moment arm. They must be included in the angular momentum equation for a control volume.

★ Tip: "Pressure is a scalar" ≠ "pressure force is non-directional." The force vector is \(\vec{F}_p = -p\hat{n}\,dA\), which has magnitude AND direction.

Question 1b — Velocity Variation Along a Streamline (5 marks)

Statement: Consider the flow depicted in the figure. If the flow is incompressible, then the velocity variation of a particle moving along the streamline going through point A will follow which curve?
Streamlines converging toward a circular body — velocity graph options a through d
🧠 Thought process

The streamlines converge from left to right toward a circular body, then diverge past it. By continuity (\(A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2\)), as the cross-sectional area between streamlines decreases, velocity must increase. Approaching the body (A → B), the streamlines squeeze together so velocity increases. After passing the body, streamlines spread again and velocity decreases back.

At point A the velocity is \(V_A\). It must increase smoothly toward the narrowest point (near the body) and then decrease. This matches curve "a" — starts at \(V_A\), rises to a peak near the body, then drops.

✅ Answer: (a) curve "a"

The velocity increases as streamlines converge (area decreases), peaks near the body's narrowest cross-section, then decreases as streamlines diverge. Curve "a" is the only one showing this bell-shaped profile starting and ending near \(V_A\).

★ Tip: When streamlines get closer together → flow speeds up. When they spread apart → flow slows down. This is a direct visual application of \(Q = AV = \text{const}\).

Question 1c — Cylindrical Tank Mass Conservation (10 marks)

Statement: A cylindrical tank has two connecting pipes of equal radius \(R\). The lower pipe provides water inflow (\(\dot{m}_{\text{water}} = 10\) kg/s) while the upper pipe is used for air outflow. The pressure inside the tank above the water surface equals one atmosphere. The tank height is \(50R\) with unknown radius. The tank is half full. Find the air mass outflow.
Cylindrical tank with water inflow (bottom) and air outflow (top)
🧠 Thought process

This is a mass conservation problem. Water flows in at the bottom, raising the water level. As the water level rises, it compresses the air above it and pushes air out the top pipe. Key insight: the volume of water entering per second equals the volume of air leaving per second (incompressible water, atmospheric-pressure air). But mass flow rate = ρ × Q, so the mass outflow of air is much smaller because \(\rho_{\text{air}} \ll \rho_{\text{water}}\).

📋 Formulas Needed

  1. Mass conservation (CV): \(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{CV}\rho\,dV + \int_{CS}\rho(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\,dA = 0\)
  2. Incompressible volume balance: \(Q_{\text{in}} = Q_{\text{out}}\) → volume flow rate of water in = volume flow rate of air out
  3. Mass flow rate: \(\dot{m} = \rho Q\)

Solution

1
Volumetric flow rate of water in
Since water is incompressible, the volume flow rate entering is: \[Q_{\text{water}} = \frac{\dot{m}_{\text{water}}}{\rho_{\text{water}}} = \frac{10\text{ kg/s}}{1000\text{ kg/m}^3} = 0.01\text{ m}^3\text{/s}\]
2
Volume balance
As the water level rises by \(dh\), the water occupies volume \(A_{\text{tank}}\,dh\) and displaces the same volume of air from the tank. Therefore the volumetric flow rate of air leaving equals the volumetric flow rate of water entering: \[Q_{\text{air}} = Q_{\text{water}} = 0.01\text{ m}^3\text{/s}\]
3
Air mass outflow
At atmospheric pressure, \(\rho_{\text{air}} \approx 1.2\text{ kg/m}^3\): \[\dot{m}_{\text{air}} = \rho_{\text{air}} \cdot Q_{\text{air}} = 1.2 \times 0.01 = 0.012\text{ kg/s}\]
✅ Answer: (d) 0.012 kg/s
★ Tip: The tank radius is irrelevant — a classic distractor. The volume balance doesn't depend on the tank dimensions, only on the fact that incompressible water in = air displaced out. The mass ratio is simply \(\rho_{\text{air}}/\rho_{\text{water}} \approx 1.2/1000\), so 10 kg/s of water → 0.012 kg/s of air.

Question 2 — Sluice Gate Force (40 marks)

Statement: A sluice gate controls flow rate in a channel. A force is exerted on the gate due to the difference between water heights \(y_1\) (upstream) and \(y_2\) (downstream) and the flow velocities \(V_1\) and \(V_2\). The width of the gate into the page is \(w\). Wall shear stresses are negligible; assume steady, uniform flow at locations 1 and 2. Develop a relationship for the force \(F_R\) acting on the sluice gate as a function of \(y_1\), \(y_2\), \(\dot{m}\), \(g\), \(w\), and \(\rho\).
Sluice gate in an open channel — upstream depth y₁, downstream depth y₂
🧠 Thought process

This is a linear momentum + Bernoulli problem. The clue is "find the force on a gate" — that means momentum equation. But we also need Bernoulli to relate the velocities and pressures upstream and downstream. The strategy is: (1) Bernoulli between the free surfaces, (2) Continuity for velocity relation, (3) Momentum equation for the force.

The control volume is the water body between the upstream and downstream cross-sections, bounded by the channel bottom, free surfaces, and the gate surface.

📋 Formulas Needed

  1. Bernoulli's Equation: \(\frac{p_1}{\rho g} + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} + z_1 = \frac{p_2}{\rho g} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} + z_2\)
  2. Continuity: \(A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2 \implies (wy_1)V_1 = (wy_2)V_2\)
  3. Linear Momentum (x-dir): \(\sum F_x = \dot{m}V_2 - \dot{m}V_1\)
  4. Hydrostatic pressure force on vertical surface: \(F_p = \rho g \frac{y}{2} \cdot (wy) = \frac{1}{2}\rho g w y^2\)

Solution

1
Apply Bernoulli between the free surfaces (points 1 and 2)
Take point 1 at the upstream free surface and point 2 at the downstream free surface. Both are at atmospheric pressure (\(p_1 = p_2 = p_{\text{atm}}\)). Take the channel bottom as the elevation reference, so \(z_1 = y_1\) and \(z_2 = y_2\): \[\frac{V_1^2}{2g} + y_1 = \frac{V_2^2}{2g} + y_2\] \[\boxed{V_2^2 - V_1^2 = 2g(y_1 - y_2)} \quad \cdots (1)\]
2
Apply Continuity
For steady, incompressible flow with a single inlet and outlet: \[A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2 \implies (wy_1)V_1 = (wy_2)V_2\] The mass flow rate is \(\dot{m} = \rho A V\), so: \[V_1 = \frac{\dot{m}}{\rho w y_1}, \quad V_2 = \frac{\dot{m}}{\rho w y_2} \quad \cdots (2)\]
3
Set up the momentum equation
Choose the control volume as the water body bounded by the upstream cross-section (area \(wy_1\)), downstream cross-section (area \(wy_2\)), the channel bottom, the free surfaces, and the inner surface of the sluice gate. The forces in the x-direction are:
  • Upstream pressure force (pushing right): \(P_1 A_1 = \rho g \frac{y_1}{2}(wy_1) = \frac{1}{2}\rho g w y_1^2\)
  • Downstream pressure force (pushing left): \(P_2 A_2 = \rho g \frac{y_2}{2}(wy_2) = \frac{1}{2}\rho g w y_2^2\)
  • Gate reaction force \(F_{Rx}\) (acts on the fluid, assumed pointing left/negative)
  • Wall shear: negligible (given)
The linear momentum equation in the x-direction: \[\sum F_x = \dot{m}V_2 - \dot{m}V_1\] \[-F_{Rx} + \frac{1}{2}\rho g w y_1^2 - \frac{1}{2}\rho g w y_2^2 = \dot{m}(V_2 - V_1)\]
4
Solve for FRx
Rearranging: \[\boxed{F_{Rx} = \dot{m}(V_1 - V_2) + \frac{w}{2}\rho g(y_1^2 - y_2^2)}\] where \(V_1 = \frac{\dot{m}}{\rho w y_1}\) and \(V_2 = \frac{\dot{m}}{\rho w y_2}\) from Eq. (2).
5
Substitute velocity expressions (expanded form)
Substituting the continuity relations into the force expression: \[F_{Rx} = \dot{m}\left(\frac{\dot{m}}{\rho w y_1} - \frac{\dot{m}}{\rho w y_2}\right) + \frac{w}{2}\rho g(y_1^2 - y_2^2)\] Or equivalently, using the Bernoulli-derived velocity expressions from Eq. (1) and (2): \[F_{Rx} = \dot{m}\left(\frac{y_2}{y_1} - 1\right)\sqrt{\frac{2g(y_1 - y_2)}{1 - y_2^2/y_1^2}} + \frac{w}{2}\rho g(y_1^2 - y_2^2)\]
📦 Final Answer
\[F_{Rx} = \dot{m}(V_1 - V_2) + \frac{w}{2}\rho g(y_1^2 - y_2^2)\]
Momentum contribution + Hydrostatic pressure contribution
★ Tips & tricks:
• The force has two parts: momentum change (\(\dot{m}\Delta V\)) and net pressure force (\(\frac{w}{2}\rho g \Delta(y^2)\)). Most students forget the pressure term — it's the hydrostatic force difference between the two cross-sections.
• \(F_{Rx}\) is the force the gate exerts on the fluid. The force on the gate is equal and opposite (Newton's 3rd law). Since \(y_1 > y_2\), the force on the gate points downstream (to the right).
• The problem says "develop a relationship" — this means a symbolic expression. Don't plug in numbers.

Question 3 — Downward Pipe Flow with Manometer (40 marks)

Statement: Water flows steadily downward in the pipe shown in the figure with negligible losses. Determine the flow rate. The upper pipe has diameter 1.2 m, the lower pipe has diameter 1 m. A U-tube manometer with oil (SG = 0.7) connects the two pipe sections. The upper manometer column height \(h_1 = 1.5\) m, the vertical distance between pipe centres is 2 m, and the lower manometer column height \(h_2 = 2\) m.
Downward pipe flow with U-tube oil manometer (SG = 0.7)
🧠 Thought process

This is a Bernoulli + Continuity + Manometer problem. "Find the flow rate" with two pipe diameters and a manometer connecting them. Strategy: (1) Use Bernoulli between points 1 and 2 (the two pipe centres), (2) Use continuity to relate \(V_1\) and \(V_2\), (3) Use the manometer to find the pressure difference \(p_2 - p_1\), (4) Combine everything to solve for \(V_2\), then compute \(Q = A_2 V_2\).

📋 Formulas Needed

  1. Bernoulli: \(\frac{p_1}{\gamma} + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} + z_1 = \frac{p_2}{\gamma} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} + z_2\)
  2. Continuity: \(A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2\)
  3. Manometer pressure trace: step-by-step through the U-tube
  4. Volume flow rate: \(Q = AV = \frac{\pi}{4}D^2 V\)

Solution

1
Write Bernoulli between points 1 (upper pipe) and 2 (lower pipe)
Let \(z_1 - z_2 = 2\) m (upper pipe is 2 m above lower pipe). With no losses: \[\frac{p_1}{\gamma} + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} + z_1 = \frac{p_2}{\gamma} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} + z_2 \quad \cdots (1)\]
2
Apply Continuity
\[A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2 \implies \frac{\pi}{4}(1.2)^2 V_1 = \frac{\pi}{4}(1.0)^2 V_2\] \[V_1 = \frac{(1.0)^2}{(1.2)^2} V_2 = 0.694\,V_2 \quad \cdots (2)\]
3
Read the manometer — trace pressure from point 1 to point 2
Starting at point 1, trace through the manometer step by step. The open end of the manometer is at the top, and the manometer fluid is oil with \(\gamma_{\text{oil}} = 0.7\gamma\):
  • At point 1: pressure = \(p_1\)
  • Go down \(h_1 = 1.5\) m in water: pressure increases by \(\gamma h_1\) → \(p_1 + \gamma h_1\)
  • Cross into oil, go up \(h_1 = 1.5\) m in oil: pressure decreases by \(\gamma_{\text{oil}} h_1 = 0.7\gamma h_1\)
  • Continue up \(h_2 = 2\) m in water on the other side to reach point 2: pressure decreases by \(\gamma h_2\)
Therefore: \[p_2 = p_1 + \gamma h_1 - 0.7\gamma h_1 - \gamma h_2\] Wait — let me be more careful. From the figure, the manometer setup is:
  • \(p_1 = \gamma h_1\) (at point 1, the pressure is due to the water column above)
  • \(p_2 = \gamma_{\text{oil}} h_1 + \gamma h_2\) (at point 2, the pressure comes from oil column + water column)
The pressure difference: \[\frac{p_2 - p_1}{\gamma} = (0.7h_1 + h_2) - h_1 = h_2 - 0.3h_1\] \[\frac{p_2 - p_1}{\gamma} = 2 - 0.3(1.5) = 2 - 0.45 = 1.55\text{ m} \quad \cdots (3)\]
4
Substitute into Bernoulli and solve
Rearranging Eq. (1): \[z_1 - z_2 = \frac{p_2 - p_1}{\gamma} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} - \frac{V_1^2}{2g}\] Substituting Eqs. (2) and (3): \[2 = 1.55 + \frac{V_2^2}{2(9.81)}\left(1 - (0.694)^2\right)\] \[0.45 = \frac{V_2^2}{19.62}(1 - 0.4816) = \frac{V_2^2}{19.62}(0.5184)\] \[V_2^2 = \frac{0.45 \times 19.62}{0.5184} = 17.03\] \[V_2 = 4.13\text{ m/s}\]
5
Compute the flow rate
\[Q = A_2 V_2 = \frac{\pi}{4}(1\text{ m})^2 \times 4.13\text{ m/s}\] \[Q = 0.7854 \times 4.13 = 3.24\text{ m}^3\text{/s}\]
📦 Final Answer
\[Q = 3.24 \text{ m}^3\text{/s}\]
★ Tips & tricks:
• When reading a manometer, always trace from one known point to the other: +ρgh going down, −ρgh going up, changing ρ at each fluid interface.
• The continuity ratio \(V_1/V_2 = (D_2/D_1)^2\) — for circular pipes, it's the diameter ratio squared (not the radius).
• Don't forget that \(z_1 - z_2 = +2\) m because flow goes downward — the upper pipe is at the higher elevation. This is a free energy source that helps the flow.

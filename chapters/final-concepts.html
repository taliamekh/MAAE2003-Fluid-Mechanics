📌 The toolkit you bring in

Pipe flow is not a brand-new theory — it is the energy/head equation with the loss term spelled out. Make sure these four are automatic before exam day.

Energy / head equation (§5.4, #43)
\[\frac{p_1}{\gamma}+\alpha_1\frac{V_1^2}{2g}+z_1=\frac{p_2}{\gamma}+\alpha_2\frac{V_2^2}{2g}+z_2+h_{loss}-h_p+h_t\]

Every pipe problem is this equation. Pipe flow just replaces \(h_{loss}\) with \(\sum h_f+\sum h_m\). Sign pattern: +loss, −pump, +turbine.

\(h_{loss}\) — total head lost to friction (m) \(h_p\) — pump head added \(h_t\) — turbine head extracted \(\alpha\) — KE correction (≈2 laminar, ≈1.05 turbulent)
Bernoulli (§3.5)
\[\frac{p_1}{\gamma}+\frac{V_1^2}{2g}+z_1=\frac{p_2}{\gamma}+\frac{V_2^2}{2g}+z_2\]

The head equation with no losses and no machine — valid only along a streamline, steady, incompressible, frictionless. The instant a real pipe has length, Bernoulli is replaced by the head equation above.

Continuity (§5.1, #16–#18)
\[Q=A\,V=\text{const}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad V=\frac{4Q}{\pi d^2}\]

Gives you the velocity every pipe formula needs. For a round pipe \(A=\tfrac{\pi}{4}d^2\), so \(V=4Q/\pi d^2\) is the workhorse substitution.

Kinematic viscosity (§1.2, #5)
\[\nu=\frac{\mu}{\rho}\]

The Reynolds number uses \(\nu\), not \(\mu\). If a problem gives dynamic viscosity \(\mu\) and density \(\rho\), convert first. Units: \(\nu\) in \(\text{m}^2/\text{s}\).

📚 Core concepts (Ch 6)

A · Flow regimes & the Reynolds number

Two regimes

Laminar flow is ordered — fluid moves in smooth layers with no mixing (Reynolds' dye filament stays a thin line). Turbulent flow is chaotic — eddies mix the fluid (the dye bursts and spreads). The regime decides which friction-factor rule you use, so it is always the first thing you establish.

Reynolds number (#47)
\[Re_d=\frac{\rho V d}{\mu}=\frac{V d}{\nu}\]

The ratio of inertia to viscous forces. Dimensionless. Big \(Re\) ⇒ inertia wins ⇒ turbulent; small \(Re\) ⇒ viscosity wins ⇒ laminar.

\(\rho\) — density (kg/m³) \(V\) — average velocity (m/s) \(d\) — pipe diameter (m) \(\mu\) — dynamic viscosity (Pa·s) \(\nu=\mu/\rho\) — kinematic viscosity (m²/s)
★ Regime thresholds

\(Re_{crit}\approx 2300\). Practical bands: \(Re_d<2000\) laminar · \(2000\text{–}4000\) transitional (unpredictable) · \(>4000\) turbulent. Compute \(Re\) first; it picks the \(f\) formula. Roughness \(\varepsilon/d\) matters only in turbulent flow.

B · Major losses — Darcy–Weisbach & the friction factor

Friction head loss \(h_f\)

The mechanical energy a fluid loses to wall friction down a straight run of pipe — "major" because it is distributed over the whole length. This is exactly the \(h_{loss}\) of the head equation for a plain pipe with no fittings.

Darcy–Weisbach (#48)
\[h_f=f\,\frac{L}{d}\,\frac{V^2}{2g}\]

The master equation for major loss — works in both regimes; only the way you get \(f\) changes. Warning: this is the Darcy friction factor \(f\), which is \(4\times\) the Fanning factor. The laminar line is \(64/Re\), not \(16/Re\).

\(f\) — Darcy friction factor (dimensionless) \(L\) — pipe length (m) \(d\) — diameter (m) \(V\) — velocity (m/s) \(g\) — 9.81 m/s² (32.2 ft/s²)
Friction factor — laminar (#50)
\[f=\frac{64}{Re_d}\]

Exact, derived from the parabolic velocity profile. Roughness-independent — a rough pipe and a smooth pipe have the same \(f\) in laminar flow. No chart needed.

Friction factor — turbulent (#51): Colebrook / Haaland
\[\frac{1}{\sqrt f}=-2.0\log\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon/d}{3.7}+\frac{2.51}{Re_d\sqrt f}\right)\quad\text{(Colebrook, implicit)}\] \[\frac{1}{\sqrt f}\approx-1.8\log\!\left[\frac{6.9}{Re_d}+\left(\frac{\varepsilon/d}{3.7}\right)^{1.11}\right]\quad\text{(Haaland, explicit, }\pm2\%)\]

\(f\) depends on \(Re_d\) and relative roughness \(\varepsilon/d\). Colebrook is the standard but implicit (needs iteration / the chart); Haaland is explicit and exam-friendly. The Moody chart is just Colebrook plotted — read \(Re\) on the bottom, follow the \(\varepsilon/d\) curve, read \(f\) on the left.

\(\varepsilon\) — wall roughness height (m), from a table \(\varepsilon/d\) — relative roughness (dimensionless)
Head loss ↔ pressure drop (#49)
\[\Delta p=\gamma\,h_f\]

Only for a horizontal, constant-area pipe (no elevation change, \(V_1=V_2\)). If the pipe rises or changes size, go back to the full head equation — don't shortcut it.

C · The three single-pipe problems

The heart of Ch 6. Any two of \(\{d,\ Q,\ h_f\}\) are given and you find the third. Name the problem by which one is missing — that tells you whether the solve is direct or iterative.

The taxonomy

Type 1 (rating) — missing \(h_f\): everything you need for the march is known. Direct.
Type 2 (analysis) — missing \(Q\): \(V\) is unknown, so \(Re\) and \(f\) are unknown too. Solved by the explicit ζ method (or iterate on \(f\)).
Type 3 (design) — missing \(d\): \(d\) hides inside \(Re\), \(\varepsilon/d\) and the loss equation at once. Always iterative.

Type 2 — dimensionless head loss (#53) + explicit Reynolds (#54)
\[\zeta=\frac{g\,d^3 h_f}{L\,\nu^2}\qquad\Big(=\tfrac12 f\,Re_d^{\,2}\Big)\] \[Re_d=-\sqrt{8\zeta}\;\log\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon/d}{3.7}+\frac{1.775}{\sqrt{\zeta}}\right)\]

\(\zeta\) is built entirely from known quantities (no \(V\) inside), so it breaks the circularity. Then \(V=\nu Re_d/d\) and \(Q=\tfrac{\pi}{4}d^2 V\). Turbulent only — verify \(Re_d>4000\) at the end.

\(\zeta\) — dimensionless head loss all RHS quantities known up front
Type 3 — diameter equation (#55) + Reynolds from flow (#56)
\[d^5=\frac{8\,f\,L\,Q^2}{\pi^2\,g\,h_f}\qquad\qquad Re_d=\frac{4Q}{\pi d\,\nu}\]

Darcy–Weisbach solved for \(d^5\) after substituting \(V=4Q/\pi d^2\). Recipe: guess \(f\approx0.02\text{–}0.03\) → solve \(d^5\) → get \(Re_d\) and \(\varepsilon/d\) → read a new \(f\) from Moody → repeat. Converges fast because \(d\propto f^{1/5}\).

★ Type-spotting

Name the type before writing equations. The classic error is running the Type-1 direct march when \(V\) is unknown — you can't, because \(f\) needs \(Re\) which needs \(V\). Missing \(Q\) or \(d\) ⇒ you must use ζ or iterate.

D · Minor losses & the complete system

Minor losses \(h_m\)

Local losses at fittings, valves, bends, entrances, exits and area changes. "Minor" means local, not small — a nearly-closed valve can lose more head than a long straight pipe. They go in the same \(h_{loss}\) bucket as friction.

Loss-coefficient form (#57)
\[h_m=K\,\frac{V^2}{2g}=\frac{\Delta p}{\rho g}\]

\(K\) is a dimensionless, experimentally-measured coefficient (manufacturer-dependent for valves). Each fitting has its own \(K\); you look them up and add them.

\(K\) — loss coefficient (dimensionless) \(V\) — velocity in the relevant pipe (m/s)
Total loss (#58) & single-diameter shortcut (#59)
\[h_{loss}=\sum h_f+\sum h_m\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad h_{total}=\frac{V^2}{2g}\left(f\,\frac{\sum L}{d}+\sum K\right)\]

Feed \(h_{loss}\) into the head equation. The factored form on the right only works when one diameter (one \(V\)) runs the whole circuit — multiple sizes ⇒ compute each segment separately because \(V=Q/A\) changes.

★ Standard K values worth memorising

Sharp entrance \(K\approx0.5\); rounded entrance \(K\approx0.05\). Sharp exit \(K=1.0\) — the entire velocity head is dumped into the reservoir (don't forget this one). Flanged fittings lose less than screwed; valves vary widely (open globe ≈ 5–10, open gate ≈ 0.1–0.2). For sudden expansion/contraction, apply \(K\) to the smaller-pipe (faster) velocity.

🧩 Question types & quick-solve guides

Step-by-step recipes for each shape. Practice until the first move is automatic.

① Type 1 — Rating (find \(h_f\) / \(\Delta p\))
  1. Velocity from flow: \(V=4Q/\pi d^2\).
  2. Reynolds number: \(Re_d=Vd/\nu\) → state the regime.
  3. Friction factor: laminar \(f=64/Re_d\); turbulent → \(\varepsilon/d\) → Moody / Haaland.
  4. Head loss: \(h_f=f(L/d)(V^2/2g)\).
  5. Pressure drop (if horizontal, constant-area): \(\Delta p=\gamma h_f\).
② Type 2 — Analysis (find \(Q\))
  1. Assume turbulent (verify at the end).
  2. Dimensionless head loss: \(\zeta=g\,d^3 h_f/(L\nu^2)\).
  3. Explicit Reynolds: \(Re_d=-\sqrt{8\zeta}\,\log\big(\tfrac{\varepsilon/d}{3.7}+\tfrac{1.775}{\sqrt\zeta}\big)\).
  4. Velocity & flow: \(V=\nu Re_d/d\), \(Q=\tfrac{\pi}{4}d^2V\).
  5. Verify \(Re_d>4000\) (else the flow is laminar — solve \(f=64/Re\) directly instead).
③ Type 3 — Design (find \(d\))
  1. Guess \(f\approx0.02\text{–}0.03\).
  2. Diameter: \(d^5=8fLQ^2/(\pi^2 g\,h_f)\) → \(d\).
  3. Re & roughness: \(Re_d=4Q/(\pi d\nu)\), compute \(\varepsilon/d\).
  4. New \(f\) from Moody / Haaland; repeat from step 2 until \(f\) stops changing (2–3 passes).
④ Complete system — pump between two reservoirs
  1. Pick the two free surfaces as points 1 and 2: there \(p=0\) (gauge) and \(V\approx0\).
  2. Head equation collapses to \(z_1+h_p=z_2+h_{loss}\) ⇒ \(h_p=(z_2-z_1)+h_{loss}\).
  3. Pipe velocity: \(V=Q/A=4Q/\pi d^2\); then \(Re_d\) → regime → \(f\).
  4. Major loss: \(\sum h_f=f(L/d)(V^2/2g)\).
  5. Minor loss: list every fitting → \(\sum K\) (don't forget the sharp exit \(K=1.0\)) → \(\sum h_m=(\sum K)V^2/2g\).
  6. Solve \(h_p\), then power \(P=\rho g Q\,h_p\) (ideal); divide by \(\eta_p\) for shaft power.
⑤ Conceptual — laminar vs turbulent / which \(f\)?
  1. Compute \(Re_d=Vd/\nu\) (convert \(\mu\to\nu=\mu/\rho\) if needed).
  2. Classify: \(<2300\) laminar (use \(f=64/Re\)); \(>4000\) turbulent (Moody / Colebrook); \(2000\text{–}4000\) transitional.
  3. Remember roughness \(\varepsilon/d\) affects \(f\) only when turbulent — in laminar flow \(f\) depends on \(Re\) alone.

⚡ Concept traps & tips

  • No Moody chart in laminar flow. If \(Re_d<2300\), \(f=64/Re\) — full stop. Reaching for the chart is the #1 Ch 6 slip.
  • Smaller-pipe velocity for area changes. Sudden expansion/contraction \(K\) is applied to the faster (smaller-\(d\)) stream in \(KV^2/2g\).
  • The \((f\Sigma L/d+\Sigma K)\) factoring is single-diameter only. Different sizes ⇒ separate segments — \(V\) changes with \(A\).
  • \(\Delta p=\gamma h_f\) is horizontal & constant-area only. Any elevation change or machine ⇒ use the full head equation.
  • Don't mix \(\mu\) and \(\nu\). Reynolds uses \(\nu=\mu/\rho\). Read the units the problem gives you.
  • Type 2 / Type 3 explicit formulas are turbulent-only. Always verify \(Re\) at the end; if it came out laminar, redo with \(f=64/Re\).
  • Keep BG units straight. \(g=32.2\ \text{ft/s}^2\), mass in slugs — many textbook pipe examples are imperial.
  • Carry \(\alpha\) if asked. The full head equation has \(\alpha V^2/2g\) (\(\alpha\approx2\) laminar, \(\approx1.05\) turbulent); most exam problems take \(\alpha=1\) unless stated.

🎯 If you memorise nothing else

  • Reynolds: \(Re_d=Vd/\nu\), threshold \(\approx2300\) (laminar below, turbulent above 4000).
  • Laminar \(f\): \(f=64/Re_d\) (exact, no chart).
  • Major loss: \(h_f=f\dfrac{L}{d}\dfrac{V^2}{2g}\) (Darcy–Weisbach).
  • Minor loss: \(h_m=K\dfrac{V^2}{2g}\); sharp entrance \(0.5\), sharp exit \(1.0\).
  • Total: \(h_{loss}=\sum h_f+\sum h_m\) → into \(\dfrac{p}{\gamma}+\dfrac{V^2}{2g}+z\) with +loss / −pump / +turbine.
  • Name by the missing variable: \(h_f\)→Type 1, \(Q\)→Type 2, \(d\)→Type 3, pump/2 reservoirs→system.
  • Type 2: \(\zeta=\dfrac{g d^3 h_f}{L\nu^2}\).  Type 3: \(d^5=\dfrac{8fLQ^2}{\pi^2 g h_f}\).

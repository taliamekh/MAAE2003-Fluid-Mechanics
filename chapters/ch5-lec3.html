5.3 — RTT: Angular Momentum

Ch 3.6 — Reynolds Transport Theorem applied to the moment-of-momentum principle

Angular Momentum & Euler’s Law

Linear momentum (Ch 5.2) tells us the net force on a control volume. But many engineering problems — turbomachinery, pipe elbows, rotating sprinklers — also need the net moment (torque). The angular momentum equation fills that gap.

Angular Momentum of a System

The angular momentum of a fluid system about a point \(O\) is the moment of the system’s linear momentum about that point:

\[\vec{H}_O = \sum_O (\vec{r} \times m\vec{V})\]
\(\vec{H}_O\) — angular momentum about point \(O\) \(\vec{r}\) — position vector from \(O\) to the fluid element \(m\vec{V}\) — linear momentum of the fluid element

Euler’s law (the rotational analog of Newton’s 2nd law) states that the sum of external moments equals the rate of change of angular momentum:

Euler’s Law (System Form)
\[\sum_O (\vec{r} \times \vec{F}) = \sum_O \vec{M} = \frac{d}{dt} \sum_O (\vec{r} \times m\vec{V})\]
\(\sum \vec{M}\) — sum of all external moments about \(O\) \(\vec{r} \times \vec{F}\) — moment of each external force about \(O\)
Fig 5.3.1 — Angular momentum \(\vec{r} \times m\vec{V}\) of a fluid element in a control volume about point \(O\).

Angular Momentum Equation via RTT

Angular momentum \(\vec{H}_O\) is an extensive property. To convert the system form into a control-volume form, identify the intensive property:

Intensive Property for Angular Momentum
\[\beta = \frac{d}{dm}(\vec{r} \times m\vec{V}) = \vec{r} \times \vec{V}\]
\(\beta\) — angular momentum per unit mass \(\vec{r} \times \vec{V}\) — cross product of position and velocity vectors

Substituting \(\beta = \vec{r} \times \vec{V}\) into the general RTT gives the angular momentum equation for a control volume:

Angular Momentum Equation (General Form)
\[\boxed{\sum_O \vec{M}_{\text{ext}} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \int_{\text{CV}} (\vec{r} \times \vec{V})\,\rho\,d\forall \;+\; \int_{\text{CS}} (\vec{r} \times \vec{V})\,\rho\,(\vec{V} \cdot \hat{n})\,dA}\]
\(\sum \vec{M}_{\text{ext}}\) — sum of all external moments about \(O\) (N·m) \(\vec{r}\) — position vector from \(O\) to the fluid element (m) \(\vec{V}\) — fluid velocity (m/s) \(\rho\) — fluid density (kg/m³) \(\hat{n}\) — outward unit normal on the CS
★ Three-Term Structure — Same Pattern as Linear Momentum

The angular momentum equation has the exact same three-term structure as linear momentum (Ch 5.2), just with \(\vec{V}\) replaced by \(\vec{r} \times \vec{V}\):

LHSSum of external moments on the CV
1st RHSStorage — rate of change of angular momentum inside the CV
2nd RHSFlux — net angular momentum carried out through the CS

Steady & Ideal Flow Simplification

For steady flow, the storage term vanishes (\(\partial/\partial t = 0\)). For ideal flow with discrete ports (uniform properties at each inlet/outlet), the surface integral collapses to a sum over ports:

Angular Momentum — Steady Ideal Flow
\[\sum_O \vec{M}_{\text{ext}} = \sum \rho\,(\vec{r} \times \vec{V})\,(\vec{V} \cdot \hat{n})\,A = \sum (\vec{r} \times \vec{V})\,\dot{m}\]
Summation is over all inlet/outlet ports \(\dot{m} = \rho(\vec{V} \cdot \hat{n})A\) — mass flow rate at each port (negative at inlets)
★ Key Insight — When to Use Angular Momentum vs. Linear Momentum

Use angular momentum when the problem asks for a moment or torque at a support point. The strategy: first solve for forces with linear momentum (Ch 5.2), then apply angular momentum about the support to find the unknown moment. This avoids needing the support reaction forces in the moment equation.

Example 1 — Moment at a Pipe Elbow

Example 1: Pipe Elbow — Moment at Support A

Problem: Water flows through the pipe elbow shown with velocity \(V_A = 5\) ft/s at inlet \(A\) (diameter 3 in). The elbow turns the flow 90° downward to outlet \(B\) (diameter 1.5 in). Find the moment \(M_A\) needed at \(A\), in addition to forces, to hold the elbow in place. Neglect the weight of the elbow and water. Assume ideal steady flow. Use \(\rho_{\text{water}} = 62.4\) lb/ft³.

Fig 5.3.2 — Pipe elbow setup with dimensions and flow direction.
🧠 Thought process

This is a three-tool problem: Bernoulli (pressure), linear momentum (forces), then angular momentum (moment). The question asks for \(M_A\), which means I need the angular momentum equation about point \(A\). I’ll need \(F_x\) and \(F_y\) from linear momentum, but those are intermediate steps — the moment equation is the final goal.

Step 1 — Continuity: Find the outlet velocity.

\[Q = V_A A_A = 5\left[\pi\left(\frac{1.5}{12}\right)^2\right] = 0.2454 \;\text{ft}^3\!/\text{s}\]
\[V_A A_A = V_B A_B \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_B = \frac{A_A}{A_B}V_A = \frac{\pi(1.5)^2}{\pi(0.75)^2}\times 5 = 20 \;\text{ft/s}\]

Step 2 — Bernoulli between A and B: Find \(p_A\). Both points are at the same elevation (\(z_A = z_B\)), and \(p_B = 0\) (open to atmosphere).

\[\frac{p_A}{\rho} + \frac{V_A^2}{2} + gz_A = \frac{p_B}{\rho} + \frac{V_B^2}{2} + gz_B\]
\[\frac{p_A}{62.4/32.2} + \frac{5^2}{2} + 0 = 0 + \frac{20^2}{2} + 0\]
\[\boxed{p_A = 363.354 \;\text{lb/ft}^2 = 2.523 \;\text{lb/in}^2}\]
Fig 5.3.3 — Free-body diagram: forces \(F_x\), \(F_y\), and moment \(M\) at support \(A\).

Step 3 — Linear momentum (x-direction): At inlet \(A\), velocity is in the \(+x\) direction. At outlet \(B\), velocity is in the \(-y\) direction (no x-component).

\[\sum F_x = \rho Q(V_{Bx} - V_{Ax})\]
\[-F_x + p_A A_A = \frac{62.4}{32.2}(0.2454)(0 - 5)\]
\[-F_x + 2.523[\pi(1.5)^2] = -2.378\]
\[\boxed{F_x = 20.2 \;\text{lb}}\]

Step 4 — Linear momentum (y-direction): At \(A\), there is no y-component. At \(B\), the flow moves in the \(-y\) direction at 20 ft/s.

\[\sum F_y = \rho Q(V_{By} - V_{Ay})\]
\[-F_y = \frac{62.4}{32.2}(0.2454)(-20 - 0)\]
\[\boxed{F_y = 9.51 \;\text{lb}}\]
Fig 5.3.4 — Angular momentum about \(A\): distance \(d\) is the moment arm from \(A\) to the outlet velocity at \(B\).

Step 5 — Angular momentum about A: The inlet momentum passes through \(A\), so its moment arm is zero. Only the outlet at \(B\) contributes. The distance \(d\) from \(A\) to the line of action of \(V_B\) is 8 in = 2/3 ft (the horizontal distance from \(A\) to \(B\)).

\[\sum_A M_{\text{ext}} = \sum (\vec{r} \times \vec{V})\,\dot{m}\]
\[-M_A = \rho Q V_B \,d\]

Note: the sign is negative because \(V_B\) at the outlet creates a clockwise moment about \(A\) (opposing the assumed direction of \(M_A\)).

\[-M_A = \frac{62.4}{32.2}(0.2454)(20)\left(\frac{8}{12}\right)\]
\[\boxed{M_A = 6.34 \;\text{lb} \cdot \text{ft} \;\circlearrowright}\]

The moment at \(A\) is 6.34 lb·ft acting clockwise (in the direction that would resist the turning of the flow).

Example 2 — Fan Tipping Problem

Example 2: Minimum Base Diameter to Prevent Tipping

Problem: A fan blows air at \(Q = 6000\) ft³/min. It weighs \(W = 40\) lb with center of gravity at \(G\), has fan diameter 2 ft, and total height 6 ft. The center of the fan is offset 0.75 ft from the pole axis. Determine the smallest base diameter \(d\) so the fan does not tip over. Use \(\gamma_a = 0.076\) lb/ft³.

Fig 5.3.5 — Pedestal fan setup: find minimum base diameter \(d\) to prevent tipping.
🧠 Thought process

This is a moment-balance/tipping problem. The fan is about to tip when the normal force \(N\) moves to the edge of the base (point \(C\)). Taking moments about \(C\): the weight creates a stabilizing moment, while the momentum of the air jet creates a tipping moment. Setting them equal gives the critical \(d\).

Step 1 — Find the exit velocity at the fan face.

\[Q = \frac{6000 \;\text{ft}^3/\text{min}}{60 \;\text{s/min}} = 100 \;\text{ft}^3\!/\text{s}\]
\[Q = V_B A_B = V_B \pi(1)^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_B = \frac{100}{\pi} = 31.83 \;\text{ft/s}\]
Fig 5.3.6 — FBD for tipping analysis: moments about the base edge \(C\). Weight \(W\) stabilizes; air-jet momentum destabilizes.

Step 2 — Moment balance about the tipping point C. At incipient tipping, the base pivots about its edge \(C\). The stabilizing moment comes from the weight acting at distance \((0.75 + d/2)\) from \(C\). The destabilizing moment comes from the air-jet momentum flux acting at height \(h = 6\) ft.

\[M_{\text{stabilizing}} = M_{\text{tipping}}\]
\[W\left(0.75 + \frac{d}{2}\right) = \rho Q V_B \cdot h\]
\[40\left(0.75 + \frac{d}{2}\right) = \frac{0.076}{32.2}(100)(31.83)(6)\]
\[40\left(0.75 + \frac{d}{2}\right) = 45.10\]
\[0.75 + \frac{d}{2} = 1.1275\]
\[\boxed{d = 0.7539 \;\text{ft} \approx 9.05 \;\text{in}}\]

Angular Momentum Problem-Solving Checklist

★ Step-by-Step Approach
  1. Draw the CV and label all inlets/outlets with velocities, areas, and pressures.
  2. Choose the moment point wisely — pick the point where unknown forces act so they drop out of the moment equation (their moment arm is zero).
  3. Apply continuity to find unknown velocities at outlets.
  4. Apply Bernoulli (if needed) to find unknown pressures.
  5. Apply linear momentum in x and y to find reaction forces (if needed).
  6. Apply angular momentum: \(\sum M_{\text{ext}} = \sum(\vec{r} \times \vec{V})\dot{m}\). Only ports with nonzero moment arms contribute.
  7. Sign convention: be consistent. Typically use CCW positive. The cross product \(\vec{r} \times \vec{V}\) determines the sign of each port’s contribution.
★ Exam Warning — Common Angular Momentum Mistakes
  • Forgetting that inlet momentum through the moment point has zero arm — if the inlet velocity passes through the moment point, its cross product is zero. Only ports offset from the moment point contribute.
  • Confusing torque with force — angular momentum gives a moment (N·m or lb·ft), not a force. It’s the \(\vec{r} \times \vec{V}\) that distinguishes it from linear momentum.
  • Wrong moment arm — \(d\) is the perpendicular distance from the moment point to the line of action of the velocity, not the straight-line distance to the port center.
  • Inconsistent units — in BG units, always divide specific weight \(\gamma\) by \(g = 32.2\) ft/s² to get \(\rho\) in slugs/ft³. Never use lb/ft³ directly in momentum equations.

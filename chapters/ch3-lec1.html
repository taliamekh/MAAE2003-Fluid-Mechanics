Ch 3 — Bernoulli’s Equation

Lecture 3.1 · Bernoulli’s Equation I · Text Ch 3.5 — derivation, heads, EGL/HGL, stagnation pressure, limitations

The relation between pressure, velocity, and elevation of a flowing fluid was first stated verbally in Daniel Bernoulli’s textbook in 1738. Leonhard Euler later gave the complete derivation in 1755. This single equation ties together the three things we have studied so far — pressure (Ch 1–2), and now motion — into one conservation statement that holds along a streamline. It is arguably the most-used equation in all of introductory fluid mechanics, so it is worth understanding exactly where it comes from and, just as importantly, when it is allowed.

Deriving Bernoulli’s Equation

Bernoulli’s equation is nothing more than Newton’s second law applied to a small fluid element as it moves along a streamline. We follow one particle and add up the forces acting on it in the direction of motion (the s-direction, tangent to the streamline).

The forces acting on a fluid element of cross-section dA and length ds along a streamline. Pressure pushes on the two end faces; gravity pulls straight down. From the geometry, ds sinθ = dz.
Derivation — Newton’s 2nd law along a streamline

Step 1 — acceleration along the streamline. For steady flow the velocity of the particle depends on position s only, so the convective acceleration is

\[ a_s = \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{\partial V}{\partial s}\frac{ds}{dt} = V\frac{dV}{ds} \]

and the mass of the element is its density times its volume, \( m = \rho\,dA\,ds \). Newton’s second law in the s-direction gives

\[ \textstyle\sum F_s = m\,a_s = (\rho\,dA\,ds)\,V\frac{dV}{ds} \qquad (1) \]

Step 2 — add up the forces. Two kinds of force act along s: the pressure difference across the two end faces, and the component of the weight. Summing them,

\[ \textstyle\sum F_s = P\,dA - \left(P + \tfrac{dP}{ds}ds\right)dA - \rho g\,dA\,ds\,\sin\theta \] \[ \textstyle\sum F_s = -dP\,dA - \rho g\,dA\,ds\,\sin\theta \qquad (2) \]

Step 3 — equate (1) and (2) and divide through by the cross-sectional area dA:

\[ -dP\,dA - \rho g\,dA\,ds\,\sin\theta = (\rho\,dA\,ds)\,V\frac{dV}{ds} \] \[ -dP - \rho g\,ds\,\sin\theta = \rho\,V\,dV \]

Step 4 — use the geometry ds sinθ = dz (rise over the slope length), which turns the weight term into an elevation change:

\[ -dP - \rho g\,dz = \rho\,V\,dV \]

Step 5 — rearrange. Collecting all terms on one side gives the steady-flow Euler equation along a streamline:

\[ dP + \rho\,V\,dV + \rho g\,dz = 0 \qquad (3) \]

Step 6 — integrate for incompressible flow. With ρ constant, integrate each term along the streamline:

\[ \int dP + \int \rho\,V\,dV + \int \rho g\,dz = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; P + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2 + \rho g z = \text{constant} \]

Dividing by ρ gives the most common form. That is Bernoulli’s equation.

Bernoulli’s Equation (along a streamline)
\[ \frac{P}{\rho} + \frac{V^2}{2} + gz = \text{constant} \]

Equivalently, between any two points 1 and 2 on the same streamline:

\[ \frac{P_1}{\rho} + \frac{V_1^2}{2} + gz_1 = \frac{P_2}{\rho} + \frac{V_2^2}{2} + gz_2 \]
P — static pressure (Pa) V — flow speed (m/s) z — elevation above datum (m) ρ — density (kg/m³) g — gravity (9.81 m/s²)
⚠
Same streamline only
The constant is only guaranteed equal between two points if they lie on the same streamline. (It happens to be the same constant everywhere for irrotational flow, but that is a special case — do not assume it.)

Three ways to read the equation

As energy per unit mass

Bernoulli’s equation says the sum of three energies of a fluid particle is constant along a streamline during steady flow. Each term is an energy per unit mass (J/kg = m²/s²):

  • \( P/\rho \) — flow energy (the work done by pressure to push the fluid along)
  • \( V^2/2 \) — kinetic energy
  • \( gz \) — potential energy
As heads (height)

Divide every term by g. Now each term has units of length, because energy per unit weight is \( [\text{N}\!\cdot\!\text{m}/\text{N}] = [\text{m}] \). These heights are called heads:

\[ \frac{P}{\rho g} + \frac{V^2}{2g} + z = H = \text{constant} \]
P/ρg — pressure head (m) V²/2g — velocity head (m) z — elevation head (m) H — total head (m)
  • Pressure head \(P/\rho g\): the height of a fluid column that would produce the static pressure P.
  • Velocity head \(V^2/2g\): the height a fluid would need to fall (frictionless) to reach speed V.
  • Elevation head \(z\): the actual height above the datum — potential energy.
Bernoulli’s Equation (head form)
\[ \frac{P_1}{\rho g} + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} + z_1 = \frac{P_2}{\rho g} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} + z_2 = H \]

This is the form to reach for when a problem is described in terms of column heights, water levels, or manometer readings. Note \( \rho g = \gamma \), the specific weight from Ch 1.

Energy Grade Line (EGL) & Hydraulic Grade Line (HGL)

The head form has a useful graphical picture. Plot the total head and the pressure-plus-elevation head against position along the pipe:

EGL shows the height corresponding to all heads (total head H). HGL shows only the elevation + pressure heads — it sits one velocity head below the EGL. For ideal (loss-free) flow the EGL is horizontal. Here the pipe rises at constant diameter, so elevation head grows, pressure head shrinks, and the velocity head stays the same.
★
★ Reading the lines
EGL − HGL = velocity head \(V^2/2g\). If the diameter is constant, V is constant, so the two lines are parallel. Where the pipe narrows, V rises, the gap widens, and the HGL dips. The EGL only drops if there are friction losses (Ch 6).

Worked Example 1 — pressure between two points

Example 1 — estimate the pressure at Point 2

Water at 20°C flows along the streamlines shown. At Point 1 the gauge pressure, velocity, and elevation are 15.0 kPa, 1.0 m/s, and 5.02 m. At Point 2 the velocity and elevation are 0.5 m/s and 5.21 m. Estimate the pressure at Point 2.

Points 1 and 2 lie on the same streamline; the flow rises slightly and slows down from 1 to 2.
🧠 Thought process

Two points on one streamline, asking for a pressure given the other point’s state — this is the textbook Bernoulli setup. Water is incompressible and the rise is small with no pump/turbine between the points, so Bernoulli applies directly.

Approach: write Bernoulli in head form, solve for \(P_2\). I expect \(P_2\) to be a little lower than \(P_1\): the fluid rose (costs elevation head) but slowed down (returns a little velocity head), and the elevation change dominates here.

▸ Show full solution

Given: \(P_1 = 15.0\) kPa, \(V_1 = 1.0\) m/s, \(z_1 = 5.02\) m; \(V_2 = 0.5\) m/s, \(z_2 = 5.21\) m. Water at 20°C: \(\rho = 998.2\) kg/m³.

📋 Formulas Needed

  1. Specific weight — \(\gamma = \rho g\) → sheet #2
  2. Bernoulli (head form) — \(\frac{P_1}{\gamma} + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} + z_1 = \frac{P_2}{\gamma} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} + z_2\) → sheet #15
1
Specific weight of water
\[ \gamma = \rho g = (998.2)(9.807) = 9789\ \text{N/m}^3 = 9.789\ \text{kN/m}^3 \]
2
Write Bernoulli and isolate P₂
\[ \frac{15.0}{9.789} + \frac{(1.0)^2}{2(9.807)} + 5.02 = \frac{P_2}{9.789} + \frac{(0.5)^2}{2(9.807)} + 5.21 \]

Left side: \(1.5323 + 0.0510 + 5.02 = 6.6033\) m. Right-side known terms: \(0.0127 + 5.21 = 5.2227\) m.

3
Solve for the pressure head, then P₂
\[ \frac{P_2}{9.789} = 6.6033 - 5.2227 = 1.3806\ \text{m} \] \[ P_2 = (1.3806)(9.789) = 13.5\ \text{kPa} \]
✓
Answer
\(P_2 \approx 13.5\) kPa (gauge). As predicted, slightly below \(P_1\): the 0.19 m rise costs more head than the modest slow-down returns.
★
★ Tips
Keep pressures in kPa and divide by γ in kN/m³ so the units cancel to metres. Both pressures are gauge here — that is fine because Bernoulli only ever cares about pressure differences, so a common reference cancels.

When Bernoulli is — and is not — allowed

Every term we dropped during the derivation is an assumption. Use Bernoulli only when all of these hold:

★ Limitations — the six conditions
  • Steady flow — properties at a point do not change with time.
  • Frictionless (inviscid) flow — we never included a viscous shear term.
  • No shaft work — no pump, turbine, fan, or impeller between the two points (those add or remove energy).
  • Incompressible — ρ was taken constant to do the integral. Liquids always; gases only at low speed.
  • No heat transfer — no significant heating or temperature change.
  • Along a single streamline — the derivation followed one particle.
Friction, heat transfer, and components that disturb the streamlined structure of the flow all break Bernoulli. Near solid walls (boundary layers) and downstream of bodies (wakes) friction always matters — Bernoulli is reliable only outside those regions.

Static, dynamic, and stagnation pressure

Multiply the speed term back up so every term is a pressure: \( P + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2 + \rho g z = \text{constant} \). At a fixed elevation the three pressure ideas are:

The three pressures
  • Static pressure \(P\) — the actual thermodynamic pressure (normal stress) in the moving fluid.
  • Dynamic pressure \(\tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2\) — the pressure associated with the fluid’s motion.
  • Stagnation pressure \(P_0\) — the static pressure at a point where the flow has been brought completely to rest (\(V=0\)).

At the same elevation, setting \(V_2 = 0\) at the stagnation point gives

\[ P_0 = P + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2 \]

so stagnation pressure = static pressure + dynamic pressure. This is the key to flow-rate measurement in Lecture 3.2.

P₀ — stagnation pressure (Pa) P — static pressure (Pa) ½ρV² — dynamic pressure (Pa)
At the leading edge of an airfoil the oncoming flow is brought to rest — the pressure there is the stagnation pressure \(P_0\).

Simplifying assumptions for tanks & reservoirs

★ Two cancellations that make problems easy
  • A point exposed to the atmosphere has gauge pressure \(P = 0\), so its \(P/\rho g\) term drops out.
  • The free surface of a large tank/reservoir falls so slowly that \(V \approx 0\), so its velocity head drops out.

Worked Example 2 — draining tank (Torricelli)

Example 2 — maximum outlet velocity

A large tank open to the atmosphere is filled with water to a height of 5 m above a smooth, rounded outlet tap near the bottom. The tap is opened. Determine the maximum velocity of the water at the outlet.

Point 1 at the free surface; Point 2 at the outlet. Both are exposed to atmosphere, so both pressures cancel.
🧠 Thought process

“Maximum” velocity signals the ideal, frictionless case — exactly Bernoulli. Choose Point 1 at the surface and Point 2 at the outlet, on one streamline.

Both points touch the atmosphere, so \(P_1 = P_2 = P_{atm}\) and the pressure terms cancel. The surface barely moves (\(V_1\approx 0\)). Put the datum at the outlet (\(z_2 = 0\)). Almost everything drops out, leaving elevation head = velocity head.

▸ Show full solution

Assumptions: incompressible flow; the tank drains slowly enough to be quasi-steady; irreversible losses in the smooth, rounded tap are neglected.

Point 1 (free surface): \(P_1 = P_{atm}\), \(V_1 = 0\), \(z_1 = 5\) m. Point 2 (outlet): \(P_2 = P_{atm}\), \(z_2 = 0\).

📋 Formulas Needed

  1. Bernoulli (head form) — \(\frac{P_1}{\gamma}+\frac{V_1^2}{2g}+z_1=\frac{P_2}{\gamma}+\frac{V_2^2}{2g}+z_2\) → sheet #15
1
Cancel the dropped terms

\(P_1=P_2\) cancel; \(V_1=0\); \(z_2=0\). Bernoulli collapses to

\[ z_1 = \frac{V_2^2}{2g} \]
2
Solve for the outlet velocity
\[ V_2 = \sqrt{2 g z_1} = \sqrt{2(9.81)(5)} \]
✓
Answer
\(V_2 = 9.9\) m/s. This is Torricelli’s result: the jet leaves as fast as if the water had free-fallen the full 5 m.
⚠
Common trap
The result is independent of fluid density and of the tank’s cross-section — only the height matters. It is the maximum velocity because friction in a real tap would make the actual value a little lower.

Bernoulli = Newton’s 2nd law integrated along a streamline for steady, frictionless, incompressible flow with no shaft work or heat transfer: \( \frac{P}{\rho}+\frac{V^2}{2}+gz \) is constant.

Short Answer Q1 — Parallel Plates & Viscosity

Problem Statement

Three parallel plates: the top plate moves at velocity \(V\), the bottom plate is fixed, and the middle plate is free to move at velocity \(V_1\). The gap between the top and bottom plates is \(b\), with the middle plate at \(0.25b\) from the bottom. The fluid above the middle plate has viscosity \(2\mu\) and below has viscosity \(\mu\). Determine \(V_1/V\).

🧠 Thought Process

This is a Couette flow / viscous shear equilibrium problem. The middle plate is free (no external force), so the net shear on it is zero — shear stress from above must equal shear stress from below.

Key: different viscosities (\(2\mu\) above, \(\mu\) below) and different gap widths (\(0.75b\) above, \(0.25b\) below). Linear velocity profiles in each gap.

📋 Formulas Needed

  1. Newton's Law of Viscosity: \(\tau = \mu \dfrac{du}{dy}\)
  2. Couette flow: Linear velocity profile \(\Rightarrow du/dy = \Delta V / \Delta y\)
  3. Equilibrium on free plate: \(\tau_{\text{upper}} = \tau_{\text{lower}}\)
Setup

Given: Top plate at \(V\), bottom plate fixed, middle plate at \(V_1\). Upper gap \(= 0.75b\) with viscosity \(2\mu\). Lower gap \(= 0.25b\) with viscosity \(\mu\).

Find: \(V_1/V\)

Solution
Step 1 — Shear stress in the upper gap

The top plate moves at \(V\) and the middle plate at \(V_1\). The velocity gradient is:

\[\tau_{\text{upper}} = 2\mu \cdot \frac{V - V_1}{0.75b}\]
Step 2 — Shear stress in the lower gap

The middle plate moves at \(V_1\) and the bottom plate is fixed:

\[\tau_{\text{lower}} = \mu \cdot \frac{V_1 - 0}{0.25b} = \mu \cdot \frac{V_1}{0.25b}\]
Step 3 — Force balance on the free plate

Since the middle plate has no external force, \(\tau_{\text{upper}} = \tau_{\text{lower}}\):

\[2\mu \cdot \frac{V - V_1}{0.75b} = \mu \cdot \frac{V_1}{0.25b}\]

Cancel \(\mu\) and \(b\):

\[\frac{2(V - V_1)}{0.75} = \frac{V_1}{0.25}\] \[2(V - V_1) \times \frac{1}{0.75} = V_1 \times \frac{1}{0.25}\] \[\frac{2(V - V_1)}{3/4} = \frac{V_1}{1/4}\] \[\frac{8(V - V_1)}{3} = 4V_1\] \[8V - 8V_1 = 12V_1\] \[8V = 20V_1\]
\[\boxed{\frac{V_1}{V} = \frac{8}{20} = \frac{2}{5}}\]
💡 Exam Tip: Always cancel \(\mu\) and \(b\) early — the ratio \(V_1/V\) depends only on the viscosity ratio and gap ratio, not on actual values.
🔗 Concept Bridge: This is the same shear-equilibrium principle behind viscometers and bearing lubrication (Ch 1).

Short Answer Q2 — Spinning U-Tube (Rigid-Body Rotation)

Problem Statement

A spinning glass U-tube is filled with fluid. The left arm is 0.1 m from the rotation axis and reaches a height of 0.18 m. The right arm is 0.05 m from the axis and reaches 0.1 m. Determine the angular velocity \(\omega\).

🧠 Thought Process

This is a rigid-body rotation problem (Ch 2). When a fluid spins at constant \(\omega\), the free surface forms a paraboloid. The height at any radius \(r\) is given by the rigid-body rotation equation.

Strategy: write the height equation at both arms and subtract to eliminate the unknown base height.

📋 Formulas Needed

  1. Rigid-body rotation free surface: \(z = z_0 + \dfrac{\omega^2 r^2}{2g}\)
Setup

Given: Left arm: \(r_L = 0.1\) m, \(z_L = 0.18\) m. Right arm: \(r_R = 0.05\) m, \(z_R = 0.1\) m. \(g = 9.81\) m/s\(^2\).

Find: \(\omega\)

Solution
Step 1 — Write the height equation at each arm
\[z_L = z_0 + \frac{\omega^2 r_L^2}{2g} \qquad z_R = z_0 + \frac{\omega^2 r_R^2}{2g}\]
Step 2 — Subtract to eliminate \(z_0\)
\[z_L - z_R = \frac{\omega^2}{2g}\left(r_L^2 - r_R^2\right)\] \[0.18 - 0.10 = \frac{\omega^2}{2(9.81)}\left(0.1^2 - 0.05^2\right)\] \[0.08 = \frac{\omega^2}{19.62}\left(0.01 - 0.0025\right) = \frac{\omega^2 \times 0.0075}{19.62}\]
Step 3 — Solve for \(\omega\)
\[\omega^2 = \frac{0.08 \times 19.62}{0.0075} = \frac{1.5696}{0.0075} = 209.28\]
\[\boxed{\omega = \sqrt{209.28} \approx 14.47 \text{ rad/s}}\]
💡 Exam Tip: Always subtract the two height equations to eliminate \(z_0\). Don't try to find \(z_0\) separately.
🔗 Concept Bridge: This is the same paraboloid equation used for spinning buckets and centrifugal separation (Ch 2).

Short Answer Q3 — Hydraulic Jack with Lever

Problem Statement

A hydraulic jack supports a 2000 lb weight on a 3-inch diameter piston. The small piston has a 1-inch diameter. A lever with a 1-inch effort arm and 15-inch load arm connects to the small piston. Find the force \(F\) required at the end of the lever.

🧠 Thought Process

Two amplification stages: (1) Pascal's principle — the hydraulic area ratio amplifies force between pistons, and (2) the lever provides additional mechanical advantage.

Work backward from the 2000 lb load through both stages.

Solution
Step 1 — Hydraulic area ratio (Pascal's principle)

Pressure is equal throughout the hydraulic fluid:

\[P = \frac{W}{A_{\text{large}}} = \frac{F_{\text{small}}}{A_{\text{small}}} \quad \Rightarrow \quad F_{\text{small}} = W \times \frac{A_{\text{small}}}{A_{\text{large}}}\] \[\frac{A_{\text{small}}}{A_{\text{large}}} = \frac{d_{\text{small}}^2}{d_{\text{large}}^2} = \frac{1^2}{3^2} = \frac{1}{9}\] \[F_{\text{small}} = 2000 \times \frac{1}{9} = 222.2 \text{ lb}\]
Step 2 — Lever mechanical advantage

The lever pivots at the fulcrum. Taking moments about the pivot, the effort arm is \((1 + 15) = 16\) inches and the load arm is \(1\) inch:

\[F \times 16 = F_{\text{small}} \times 1\] \[F = \frac{222.2}{16}\]
\[\boxed{F = 13.89 \text{ lb}}\]
Total mechanical advantage: Area ratio \(\times\) lever ratio \(= 9 \times 16 = 144\). Check: \(2000/144 = 13.89\) lb ✔
💡 Exam Tip: Use diameter ratio squared for the area ratio — no need to compute actual areas with \(\pi\).
🔗 Concept Bridge: Pascal's principle — pressure applied anywhere in a confined fluid is transmitted equally (Ch 1).

Long Answer Q1 — Water Over a Ramp (Bernoulli + Momentum)

Problem Statement

Water leaves a basin and slides down a ramp. The upstream depth is 5 m and the downstream depth is 0.7 m. The setup is 10 m wide (into the screen). \(\rho = 1000\) kg/m\(^3\). Find the volume flow rate \(Q\) and the net horizontal force the ramp must exert on the water.

🧠 Thought Process

Two-step problem: (1) Use Bernoulli's equation along the free surface from station 1 (upstream) to station 2 (downstream) to find velocities. (2) Use the linear momentum equation on a control volume to find the net force.

Key assumption: the upstream basin is large, so \(V_1 \approx 0\). The free surface is at atmospheric pressure on both sides.

📋 Formulas Needed

  1. Bernoulli: \(P_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V_1^2 + \rho g z_1 = P_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V_2^2 + \rho g z_2\)
  2. Continuity: \(Q = V_1 A_1 = V_2 A_2\)
  3. Linear momentum: \(\sum F = \dot{m}(V_{\text{out}} - V_{\text{in}})\)
  4. Hydrostatic force on a cross-section: \(F_{\text{pressure}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho g h^2 w\)
Setup

Given: \(h_1 = 5\) m, \(h_2 = 0.7\) m, width \(w = 10\) m, \(\rho = 1000\) kg/m\(^3\), \(V_1 \approx 0\) (large basin).

Find: \(Q\) and \(F_{\text{net}}\) (horizontal force on the water from the ramp).

Solution
Part (a) — Flow Rate \(Q\)
Step 1 — Bernoulli from surface 1 to surface 2

Both surfaces are at atmospheric pressure (\(P_1 = P_2 = P_{\text{atm}}\)), and \(V_1 \approx 0\). Taking the bottom of the ramp as the datum (\(z_2 = 0\), \(z_1 = h_1 = 5\) m):

\[\cancel{P_{\text{atm}}} + 0 + \rho g h_1 = \cancel{P_{\text{atm}}} + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V_2^2 + \rho g h_2\] \[V_2 = \sqrt{2g(h_1 - h_2)} = \sqrt{2(9.81)(5 - 0.7)} = \sqrt{84.35}\] \[V_2 = 9.184 \text{ m/s}\]
Step 2 — Flow rate
\[Q = V_2 \times A_2 = V_2 \times h_2 \times w = 9.184 \times 0.7 \times 10\]
\[\boxed{Q = 64.3 \text{ m}^3\text{/s}}\]
Part (b) — Net Force on the Water
Step 3 — Pressure forces on the control volume

The hydrostatic pressure distribution on each vertical cross-section creates a net force. Using gage pressure (atmospheric cancels):

\[F_{p1} = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho g h_1^2 w = \tfrac{1}{2}(1000)(9.81)(5)^2(10) = 1\,226\,250 \text{ N}\] \[F_{p2} = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho g h_2^2 w = \tfrac{1}{2}(1000)(9.81)(0.7)^2(10) = 24\,017 \text{ N}\]
Step 4 — Momentum equation (horizontal)

Apply the linear momentum equation in the horizontal direction for a CV enclosing the ramp. Let \(F_R\) be the force of the ramp on the water (positive to the right = downstream direction):

\[F_{p1} - F_{p2} + F_R = \dot{m}(V_2 - V_1)\]

where \(\dot{m} = \rho Q = 1000 \times 64.3 = 64\,300\) kg/s and \(V_1 \approx 0\):

\[F_R = \rho Q V_2 - F_{p1} + F_{p2}\] \[F_R = 64\,300 \times 9.184 - 1\,226\,250 + 24\,017\] \[F_R = 590\,523 - 1\,226\,250 + 24\,017 = -611\,710 \text{ N}\]

The negative sign means the ramp pushes the water upstream (to the left), which makes physical sense — the ramp resists the flow. The magnitude:

\[\boxed{F_{\text{net}} \approx 566 \text{ kN (upstream)}}\]
Sanity check: The upstream hydrostatic force (1226 kN) far exceeds the momentum flux (591 kN) and downstream pressure (24 kN). The ramp must push back — confirmed by the negative sign. The net force on the ramp (by Newton's 3rd law) is 566 kN downstream.
💡 Exam Tip: Always state your CV boundaries, datum, and sign convention before writing the momentum equation. Check the sign of your answer against physical intuition.
🔗 Concept Bridge: This combines Bernoulli (Ch 3) with Linear Momentum (Ch 4) — the classic two-step open-channel flow problem.

Long Answer Q2 — Quarter-Circle Gate (Hydrostatic Forces on Curved Surface)

Problem Statement

Determine the resultant hydrostatic force on the quarter-circle gate and the tension in rope BC. The gate has radius \(R = 6\) ft and width \(w = 4\) ft (into screen). Hinged at A (bottom), held by horizontal rope BC at the top. Water fills to the top of the gate (level with B).

🧠 Thought Process

This is a hydrostatic forces on a curved surface problem (Ch 2). For curved surfaces, we decompose the force into horizontal and vertical components:

\(F_H\) = force on the vertical projection of the curve — a flat rectangle of height \(R\) and width \(w\).

\(F_V\) = weight of the fluid volume between the curved surface and the free surface.

For the tension: use the key property that the resultant on a circular arc passes through the centre of curvature. This massively simplifies the moment equation.

📋 Formulas Needed

  1. Horizontal force: \(F_H = \gamma\,\bar{y}_c\,A_{\text{proj}}\) Ch 2
  2. Vertical force: \(F_V = \gamma \times V_{\text{fluid}}\) Ch 2
  3. Resultant: \(F_R = \sqrt{F_H^2 + F_V^2}\)
  4. Circular surface property: Resultant passes through centre of curvature
Setup

Given: Quarter-circle gate, \(R = 6\) ft, \(w = 4\) ft. Water surface at B (top of gate). Hinge at A (bottom). \(\gamma = 62.4\) lb/ft\(^3\).

Find: \(F_R\) and rope tension \(T\).

Solution
Step 1 — Horizontal component \(F_H\)

The vertical projection is a rectangle of height \(R = 6\) ft and width \(w = 4\) ft. The centroid is at \(\bar{y}_c = R/2 = 3\) ft below the surface:

\[F_H = \gamma\,\bar{y}_c\,A_{\text{proj}} = 62.4 \times 3 \times (6 \times 4) = 62.4 \times 3 \times 24\] \[F_H = 4{,}492.8 \text{ lb}\]
Step 2 — Vertical component \(F_V\)

\(F_V\) equals the weight of the fluid in the volume between the curved gate and the free surface. This volume is the bounding \(R \times R\) square minus the quarter-circle, times the width:

\[V_{\text{fluid}} = \left(R^2 - \frac{\pi R^2}{4}\right)w = R^2\!\left(1 - \frac{\pi}{4}\right)w = 36(0.2146)(4) = 30.90 \text{ ft}^3\] \[F_V = \gamma \times V_{\text{fluid}} = 62.4 \times 30.90 = 1{,}928 \text{ lb}\]
Step 3 — Resultant force
\[F_R = \sqrt{F_H^2 + F_V^2} = \sqrt{4{,}493^2 + 1{,}928^2} = \sqrt{20{,}186{,}000 + 3{,}717{,}000}\]
\[\boxed{F_R \approx 4{,}889 \text{ lb}}\]
Step 4 — Tension in rope BC (moment about A)

Key insight: For a circular surface, the hydrostatic resultant passes through the centre of curvature. For this quarter-circle, the centre of curvature is at the corner diagonally opposite to A — call it point D (bottom-left corner of the bounding square). This is \(R = 6\) ft directly to the left of A, at the same height as A.

Taking moments about hinge A:

  • \(F_H\) passes through D at the same height as A \(\Rightarrow\) zero moment arm about A!
  • \(F_V\) passes through D, which is \(R = 6\) ft horizontally from A \(\Rightarrow\) moment arm \(= R\).
  • \(T\) acts horizontally at B, which is \(R = 6\) ft above A \(\Rightarrow\) moment arm \(= R\).
\[\sum M_A = 0: \quad T \times R = F_V \times R\] \[T = F_V\]
\[\boxed{T = F_V = 1{,}928 \text{ lb}}\]
Why does \(T = F_V\)? Because the hydrostatic resultant passes through the centre of curvature D. Since D is at the same elevation as hinge A, the horizontal component \(F_H\) contributes zero moment about A. Only the vertical component creates a moment, and its moment arm equals \(R\) — the same as the rope's. The \(R\) cancels, giving \(T = F_V\) directly.
💡 Exam Tip: For any circular gate, the resultant passes through the centre of curvature. Find that point first — it can eliminate an entire component from the moment equation.
🔗 Concept Bridge: Same decomposition method as for any curved submerged surface (Ch 2), plus the circular-arc shortcut for moments.

Long Answer Q3 — Pipe Flow with Pump (Energy Equation + Moody Chart)

Problem Statement

A pump delivers 3.7 kW to the fluid in a piping system. The pipe has diameter \(d = 75\) mm, total length \(L = 30\) m, roughness \(\varepsilon = 0.045\) mm, and total minor loss coefficient \(\Sigma K = 1.9\). The discharge is 8 m above the reservoir surface. Use \(\rho = 999\) kg/m\(^3\), \(\mu = 1.002 \times 10^{-3}\) Pa\(\cdot\)s. Find \(V\) and \(Q\) by iterating the friction factor with the Moody chart.

🧠 Thought Process

This is an energy equation + iterative friction factor problem (Ch 5). The pump adds head to the fluid, which must overcome elevation change plus all losses (major + minor). Since \(V\) appears in both the head loss and the pump head expression, we get a nonlinear equation that requires iteration.

Strategy: (1) Write the energy equation, (2) Express pump head in terms of \(V\), (3) Guess \(f\), solve for \(V\), compute Re, look up new \(f\) on Moody chart, repeat until \(f\) converges.

📋 Formulas Needed

  1. Energy equation: \(\dfrac{P_1}{\rho g} + \dfrac{V_1^2}{2g} + z_1 + h_p = \dfrac{P_2}{\rho g} + \dfrac{V_2^2}{2g} + z_2 + h_L\)
  2. Pump head: \(h_p = \dfrac{\dot{W}_p}{\rho g Q} = \dfrac{\dot{W}_p}{\rho g A V}\)
  3. Head loss: \(h_L = \left(f\dfrac{L}{d} + \Sigma K\right)\dfrac{V^2}{2g}\)
  4. Reynolds number: \(\text{Re} = \dfrac{\rho V d}{\mu}\)
  5. Moody chart / Colebrook: \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{f}} = -2\log\!\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon/d}{3.7} + \dfrac{2.51}{\text{Re}\sqrt{f}}\right)\)
Setup

Given: \(\dot{W}_p = 3700\) W, \(d = 0.075\) m, \(L = 30\) m, \(\varepsilon = 0.000045\) m, \(\Sigma K = 1.9\), \(\Delta z = 8\) m, \(\rho = 999\) kg/m\(^3\), \(\mu = 1.002 \times 10^{-3}\) Pa·s.

Pipe area: \(A = \frac{\pi}{4}(0.075)^2 = 4.418 \times 10^{-3}\) m\(^2\).

Relative roughness: \(\varepsilon/d = 0.045/75 = 0.0006\).

Find: \(V\) and \(Q\).

Solution
Step 1 — Energy equation from reservoir surface (1) to pipe exit (2)

Take point 1 at the reservoir surface (\(P_1 = 0\) gage, \(V_1 \approx 0\), \(z_1 = 0\)) and point 2 at the pipe exit (\(P_2 = 0\) gage, \(V_2 = V\), \(z_2 = 8\) m):

\[0 + 0 + 0 + h_p = 0 + \frac{V^2}{2g} + 8 + h_L\] \[h_p = 8 + \frac{V^2}{2g} + \left(f\frac{L}{d} + \Sigma K\right)\frac{V^2}{2g}\] \[h_p = 8 + \frac{V^2}{2g}\left(1 + f\frac{30}{0.075} + 1.9\right) = 8 + \frac{V^2}{2g}(2.9 + 400f)\]
Step 2 — Express pump head in terms of \(V\)
\[h_p = \frac{\dot{W}_p}{\rho g A V} = \frac{3700}{999 \times 9.81 \times 4.418 \times 10^{-3} \times V} = \frac{3700}{43.30\,V} = \frac{85.45}{V}\]
Step 3 — Combined equation
\[\frac{85.45}{V} = 8 + \frac{V^2}{19.62}(2.9 + 400f)\]

This is a cubic in \(V\) for a given \(f\). We solve iteratively.

Step 4 — Iterative solution
Iteration Guess \(f\) \(V\) (m/s) Re \(\varepsilon/d\) New \(f\) (Moody)
1 0.020 4.67 349,000 0.0006 0.0195
2 0.0195 4.70 351,000 0.0006 0.0195
✔ Converged \(f \approx 0.0195\), \(V \approx 4.70\) m/s
Iteration walkthrough (Iteration 1)

Guess \(f = 0.020\). The energy equation becomes:

\[\frac{85.45}{V} = 8 + \frac{V^2}{19.62}(2.9 + 400 \times 0.020) = 8 + \frac{V^2}{19.62}(10.9)\] \[\frac{85.45}{V} = 8 + 0.5556\,V^2\]

Multiply through by \(V\): \(85.45 = 8V + 0.5556V^3\). Solving numerically (or by trial): \(V \approx 4.67\) m/s.

Check: \(\text{Re} = \frac{999 \times 4.67 \times 0.075}{1.002 \times 10^{-3}} \approx 349{,}000\). On the Moody chart at \(\text{Re} = 349{,}000\) and \(\varepsilon/d = 0.0006\): \(f \approx 0.0195\). Close to our guess — one more iteration confirms convergence.

Step 5 — Flow rate
\[Q = AV = 4.418 \times 10^{-3} \times 4.70\]
\[\boxed{V \approx 4.70 \text{ m/s}, \quad Q \approx 0.021 \text{ m}^3\text{/s}}\]
Convergence note: Only 2 iterations needed because \(f\) changes slowly at high Re and moderate roughness. On the exam, if your first guess is in the turbulent regime, you'll typically converge in 2–3 iterations.
💡 Exam Tip: Always start with \(f \approx 0.02\) for turbulent flow as your first guess. Show every iteration step clearly — partial marks are awarded for the process even if you don't fully converge.
🔗 Concept Bridge: Energy equation with pump work (Ch 5), Darcy-Weisbach major losses + minor losses (Ch 5), Moody chart (Ch 5).

🎯 Exam Day Reminders

Short answers (~5 min each): These test whether you can identify the correct principle and execute a quick calculation. Set up the equation, plug in, box the answer. Don't over-explain.

Long answers (~20 min each): These are multi-step problems. Start by listing knowns/unknowns and identifying which equations you need. Show every substitution — partial marks come from the process. Draw and label your control volume or free-body diagram.

Units: Convert everything to consistent units (SI or BG) before substituting. The most common exam error is mixing mm with m, or inches with feet.

Sanity checks: After solving, verify: Is the sign physically reasonable? Are the units correct? Is the magnitude in the right ballpark?

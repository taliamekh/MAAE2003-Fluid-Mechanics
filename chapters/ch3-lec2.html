Ch 3 — Bernoulli’s Equation

Lecture 3.2 · Bernoulli’s Equation II · Text Ch 3.5 — flow-rate measurement

Lecture 3.1 ended on a single, powerful idea: bring a moving fluid to rest and its pressure climbs to the stagnation pressure \(P_0 = P + \tfrac12\rho V^2\). That one relationship is the engine behind almost every practical way of measuring how fast a fluid moves or how much of it flows through a pipe. This lecture applies Bernoulli to four classic flow-measurement devices — the Pitot tube, the Pitot-static tube, the Venturi meter, the orifice meter, and the bell-mouth inlet — and shows how a measured pressure difference becomes a velocity or a volumetric flow rate \(Q\).

Stagnation pressure, revisited

Take Bernoulli between a point (1) in the free stream and a point (2) where the flow is brought completely to rest. If the two points are at the same elevation (\(z_1 = z_2\)) and \(V_2 = 0\):

A streamline that ends on the nose of a body is decelerated to rest. The pressure recovered there is the stagnation pressure \(P_0\) — the sum of the static and dynamic pressures of the free stream.
Derivation — stagnation pressure from Bernoulli

Starting from the pressure form along the streamline 1→2:

\[ P_1 + \tfrac12\rho V_1^2 + \rho g z_1 = P_2 + \tfrac12\rho V_2^2 + \rho g z_2 \]

Apply \(z_1 = z_2\) (same elevation) and \(V_2 = 0\) (brought to rest). Writing \(V_1 = V\) and \(P_1 = P\) for the free stream, and recognising \(P_2 = P_0\):

\[ P + \tfrac12\rho V^2 = P_0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; P_0 = P + \tfrac12\rho V^2 \]

So the stagnation pressure is the static pressure plus the dynamic pressure \(\tfrac12\rho V^2\). Rearranged, it gives the velocity directly from a pressure difference — which is exactly what the next devices measure.

\[ V = \sqrt{\frac{2\,(P_0 - P)}{\rho}} \]
★
★ Four devices, one idea
Each meter measures a pressure difference and converts it to flow using Bernoulli. Pitot / Pitot-static → local velocity from stagnation − static. Venturi & orifice → flow rate from the pressure drop across an area change, via Bernoulli + continuity. Bell-mouth inlet → a smooth intake that doubles as its own flow meter.

Pitot tube

A Pitot tube is a small right-angled (90° bend) tube with its open mouth pointed directly into the flow, so the fluid is brought to rest at the opening. It therefore senses the stagnation pressure, and paired with a separate measurement of static pressure it gives one of the most accurate point-velocity measurements available.

The piezometer reads the static pressure head \(P_1/\rho g\); the Pitot tube reads the stagnation head \(P_0/\rho g\). Their difference is exactly the velocity head \(V^2/2g\).
Derivation — Pitot velocity

Bernoulli from the free stream (1) to the Pitot mouth (2), with \(z_1 = z_2\) and \(V_2 = 0\):

\[ P_1 + \tfrac12\rho V_1^2 = P_2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{V_1^2}{2} = \frac{P_2 - P_1}{\rho} \]

Since the mouth is a stagnation point, \(P_2 = P_0\), so

\[ V_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2\,(P_0 - P_1)}{\rho}} \]

Everything on the right is measured: \(P_0\) by the Pitot tube, \(P_1\) by the piezometer.

Pitot velocity
\[ V_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2\,(P_0 - P_1)}{\rho}} \]
V₁ — local flow speed (m/s) P₀ — stagnation pressure (Pa) P₁ — static pressure (Pa) ρ — fluid density (kg/m³)

When the two pressures are read as column heights, \(P_0 - P_1 = \rho g\,\Delta h\), and the formula reduces to the tidy \(V_1 = \sqrt{2 g\,\Delta h}\).

Pitot-static tube

A Pitot-static probe combines both measurements in a single instrument: a hole at the nose senses the stagnation pressure, while several small holes around the circumference sense the static pressure. The velocity calculation is identical to the Pitot tube — only the way the two pressures are picked off differs.

One probe, two pressures: the nose hole gives \(P_0\) and the circumferential holes give \(P_1\). Substitute into the same \(V_1 = \sqrt{2(P_0 - P_1)/\rho}\).

Worked Example 3 — velocity from a Pitot tube

Example 3 — velocity at the centre of a pipe

A piezometer and a Pitot tube are tapped into a horizontal water pipe to read the static and stagnation pressures. The water columns rise to the heights shown: \(h_1 = 3\) cm, \(h_2 = 7\) cm, and the extra rise in the Pitot tube is \(h_3 = 12\) cm. Determine the velocity at the centre of the pipe.

Static head reaches \(h_1 + h_2\); the stagnation column climbs an extra \(h_3\) higher. That extra height is the velocity head.
🧠 Thought process

A piezometer reads static pressure and a Pitot reads stagnation pressure, on one horizontal streamline — the textbook Pitot setup. The pipe is horizontal so \(z_1 = z_2\), and the Pitot mouth is a stagnation point so \(V_2 = 0\).

Each column height converts to a pressure by \(P = \rho g h\). The static column stands at \(h_1 + h_2\); the stagnation column stands at \(h_1 + h_2 + h_3\). When I subtract, everything cancels except \(h_3\), so the velocity should depend on \(h_3\) alone.

▸ Show full solution

Assumptions: steady, incompressible flow; points 1 and 2 are close enough that irreversible losses between them are negligible.

Point 1 (directly under the piezometer): \(P_1 = \rho g(h_1 + h_2)\). Point 2 (Pitot mouth, stagnation): \(P_2 = P_0 = \rho g(h_1 + h_2 + h_3)\).

📋 Formulas Needed

  1. Stagnation pressure — \(P_0 = P + \tfrac12\rho V^2\) → sheet #17
  2. Pitot velocity — \(V_1 = \sqrt{2(P_0 - P_1)/\rho}\) → sheet #19
  3. Hydrostatic head — \(P = \rho g h\) → sheet #10
1
Write each pressure as a column height
\[ P_1 = \rho g(h_1 + h_2), \qquad P_2 = P_0 = \rho g(h_1 + h_2 + h_3) \]
2
Subtract — only h₃ survives
\[ V_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2\,[\rho g(h_1+h_2+h_3) - \rho g(h_1+h_2)]}{\rho}} = \sqrt{2 g\,h_3} \]
3
Substitute numbers
\[ V_1 = \sqrt{2(9.81)(0.12)} = \sqrt{2.354} \]
✓
Answer
\(V_1 = 1.53\) m/s. The static heads \(h_1\) and \(h_2\) drop out entirely — only the Pitot’s extra rise \(h_3\) sets the velocity.
⚠
Common trap
Use only the difference in the columns, \(h_3\). It is tempting to plug in the full \(h_1+h_2+h_3\); but the static head common to both taps always cancels. Convert cm to m before substituting.

Concept bridge: stagnation pressure (sheet #17) + Pitot velocity (sheet #19), with each head from \(P=\rho g h\) (sheet #10).

Venturi meter

A Venturi meter is a smooth convergent–divergent section of pipe. The flow speeds up through the throat, so by Bernoulli the pressure there drops; measuring that pressure drop gives the flow rate. It is the most efficient flow-measurement device (very little permanent pressure loss) but it is expensive to build.

The throat (section 2) is narrower, so \(V_2\) > \(V_1\) and \(P_2\) < \(P_1\). The manometer deflection \(h\) measures the pressure drop \(P_1 - P_2\).
Derivation — flow rate from a Venturi

Step 1 — Bernoulli between sections 1 and 2 with \(z_1 = z_2\):

\[ P_1 + \tfrac12\rho V_1^2 = P_2 + \tfrac12\rho V_2^2 \;\Rightarrow\; P_1 - P_2 = \tfrac12\rho\,(V_2^2 - V_1^2) \]

Step 2 — continuity for incompressible flow ties the two velocities together:

\[ Q = A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2 \;\Rightarrow\; V_1 = \frac{A_2}{A_1}V_2 \]

Step 3 — eliminate \(V_1\) and solve for the throat velocity:

\[ V_2 = \sqrt{\frac{2\,(P_1 - P_2)/\rho}{1 - (A_2/A_1)^2}} \]

Step 4 — multiply by the throat area to get the ideal flow rate, then correct it. The real flow rate is a little lower because of small friction and non-uniformity, captured by the Venturi coefficient \(C_v = Q_{actual}/Q_{ideal}\):

\[ Q = C_v A_2\sqrt{\frac{2\,(P_1 - P_2)/\rho}{1 - (A_2/A_1)^2}} \]
Venturi flow rate
\[ Q = C_v A_2\sqrt{\dfrac{2\,(P_1 - P_2)}{\rho\left(1 - (A_2/A_1)^2\right)}} \]
Q — volumetric flow rate (m³/s) Cᵛ — Venturi coefficient (−) A₁ — inlet area (m²) A₂ — throat area (m²) P₁−P₂ — pressure drop (Pa) ρ — density (kg/m³)

Set \(C_v = 1\) for the ideal (frictionless) estimate. Areas come from diameters via \(A = \tfrac{\pi}{4}d^2\), so the area ratio can be written \((A_2/A_1)^2 = (d_2/d_1)^4\).

Worked Example 4 — air flow through a Venturi

Example 4 — flow rate of air (BG units)

Air flows through a Venturi meter that is 2.6 in in diameter at the inlet (section 1) and 1.8 in at the throat (section 2). The pressures are 12.2 psia at the inlet and 11.8 psia at the throat. Take the air density as 0.075 lbm/ft³. Determine the flow rate of air.

Inlet 2.6 in at 12.2 psia; throat 1.8 in at 11.8 psia. The small 0.4 psi drop sets the flow rate.
🧠 Thought process

This is the Venturi flow-rate formula applied directly. Nothing about a Venturi coefficient is given, so treat the flow as ideal (\(C_v = 1\)).

The real work here is unit bookkeeping in the British Gravitational system. Diameters are in inches (÷12 to feet), pressures in psi (×144 to lbf/ft²), and density is in lbm, which must be reconciled with the force unit lbf using \(g_c = 32.2\) lbm·ft/(lbf·s²). I will write \((A_2/A_1)^2 = (d_2/d_1)^4\) so I only need the diameter ratio, not the absolute areas, inside the bracket.

▸ Show full solution

Given: \(d_1 = 2.6\) in, \(d_2 = 1.8\) in, \(P_1 = 12.2\) psia, \(P_2 = 11.8\) psia, \(\rho = 0.075\) lbm/ft³. Assume ideal flow, \(C_v = 1\).

📋 Formulas Needed

  1. Continuity — \(Q = A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2\) → sheet #18
  2. Venturi flow rate — \(Q = C_v A_2\sqrt{2(P_1-P_2)/[\rho(1-(A_2/A_1)^2)]}\) → sheet #20
1
Throat area from the diameter
\[ A_2 = \frac{\pi d_2^2}{4} = \frac{\pi (1.8/12)^2}{4}\ \text{ft}^2 \]

and the area ratio simplifies to \((A_2/A_1)^2 = (d_2/d_1)^4 = (1.8/2.6)^4\).

2
Assemble the flow-rate expression
\[ Q = \frac{\pi (1.8/12)^2}{4}\sqrt{\frac{2(12.2 - 11.8)}{(0.075)\,[\,1 - (1.8/2.6)^4\,]}}\left(\frac{144\ \text{lbf/ft}^2}{1\ \text{psi}}\right)\left(\frac{32.2\ \text{lbm}\!\cdot\!\text{ft/s}^2}{1\ \text{lbf}}\right) \]
3
Evaluate

The two conversion factors turn psi and lbm into a consistent set, and the bracket \(1-(1.8/2.6)^4 = 0.770\) accounts for the area change. Carrying the arithmetic through:

\[ Q \approx 4.48\ \text{ft}^3/\text{s} \]
✓
Answer
\(Q = 4.48\) ft³/s.
⚠
Common trap
In BG units the density is in lbm but the pressure force is in lbf. They differ by \(g_c = 32.2\) lbm·ft/(lbf·s²) — forget that factor and the answer is off by \(\sqrt{32.2}\). Also convert psi to lbf/ft² with the factor 144, and inches to feet before squaring.

Concept bridge: continuity (sheet #18) combined with the Venturi flow rate (sheet #20), all in BG units.

Orifice meter

An orifice meter is just a thin plate with a hole, clamped between two pipe flanges. It is far cheaper than a Venturi, but the sharp edge makes the jet separate and the device has a much higher permanent pressure loss.

The jet keeps contracting after the hole to a minimum area \(A_v\) (the vena contracta) before spreading out into recirculating eddies. The pressure drop is read across the plate.
Contraction and discharge coefficients

Because the jet contracts, the effective flow area is not the hole area \(A_0\) but the smaller vena-contracta area \(A_v\). Their ratio is the coefficient of contraction:

\[ C_c = \frac{A_v}{A_0} \approx 0.6, \qquad A_2 = C_c A_0 \]

Substituting \(A_2 = C_c A_0\) into the Venturi result and folding the friction correction \(C_v\) and the contraction \(C_c\) into a single discharge coefficient \(C_D = C_c C_v\) gives the orifice flow rate. \(C_D\) is fixed by experiment or by ASME standards.

Orifice flow rate
\[ Q = C_D A_0\sqrt{\dfrac{2\,(P_1 - P_2)}{\rho\left(1 - C_c^2 (A_0/A_1)^2\right)}} \]
Q — volumetric flow rate (m³/s) Cᵀ — discharge coefficient = CᶜCᵛ (−) Cᶜ — contraction coeff ≈ 0.6 (−) A₀ — orifice area (m²) A₁ — pipe area (m²) P₁−P₂ — pressure drop (Pa)

Bell-mouth inlet

A bell-mouth inlet is a smooth, curved (trumpet-shaped) entrance that guides flow gently into a pipe or duct. Because the contraction is gradual there is essentially no jet contraction — the contraction coefficient is \(C_c = 1\) — and over the very short inlet length the flow is effectively one-dimensional and frictionless. That makes the inlet itself a flow-measurement device.

Point A sits in the still air far upstream (\(V \approx 0\), \(P = P_{atm}\)); point B is just inside the pipe where the flow has reached speed \(V_B\). A static tap reads \(P_B\).
Derivation — bell-mouth velocity

Bernoulli along a streamline from the still upstream point (subscript 2 here, \(V_2 = 0\), \(P_2 = P_{atm}\)) to the pipe point (subscript 1), with \(z_1 = z_2\):

\[ P_2 + \tfrac12\rho V_2^2 = P_1 + \tfrac12\rho V_1^2 \;\Rightarrow\; P_{atm} = P_1 + \tfrac12\rho V_1^2 \] \[ V_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2\,(P_{atm} - P_1)}{\rho}} \]

Identical in form to the Pitot result — the “stagnation” point here is simply the still atmosphere outside the inlet.

Worked Example 5 — bell-mouth wind tunnel

Example 5 — find the manometer deflection h

Air is drawn into a wind tunnel through a bell-mouth inlet for testing automobiles. The air speed in the tunnel is 60 mph. A water manometer, with a 1-inch column of oil (\(SG = 0.9\)) on the open leg, reads the pressure drop. Take \(\rho_{air} = 0.00238\) slug/ft³ and \(\rho_{water} = 1.94\) slug/ft³. Determine the deflection \(h\).

The low static pressure inside the tunnel pulls the water up by \(h\) against atmospheric pressure and a 1-inch oil column on the open leg.
🧠 Thought process

Two stages. First, Bernoulli from the still air (point 1, \(V = 0\), \(P = P_{atm}\)) to the tunnel interior (point 2, \(V_2 = 88\) ft/s) gives the pressure drop \(P_1 - P_2 = \tfrac12\rho_{air}V_2^2\). Second, a manometer balance converts that pressure drop into the column height \(h\).

Watch the units: speed must be in ft/s (60 mph = 88 ft/s) and densities in slug/ft³ so that \(\tfrac12\rho V^2\) comes out in lbf/ft² directly — no \(g_c\) needed in the slug system. The oil column partly offsets the water column, so it is subtracted in the balance.

▸ Show full solution

Given: \(V_2 = 60\) mph \(= 88\) ft/s, \(\rho_{air} = 0.00238\) slug/ft³, \(\rho_{water} = 1.94\) slug/ft³, oil \(SG = 0.9\), oil column \(= 1\) in. \(z_1 = z_2\), \(V_1 = 0\).

📋 Formulas Needed

  1. Bell-mouth / stagnation — \(P_1 - P_2 = \tfrac12\rho V_2^2\) → sheet #17
  2. Manometer balance — \(\Delta P = \sum \gamma_i \Delta h_i\) → sheet #11
1
Pressure drop from Bernoulli
\[ P_1 - P_2 = \tfrac12\rho_{air}V_2^2 = \tfrac12 (0.00238)(88)^2 \] \[ P_1 - P_2 = 9.22\ \text{lbf/ft}^2 \]
2
Manometer balance

Balancing the columns (water of height \(h\) minus the 1-inch oil column), with \(\gamma = \rho g\) and \(g = 32.2\) ft/s²:

\[ 9.22 = \rho_{water}\,g\,h - \rho_{oil}\,g\,(1\ \text{in}) \]

where \(\rho_{oil} = 0.9\,\rho_{water} = 1.746\) slug/ft³ and 1 in \(= 0.0833\) ft.

3
Solve for h
\[ (1.94)(32.2)\,h = 9.22 + (1.746)(32.2)(0.0833) \] \[ 62.5\,h = 9.22 + 4.68 = 13.90 \;\Rightarrow\; h = 0.2226\ \text{ft} \]
✓
Answer
\(h = 0.2226\) ft \(= 2.67\) in.
⚠
Common trap
Keep everything in slugs and feet: in that system \(\tfrac12\rho V^2\) is already lbf/ft² with no \(g_c\). Convert 60 mph to 88 ft/s and the 1-inch oil column to feet, and remember the oil column subtracts from the water column in the balance.

Concept bridge: the bell-mouth/stagnation drop (sheet #17) feeds the manometer equation from Ch 1 (sheet #11).

Choosing a meter

★ Venturi vs orifice vs bell-mouth
  • Venturi — most accurate and most efficient (smooth contraction, very low permanent loss), but the bulkiest and most expensive to build. Uses \(C_v\) only.
  • Orifice — cheapest and most compact, but the sharp edge causes a vena contracta and a large permanent pressure loss. Uses \(C_D = C_c C_v\) with \(C_c \approx 0.6\).
  • Bell-mouth inlet — a smooth intake that doubles as a flow meter; no contraction (\(C_c = 1\)), so the velocity follows directly from \(P_{atm} - P_1\).
⚑
Lecture 3.2 in one line
Every flow meter is Bernoulli in disguise: bring the flow to rest (Pitot/Pitot-static) to get \(V = \sqrt{2(P_0-P_1)/\rho}\), or squeeze it through an area change (Venturi/orifice/bell-mouth) and pair Bernoulli with continuity to get \(Q\). Real devices need a coefficient — \(C_v\) for a Venturi, \(C_D = C_c C_v\) for an orifice, and \(C_c = 1\) for a smooth bell-mouth.

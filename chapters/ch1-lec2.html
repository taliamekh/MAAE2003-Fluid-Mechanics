1.2 — Units, Dimensional Analysis & Fluid Properties

Dimensions and Units

To describe the physical state of a fluid quantitatively, we need two concepts: dimensions (the measure by which a physical variable is expressed) and units (a particular way of attaching a number to that dimension). We use curly braces { } to denote dimensions and square brackets [ ] to denote units.

Dimension

The measure by which a physical variable is expressed quantitatively. For example, the dimension of the height of a fluid container is Length {L}.

Unit

A particular way of attaching a number to a quantitative dimension. For example, the unit of the dimension of length in the metric system is the meter [m].

Primary Dimensions

In fluid mechanics, four primary (independent) dimensions form the foundation from which all other quantities are derived:

DimensionSymbol

Temperature{Θ}

A fifth primary dimension, Electric Current {I}, exists for electrically conducting fluids like liquid metals and electrolytes, but it is not used in this course. All other physical properties are derived from these four primaries. For example, force comes from Newton’s second law:

Other Unit Systems

The CGS (Centimeter-Gram-Second) system uses cm, g, and s as primary units. Its unit of force is the dyne: \(1\;\text{dyne} = 1\;\text{g}\!\cdot\!\text{cm}\!\cdot\!\text{s}^{-2} = 10^{-5}\;\text{N}\). CGS requires no proportionality constants, but its units are too small for most engineering applications — the weight of a nickel is about 4900 dynes!

The English Engineering System (lbf, lbm, ft, s, °R) uses a proportionality constant \(g_c\) in Newton’s second law: \(\mathbf{F} = \frac{m \cdot \mathbf{a}}{g_c}\) where \(g_c = 32.174\;\text{lbm/slug}\). This extra constant is why we prefer SI and BG — they keep \(\mathbf{F} = m \cdot \mathbf{a}\) clean. The relation is \(1\;\text{slug} = 32.174\;\text{lbm}\).

Consistency and Homogeneity

★ Three Rules for Unit Consistency

Rule 1: Never mix BG and SI units. Select one system and convert everything to it before computing.
Example: \(1\;\text{slug} + 1\;\text{kg} \to 14.5939\;\text{kg} + 1\;\text{kg} = 15.5939\;\text{kg}\)

Rule 2: Never mix prefixes when adding or subtracting. Convert to the same power of 10.
Example: \(0.1\;\text{MPa} + 10{,}000\;\text{Pa} = 100{,}000\;\text{Pa} + 10{,}000\;\text{Pa} = 110{,}000\;\text{Pa}\)

Rule 3: Never use dimensionally inconsistent equations. Adding force and velocity is nonsense:
\(\{F\} + \{V\} = \text{nonsense} \equiv \{MLT^{-2}\} + \{LT^{-1}\}\)

Principle of Dimensional Homogeneity

Every term in a valid equation must have the same dimensions. Consider Bernoulli’s equation (derived in Ch 3):

Dimensional Check — Bernoulli’s Equation

\[ p + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2 + \rho g Z = \text{constant} \]

Check each term:

\(\{p\} = \{ML^{-1}T^{-2}\}\)

\(\{\tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2\} = \{ML^{-3}\} \cdot \{LT^{-1}\}^2 = \{ML^{-1}T^{-2}\}\) ✓

\(\{\rho g Z\} = \{ML^{-3}\} \cdot \{LT^{-2}\} \cdot \{L\} = \{ML^{-1}T^{-2}\}\) ✓

All terms have dimensions \(\{ML^{-1}T^{-2}\}\) — dimensionally consistent.

Some manufacturer-quoted formulas appear dimensionally inconsistent at first glance. For example, a valve flow coefficient \(Q = C_V \sqrt{\Delta p / SG}\) looks inconsistent because \(C_V\) carries hidden dimensions \(\{L^{7/2} M^{-1/2}\}\) to make the equation work. Always check whether “constants” carry dimensions.

Fluid Properties: Overview

Fluid properties are classified into two categories:

Extensive Properties

Properties that depend on the total amount of fluid (total mass of the system). Typically represented by uppercase letters. Examples: Mass \(M\), Weight \(W\), Volume \(\mathcal{V}\).

Intensive Properties

Properties that are independent of the amount of fluid — they describe the state at a point. Typically represented by lowercase letters. Examples: Density \(\rho\), Pressure \(p\), Temperature \(T\).

Pressure and Stress

Stress is force per unit area. There are two kinds relevant to fluids:

Normal stress acts perpendicular to a surface. Tensile stress (pulling apart): \(\sigma_{\text{tensile}} = +F/A\). Compressive stress (pushing together): \(\sigma_{\text{compressive}} = -F/A\). Recall from Sec. 1.1 that fluids cannot resist shear stresses — they deform continuously under any applied shear \(\tau = F/A\).

Pressure

Pressure is the compressive normal force per unit area acting on a fluid element. Using the continuum assumption, at a point:

\[ p = \lim_{\Delta A \to 0} \frac{\Delta F}{\Delta A} = \frac{dF}{dA} \]

For a finite area with uniform pressure: \(p_{\text{avg}} = F/A\)

p = pressure F = normal force A = area SI: Pa = N/m² BG: psf = lbf/ft² or psi = lbf/in²
★ Pressure in a Static Fluid

In a static fluid, pressure acts equally in all directions at a point (Pascal’s law). The shear stress is zero: \(\tau = 0\). Pressure is a scalar — it has magnitude but no direction. The compressive normal stress equals the negative of pressure: \(-\sigma = p\).

Density

Density

\[ \rho = \frac{m}{V} \]

Mass of the fluid contained in a unit volume.

ρ = density m = mass V = volume SI: kg/m³ BG: slugs/ft³

Liquids are nearly incompressible — density varies little with pressure. It varies slightly more with temperature: \(\rho_{\text{water}}\) at 4°C is 1000 kg/m³ but drops to 958.1 kg/m³ at 100°C. For small temperature ranges, density change is typically negligible.

Gases are compressible — their density changes significantly with both pressure and temperature. The ideal gas law (covered later in this lecture) governs this relationship.

Specific Weight

Specific Weight

\[ \gamma = \frac{W}{V} = \rho g \]

Weight of the fluid per unit volume. Since \(W = mg\) and \(V = m/\rho\), we get \(\gamma = \rho g\).

γ = specific weight W = weight ρ = density g = gravitational acceleration SI: N/m³ BG: lbf/ft³

Density and specific weight are easy to mix up. Density \(\rho\) is mass per unit volume, while specific weight \(\gamma\) is weight — the force of gravity — per unit volume; the two are linked by \(\gamma = \rho g\). Reach for \(\rho\) whenever you need mass (as in \(F = ma\) or momentum), and for \(\gamma\) whenever you need the gravitational force the fluid exerts (as in the hydrostatic pressure \(\gamma h\) of Ch 2).

Because \(\gamma\) carries the \(g\) in \(\rho g\), it depends on where you are: the same water has \(\gamma \approx 9.81\;\text{kN/m}^3\) on Earth but only about \(1.6\;\text{kN/m}^3\) on the Moon, even though its density \(\rho = 1000\;\text{kg/m}^3\) is unchanged. Density is a property of the fluid alone; specific weight is a property of the fluid and the local gravity.

Specific Gravity

Specific Gravity

\[ SG = \frac{\rho}{\rho_W} = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma_W} \]

Ratio of a fluid’s density to that of the reference: water at 4°C and 101.3 kPa. \(\rho_W = 1000\;\text{kg/m}^3 \equiv 62.4\;\text{lbf/ft}^3\)

SG = specific gravity (dimensionless) ρW = density of water at 4°C
★ Specific Gravity in Practice

SG is dimensionless — the same number in SI and BG. \(SG > 1\) means the fluid is denser than water (it sinks); \(SG < 1\) means it floats. Mercury: \(SG \approx 13.6\); gasoline: \(SG \approx 0.68\).

Why bother with a ratio instead of just quoting density? Because \(SG\) is a single number that stays the same in every unit system, it doubles as a shortcut for recovering the actual density (or specific weight) in whatever units you are working in: just multiply by water's value, \(\rho = SG \cdot \rho_{\text{water}}\) and \(\gamma = SG \cdot \gamma_{\text{water}}\). It also gives an instant float test — anything with \(SG < 1\) floats on water. (The reference is water at 4°C because that is where water is densest, which makes it a fixed, repeatable benchmark.)

Bulk Modulus

Bulk Modulus

\[ E_V = -\frac{dp}{dV/V} \]

A measure of how much a fluid resists compression. As pressure increases (\(dp > 0\)), volume decreases (\(dV < 0\)), so the negative sign makes \(E_V\) positive. Has dimensions of pressure.

EV = bulk modulus dp = change in pressure dV/V = volumetric strain SI: Pa

Liquids have very high \(E_V\) — nearly incompressible. Gases have very low \(E_V\) — easily compressed. This is why we often treat liquids as incompressible (\(\rho = \text{const}\)) in this course.

Read the formula this way: \(E_V\) is the pressure rise needed to squeeze out a given fractional change in volume (the volumetric strain \(dV/V\) is simply that fractional change). A large \(E_V\) therefore means the fluid barely budges under pressure. For water \(E_V \approx 2.2\;\text{GPa}\), so compressing it by even 1% takes about \(22\;\text{MPa}\) — roughly 220 atmospheres — which is exactly why we can treat liquids as incompressible.

Temperature

Temperature Conversions

\[ {^\circ\text{R}} = {^\circ\text{F}} + 459.69 \qquad \text{K} = {^\circ\text{C}} + 273.16 \qquad {^\circ\text{C}} = ({^\circ\text{F}} - 32) \times \tfrac{5}{9} \]

K and °R are absolute scales 0 K = absolute zero = 0 °R

Viscosity

Recall from Sec. 1.1 that fluids cannot resist shear forces — they deform continuously. But how fast they deform depends on the fluid. Water layers slide past each other quickly under shear; maple syrup layers move much more slowly. The property that quantifies this resistance to flow is viscosity.

Imagine a fluid confined between a fixed lower surface and a moving upper plate. When a force \(F\) is applied to the plate, it drags the fluid along. The fluid in contact with the moving plate moves at the plate’s velocity (\(u = V\) at the top), while the fluid in contact with the fixed surface is stationary (\(u = 0\) at the bottom). This creates a velocity gradient across the gap — thin layers of fluid slide over each other. Viscosity causes internal friction, leading to energy loss.

Newton’s law of viscosity. Left: deformation of a fluid element under shear. Center: velocity profile u(y) between plates. Right: Couette flow setup with no-slip condition at both surfaces.

The shear angle \(\delta\theta\) relates to velocity displacement: \(\delta\theta \approx \tan\delta\theta = \delta u \cdot \delta t / \delta y\). Taking limits gives the strain rate \(d\theta/dt = du/dy\). The shear stress is proportional to this strain rate:

Newton’s Law of Viscosity

\[ \tau = \mu \frac{du(y)}{dy} \]

The shear stress in a fluid is proportional to the velocity gradient (rate of shear strain). The constant of proportionality \(\mu\) is the dynamic (absolute) viscosity.

τ = shear stress μ = dynamic viscosity du/dy = velocity gradient (strain rate) SI: N·s/m² = Pa·s BG: lbf·s/ft²

It helps to read \(du/dy\) physically: it is how quickly the fluid's velocity changes as you move across the gap — geometrically, the slope of the velocity profile \(u(y)\). A steeper profile (a large velocity change packed into a small distance) means a larger \(du/dy\), and therefore a larger shear stress. When the profile is a straight line — as it is between two closely spaced plates — that slope is simply the total velocity change over the gap, \(du/dy = V/h\), which is why the worked example below can use \(V/h\) directly.

No-Slip Condition

At any solid boundary, the fluid velocity equals the surface velocity. At a stationary wall: \(u = 0\). At a moving plate with speed \(V\): \(u = V\). This is a fundamental boundary condition in fluid mechanics, observed experimentally for all viscous fluids in contact with solid surfaces.

Newtonian vs Non-Newtonian Fluids

Newtonian Fluid

A fluid with constant viscosity regardless of the applied shear stress. The \(\tau\) vs \(du/dy\) plot is a straight line through the origin with slope \(\mu\). Examples: air, water, gasoline, mercury, crude oil. The steeper the line, the higher the viscosity and the harder the fluid is to move.

Classification of fluid behavior. Newtonian fluids: linear τ vs dθ/dt. Non-Newtonian: dilatant (slope increases), pseudoplastic (slope decreases), and Bingham plastic (yield stress required to start flow).
Non-Newtonian Fluids

Fluids with a nonlinear shear stress vs. strain rate relationship. The apparent viscosity (slope at any point) changes with shear rate:

Dilatant (shear-thickening): Viscosity increases with strain rate. Example: water with high sugar concentration.

Pseudoplastic (shear-thinning): Viscosity decreases with strain rate. Examples: ketchup, gelatin, blood.

Ideal Bingham plastic: Requires a yield stress before deformation begins, then behaves linearly. Example: toothpaste.

Effect of Temperature on Viscosity

Viscosity changes only slightly with pressure (generally neglected). It is strongly temperature-dependent:

Liquids: Viscosity decreases with temperature. Higher T increases molecular vibration, breaking intermolecular bonds and allowing layers to slide more easily.

Gases: Viscosity increases with temperature. Higher T brings weakly bonded molecules closer during collisions, enhancing momentum exchange between layers.

Variation of dynamic (absolute) viscosity with temperature at 1 atm. Liquids (top curves) decrease with T; gases (bottom curves) increase with T. Note the log scale.
Empirical Viscosity–Temperature Relations

Liquids (Andrade’s equation): \(\;\mu = B \, e^{C/T}\)

Gases (Sutherland equation): \(\;\mu = \dfrac{B \, T^{3/2}}{T + C}\)

where \(T\) is absolute temperature; \(B\) and \(C\) are constants determined from known viscosities at specific temperatures.

T = absolute temperature [K or °R] B, C = empirical constants (fluid-specific)

“Empirical” means these come from fitting curves to measured data, not from first-principles theory. In practice you measure \(\mu\) at two known temperatures, solve for that fluid's \(B\) and \(C\), and then use the formula to predict \(\mu\) at any other temperature. The forms also encode the physical trend from above: for a liquid the factor \(e^{C/T}\) shrinks as \(T\) rises (viscosity falls), while for a gas the \(T^{3/2}\) growth dominates (viscosity climbs).

Kinematic Viscosity

Kinematic Viscosity

\[ \nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho} \]

Called “kinematic” because force is not involved in its dimensions — only length and time.

ν = kinematic viscosity μ = dynamic viscosity ρ = density SI: m²/s BG: ft²/s
★ Dynamic vs Kinematic Viscosity

Dynamic viscosity \(\mu\) is used in Newton’s law of viscosity when shear forces matter. Kinematic viscosity \(\nu\) appears in dimensionless numbers like the Reynolds number \(Re = VL/\nu\) (Ch 4–6). Mercury has high \(\mu\) but low \(\nu\) because of its very high density.

Inviscid and Ideal Fluids

Inviscid Fluid

A fluid with zero viscosity (\(\mu = 0\)). Many engineering fluids have very low viscosity — at 20°C: \(\mu_{\text{water}} = 10^{-3}\;\text{N}\!\cdot\!\text{s/m}^2\), \(\mu_{\text{air}} = 1.81 \times 10^{-5}\;\text{N}\!\cdot\!\text{s/m}^2\).

Ideal Fluid

A fluid that is both inviscid (\(\mu = 0\)) and incompressible (\(\rho = \text{const}\)). This is the simplest fluid model and underlies Bernoulli’s equation (Ch 3).

These are modeling idealizations, not real fluids — no ordinary fluid truly has \(\mu = 0\). Their value is that when viscosity is very small, ignoring it makes the equations far simpler and still gives accurate answers in the bulk of the flow, away from solid surfaces. The catch is that viscosity always matters in a thin layer right next to a wall (the boundary layer of Ch 4–6), so the inviscid picture is an excellent approximation everywhere except there.

Worked Example: Determining Viscosity

Example — Viscosity from Plate Experiment

Problem: A plate moves at 0.6 mm/s with an applied force of 4 mN. Contact area is 0.5 m², gap is 4 mm. Assuming a linear velocity profile, find the viscosity.

Solution

Step 1 — Shear stress:

\[ \tau = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{4 \times 10^{-3}\;\text{N}}{0.5\;\text{m}^2} = 8.0 \times 10^{-3}\;\text{N/m}^2 \]

Step 2 — Velocity gradient (linear profile ⇒ constant):

\[ \frac{du}{dy} = \frac{V}{h} = \frac{0.6 \times 10^{-3}\;\text{m/s}}{4 \times 10^{-3}\;\text{m}} = 0.15\;\text{s}^{-1} \]

Step 3 — Apply Newton’s law:

\[ \mu = \frac{\tau}{du/dy} = \frac{8.0 \times 10^{-3}}{0.15} = \boxed{0.0533\;\text{N}\!\cdot\!\text{s/m}^2} \]

Surface Tension and Capillarity

Cohesion and Adhesion

Cohesion: The attractive force between molecules of the same liquid. Enables a liquid to maintain its form.

Adhesion: The attractive force between liquid molecules and the molecules of a different material (e.g., the container wall).

At a liquid surface, molecules are pulled inward by cohesive forces from neighbors below but have no liquid neighbors above. The net force is directed downward into the bulk, causing the surface to contract like a stretched elastic membrane.

Surface Tension

\[ \Upsilon = \frac{F}{L} \]

Force per unit length needed to separate surface molecules. Also interpreted as free-surface energy (work per increase in surface area). Temperature-dependent — \(\Upsilon\) decreases as temperature rises.

Υ = surface tension SI: N/m BG: lbf/ft

The two readings of \(\Upsilon\) — force per unit length and energy per unit area — describe the same thing, since \(\text{N/m}\) and \(\text{J/m}^2\) are identical units. Because the surface acts like a stretched skin that wants to shrink, a free blob of liquid pulls itself into the shape with the least surface area for its volume: a sphere. That is why raindrops and small droplets are round, and why \(\Upsilon \approx 0.073\;\text{N/m}\) for water is enough to hold up a floating paperclip or a water strider.

Pressure Inside a Liquid Droplet

Force balance on a half-droplet: internal pressure acts on the projected area πR², surface tension acts along the circumference 2πR.
Droplet and Bubble Pressure

From \(\sum F_x = 0\): \(\pi R^2 \Delta p - 2\pi R \Upsilon = 0\)

\[ \Delta p_{\text{droplet}} = \frac{2\Upsilon}{R} \qquad \Delta p_{\text{bubble}} = \frac{4\Upsilon}{R} \]

A bubble has two surfaces (inner and outer), hence double the droplet pressure.

Δp = pinternal − patmosphere R = radius Υ = surface tension

Here is where that force balance comes from: imagine slicing the droplet in half. The internal pressure pushes the two halves apart across the flat circular cut, whose area is \(\pi R^2\), giving an outward force \(\pi R^2 \Delta p\); surface tension holds them together all around the rim, whose length is \(2\pi R\), giving an inward pull \(2\pi R\,\Upsilon\). Setting the two equal gives \(\Delta p = 2\Upsilon/R\). The striking consequence is \(\Delta p \propto 1/R\): the smaller the droplet, the higher its internal pressure — which is why very small bubbles are hard to inflate and tend to collapse into larger ones.

Capillarity

Left: wetting liquid (water in glass) — adhesion > cohesion → concave meniscus, liquid rises. Right: non-wetting liquid (mercury) — cohesion > adhesion → convex meniscus, liquid falls.

The contact angle \(\theta\) is the angle the liquid's surface makes with the wall, and it encodes the cohesion-versus-adhesion balance shown above. If the liquid wets the wall (adhesion wins, \(\theta < 90°\)), then \(\cos\theta > 0\) and the liquid climbs — water in glass; if it does not wet the wall (cohesion wins, \(\theta > 90°\)), then \(\cos\theta < 0\) and the liquid is pushed down — mercury in glass. That single sign change is exactly why the formula below predicts a rise for water but a drop for mercury.

Capillary Height

Balancing surface tension (vertical component) against the fluid column weight:

\[ h = \frac{2\Upsilon \cos\theta}{\gamma R} \]

Positive (rises) for \(\theta < 90°\) (wetting); negative (falls) for \(\theta > 90°\) (non-wetting).

h = capillary rise height θ = contact angle Υ = surface tension γ = ρg = specific weight R = tube radius

The height formula is itself a force balance: surface tension pulls the liquid upward around the tube's rim with a vertical force \(2\pi R\,\Upsilon\cos\theta\), and this raises the column until its weight \(\gamma(\pi R^2 h)\) matches. Solving \(2\pi R\,\Upsilon\cos\theta = \gamma\,\pi R^2 h\) for \(h\) cancels the \(\pi R\) and leaves \(h = 2\Upsilon\cos\theta/(\gamma R)\). The takeaway is \(h \propto 1/R\): the narrower the tube, the higher the liquid climbs — which is why capillary rise is dramatic in fine tubes and has to be corrected for in thin pressure-measurement tubes.

Example — Capillary Rise

Water–air–glass (\(R = 1\) mm, \(\theta \approx 0°\), \(\Upsilon = 0.073\) N/m, \(\rho = 1000\) kg/m³):

\[ h = \frac{2(0.073)(\cos 0°)}{(1000)(9.81)(0.001)} = 0.015\;\text{m} = 1.5\;\text{cm (rises)} \]

Mercury–air–glass (\(\theta = 130°\), \(\Upsilon = 0.48\) N/m, \(\rho = 13{,}600\) kg/m³):

\[ h = \frac{2(0.48)(\cos 130°)}{(13{,}600)(9.81)(0.001)} = -0.0046\;\text{m} = -0.46\;\text{cm (falls)} \]

These capillary effects must be corrected when using small-diameter tubes for pressure measurements (Ch 2).

Ideal Gas Law

Ideal Gas Law

\[ p = \rho R T \]

Relates pressure, density, and absolute temperature for an ideal gas. \(R\) is the specific gas constant (gas-dependent).

p = absolute pressure [Pa] ρ = density [kg/m³] R = specific gas constant [J/(kg·K)] T = absolute temperature [K]

This is the same ideal gas law you met in chemistry as \(pV = nRT\), just rewritten for fluid mechanics. Dividing through by volume turns \(n/V\) into density, and the molar gas constant becomes a specific constant for each gas, \(R = R_u/M\) (the universal constant \(R_u = 8.314\) J/(mol·K) divided by the molar mass \(M\)) — which is why \(R\) is different for every gas. Two cautions: \(T\) and \(p\) must be absolute (kelvin and absolute pressure, never °C or gauge pressure), and the law works best for gases at ordinary temperatures and low-to-moderate pressures, far from condensing into a liquid.

Example — Ideal Gas Law

Problem: 8 m³ of oxygen at 80 kPa and 15°C is compressed to 25 kPa at constant T. Find the new density and volume. (\(R_{O_2} = 259.8\) J/(kg·K))

Solution

Step 1: Initial density: \(\rho_1 = \frac{p_1}{RT_1} = \frac{80{,}000}{259.8 \times 288} = 1.069\;\text{kg/m}^3\)

Step 2: At constant T: \(\rho_2 = \rho_1 \cdot \frac{p_2}{p_1} = 1.069 \times \frac{25}{80} = 0.334\;\text{kg/m}^3\)

Step 3: Mass conserved: \(m = \rho_1 V_1 = 1.069 \times 8 = 8.554\;\text{kg}\)

\[ V_2 = \frac{m}{\rho_2} = \frac{8.554}{0.334} = \boxed{25.6\;\text{m}^3} \]

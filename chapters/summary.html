Which Property Do I Need?

Fluid Property Selection

Need mass per unit volume? → Density: \(\rho = m/V\)
Need weight per unit volume? → Specific weight: \(\gamma = \rho g\)
Comparing to water (dimensionless)? → Specific gravity: \(SG = \rho / \rho_W\)
SG > 1? → sinks   |   SG < 1? → floats
Shear stress from velocity profile? → Newton’s law: \(\tau = \mu \, du/dy\) (Newtonian only)
Reynolds number / diffusivity? → Kinematic viscosity: \(\nu = \mu/\rho\)
Pressure inside a droplet/bubble? → \(\Delta p = 2\Upsilon/R\) (droplet) or \(4\Upsilon/R\) (bubble)
Gas density at given p and T? → Ideal gas law: \(\rho = p/(RT)\)

Ch 1 — Fluid Properties & Pressure

Ch 1 — Fluid Properties & Pressure

Key ideas:

  • → A fluid is any substance that deforms continuously under shear stress — it never reaches static equilibrium under shear
  • → The continuum assumption lets us treat density, pressure, and velocity as smooth functions of position, enabling calculus-based governing equations
  • → Every valid equation must be dimensionally homogeneous — every term has the same primary dimensions
  • → Viscosity measures resistance to shear deformation; surface tension measures resistance to surface stretching

When to use what:

  • → Need density? — \(\rho = m/V\)  (or ideal gas: \(\rho = p/(RT)\) for gases)
  • → Need weight per volume? — \(\gamma = \rho g\)
  • → Does it sink or float? — \(SG = \rho/\rho_W\); >1 sinks, <1 floats
  • → Shear stress on a surface? — \(\tau = \mu \, du/dy\) (linear velocity profile ⇒ constant gradient)
  • → Reynolds number? — use kinematic viscosity \(\nu = \mu/\rho\)
  • → Pressure inside a droplet? — \(\Delta p = 2\Upsilon/R\); bubble gets \(4\Upsilon/R\)
  • → Capillary rise in a tube? — \(h = 2\Upsilon\cos\theta / (\gamma R)\)
  • → Is the equation valid? — check dimensional homogeneity of every term

‏⚠ Traps:

  1. “Cannot resist shear” ≠ “has no viscosity.” Fluids resist the rate of deformation through viscosity — they just never stop deforming
  2. Never mix SI and BG units in the same calculation — convert everything to one system first
  3. Liquid viscosity decreases with temperature; gas viscosity increases — opposite trends, easy to swap
  4. Don’t confuse \(\rho\) with \(\gamma\) (differ by factor of \(g\)), or \(\mu\) with \(\nu\) (differ by factor of \(\rho\))
  5. Bubble pressure is 2× droplet pressure (two surfaces). Capillary height is negative for non-wetting fluids (\(\theta > 90°\))
  6. Ideal gas law requires absolute pressure and temperature — not gauge pressure or °C

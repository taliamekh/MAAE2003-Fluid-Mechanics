Which Tool Do I Use?

Fluid Property Selection

Need mass per unit volume? → Density: \(\rho = m/V\)
Need weight per unit volume? → Specific weight: \(\gamma = \rho g\)
Comparing to water (dimensionless)? → Specific gravity: \(SG = \rho / \rho_W\)
SG > 1? → sinks  |  SG < 1? → floats
Shear stress from velocity profile? → Newton: \(\tau = \mu \, du/dy\) (Newtonian only)
Reynolds number / diffusivity? → Kinematic viscosity: \(\nu = \mu/\rho\)
Pressure inside a droplet/bubble? → \(\Delta p = 2\Upsilon/R\) (droplet) or \(4\Upsilon/R\) (bubble)
Gas density at given p and T? → Ideal gas law: \(\rho = p/(RT)\)

Pressure & Manometry

Need pressure at a depth? → \(p = p_a + \gamma h\) (static, incompressible)
Converting gage ↔ absolute? → \(p_{\text{abs}} = p_{\text{gage}} + p_{\text{atm}}\)
Pressure difference via manometer? → Walk through: +γΔh down, −γΔh up
Multiple stacked fluids? → Sum γihi for each layer

Hydrostatic Forces on Surfaces

Total force on a flat submerged surface? → \(F_R = \gamma \bar{h} A\)
Where does the force act? → Center of pressure: \(y_{cp} = \bar{y} + I_{xx}/(\bar{y}A)\)
Curved surface? → Project onto horizontal & vertical planes, combine components
Equilibrium (hinge/stop/bolt forces)? → Draw FBD, sum moments about hinge using F_R at y_cp

Flow Measurement & Bernoulli

Relating P, V, z between two points on a streamline? → Bernoulli: \(P/\rho + V^2/2 + gz = \text{const}\)
Tank draining / free-jet exit speed? → Torricelli: \(V = \sqrt{2gz}\)
Speed from a Pitot / stagnation tube? → \(V = \sqrt{2(P_0 - P)/\rho}\)
Flow rate through a Venturi? → \(Q = C_v A_2\sqrt{\dfrac{2(P_1-P_2)}{\rho\,(1-(A_2/A_1)^2)}}\)  (\(C_v \approx 0.98\))
Flow rate through an orifice plate? → \(Q = C_D A_0\sqrt{\dfrac{2(P_1-P_2)}{\rho\,(1-C_c^2(A_0/A_1)^2)}}\)
Sharp orifice: \(C_c \approx 0.6\)  |  Bell-mouth inlet: \(C_c = 1\)
Relating velocities at two pipe sections? → Continuity: \(A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2\)

Fluid Kinematics & Rate of Flow

Velocity at fixed points / locating a stagnation point? → Eulerian field \(\vec{V} = u\,\vec{i} + v\,\vec{j} + w\,\vec{k}\); stagnation: set \(\vec{V} = 0\)
Following one particle instead? → that is the Lagrangian view
Discharge from a velocity profile? → \(Q = \int_A v_n\,dA\)  (only the component normal to \(dA\))
One representative speed for a profile? → Average velocity \(\bar{U} = Q/A\)  (laminar pipe: \(\bar{U} = V_{\max}/2\))
Mass per unit time? → \(\dot{m} = \rho Q = \rho \bar{U} A\)
Visualizing the flow? → streamline (tangent to \(\vec{V}\) now)  |  pathline (one particle’s track)  |  streakline (dye trace)  |  timeline (marked row)
Steady flow? → all three coincide
Steady or uniform? → steady = no change in time at a point (\(\partial/\partial t = 0\))  |  uniform = no change in space along the flow

Ch 1 — Fluid Properties & Pressure

Ch 1 — Fluid Properties & Pressure

Key ideas:

  • → A fluid deforms continuously under shear stress — it never reaches static equilibrium under shear
  • → The continuum assumption lets us treat ρ, p, V as smooth functions, enabling calculus-based equations
  • → Every valid equation must be dimensionally homogeneous — every term has the same primary dimensions
  • → Viscosity measures resistance to shear deformation; surface tension measures resistance to surface stretching
  • → Pressure in a static fluid increases linearly with depth: p = pa + γh
  • → Manometers measure pressure differences by walking through connected fluid columns

When to use what:

  • → Need density? — \(\rho = m/V\); for gases: \(\rho = p/(RT)\)
  • → Need weight per volume? — \(\gamma = \rho g\)
  • → Sinks or floats? — \(SG = \rho/\rho_W\); >1 sinks, <1 floats
  • → Shear stress? — \(\tau = \mu \, du/dy\) (linear profile ⇒ constant gradient)
  • → Reynolds number? — \(\nu = \mu/\rho\)
  • → Droplet pressure? — \(\Delta p = 2\Upsilon/R\); bubble: \(4\Upsilon/R\)
  • → Capillary rise? — \(h = 2\Upsilon\cos\theta / (\gamma R)\)
  • → Pressure at depth? — \(p = p_a + \gamma h\)
  • → Convert gage ↔ absolute? — \(p_{\text{abs}} = p_{\text{gage}} + p_{\text{atm}}\)
  • → Pressure difference via manometer? — Walk: +γΔh down, −γΔh up

⚠ Traps:

  1. “Cannot resist shear” ≠ “has no viscosity.” Fluids resist the rate of deformation — they just never stop deforming
  2. Never mix SI and BG units in the same equation
  3. Liquid viscosity decreases with T; gas viscosity increases — opposite trends
  4. Don’t confuse ρ with γ (differ by g), or μ with ν (differ by ρ)
  5. Bubble pressure is 2× droplet (two surfaces). Capillary h is negative for non-wetting (θ > 90°)
  6. Ideal gas law needs absolute p and T — never gauge or °C
  7. Manometer sign convention: going DOWN in a fluid ADDS γΔh. Easy to reverse
  8. pgage can be negative (vacuum). pabs is never negative

Ch 2 — Hydrostatic Forces & Buoyancy

Ch 2 — Hydrostatic Forces & Buoyancy

Key ideas:

  • → Hydrostatic pressure on a submerged surface varies linearly with depth — the resultant force equals the pressure at the centroid times the area
  • → The center of pressure is always below the centroid — the offset depends on the shape’s second moment of area
  • → For curved surfaces, decompose into horizontal and vertical components using projected areas

When to use what:

  • → Total force on a flat surface? — \(F_R = \gamma \bar{h} A\)
  • → Where does FR act? — \(y_{cp} = \bar{y} + I_{xx}/(\bar{y}A)\)
  • → Lateral offset? — \(x_{cp} = \bar{x} + I_{xy}/(\bar{y}A)\) (for asymmetric shapes)
  • → Moment about a hinge? — Draw FBD, use FR at ycp, sum moments
  • → Gage pressure simplification? — Set pa = 0, then \(\bar{h}\) is depth below liquid surface
  • → Curved surface, horizontal force? — Flat-plate force on the vertical projection: \(F_H = (\gamma h_{CG} + p_a)A_{\text{proj}}\)
  • → Curved surface, vertical force? — Weight of fluid above (real or imaginary): \(F_V = \gamma\forall_{\text{above}}\) (+ \(p_aA\))
  • → Curved surface, resultant & angle? — \(F = \sqrt{F_H^2 + F_V^2}\), \(\theta = \tan^{-1}(F_V/F_H)\)
  • → Where do they act? — \(x_{CP} = \sum W_ix_i/F_V\) (for \(F_V\)); \(z_{CP} = h_{CG} + I_{xx}/(h_{CG}A)\) (for \(F_H\))

⚠ Traps:

  1. \(\bar{y}\) is measured along the inclined surface from the free-surface line, not vertically — vertical depth is \(\bar{h}\) = \(\bar{y}\) sinθ
  2. Ixx must be about the centroidal axis of the shape — don’t use the parallel axis value
  3. Center of pressure approaches centroid as depth increases (offset ∝ 1/\(\bar{y}\)) — deep surfaces have negligible offset
  4. On a curved surface, \(F_H\) uses the projected area and \(F_V\) uses a volume (weight of fluid) — never multiply a pressure by the curved area
  5. Get the direction of \(F_V\) right: fluid physically above pushes down; fluid below pushes up (imaginary column / buoyancy)
  6. In a pressurised chamber, keep \(p_a\) in \(F_H\); for \(F_V\) on a symmetric surface (e.g. hemisphere) uniform \(p_a\) cancels vertically

Ch 3 — Bernoulli’s Equation

Ch 3 — Bernoulli’s Equation

Key ideas:

  • → Bernoulli is the steady, incompressible, frictionless energy balance along a single streamline: \(P/\rho + V^2/2 + gz\) stays constant
  • → It is the steady-flow Euler equation integrated along a streamline — so every restriction on Euler carries over to Bernoulli
  • → In head form (÷ g) the three terms are the pressure, velocity and elevation heads; their sum is the EGL, and the HGL sits one velocity head \(V^2/2g\) below it
  • → Stagnation pressure \(P_0 = P + \tfrac12\rho V^2\) (static + dynamic) is the basis of velocity measurement
  • → The four flow meters (Pitot, Venturi, orifice, bell-mouth) are all Bernoulli + continuity with a calibration coefficient

When to use what:

  • → Pressure/speed/height on a streamline? — \(P/\rho + V^2/2 + gz = \text{const}\)
  • → Tank draining / free jet? — Torricelli: \(V = \sqrt{2gz}\)
  • → Speed from a Pitot tube? — \(V = \sqrt{2(P_0 - P)/\rho}\)
  • → Flow rate from a Venturi? — \(Q = C_v A_2\sqrt{2(P_1-P_2)/[\rho(1-(A_2/A_1)^2)]}\), \(C_v \approx 0.98\)
  • → Flow rate from an orifice? — \(Q = C_D A_0\sqrt{2(P_1-P_2)/[\rho(1-C_c^2(A_0/A_1)^2)]}\), \(C_D = C_c C_v\)
  • → Two pipe sections? — Continuity: \(A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2\)

⚠ Traps:

  1. Bernoulli holds along one streamline — only equate two points on the same streamline (or everywhere in irrotational flow)
  2. No friction, no pump/turbine, steady & incompressible — if any of these fails, use the steady-flow energy equation instead
  3. The jet area at a sharp orifice is the vena contracta \(C_c A_0\), not the hole \(A_0\) — ignoring contraction overestimates the flow
  4. Use the throat area for a Venturi (\(A_2\)), never the upstream pipe area
  5. The dynamic term \(\tfrac12\rho V^2\) assumes incompressible flow — it needs a correction at high Mach number
  6. Keep one datum for z; in BG units mind the \(g_c\) factor and convert psi to lbf/ft² (×144)

Ch 4 — Introduction to Fluid Dynamics

Ch 4 — Introduction to Fluid Dynamics

Key ideas:

  • → Fluid dynamics studies fluids in motion; kinematics describes the motion — velocity and acceleration — without asking what force causes it
  • → Two viewpoints: the Lagrangian view follows individual particles, while the Eulerian view records the velocity field \(\vec{V}(x,y,z,t)\) at fixed points in space — we almost always use Eulerian
  • → A stagnation point is where the flow comes to rest: set \(\vec{V} = 0\) and solve for position
  • → Flow lines: a streamline is tangent to \(\vec{V}\) everywhere at one instant; a pathline is the actual track of one particle; a streakline joins all particles that passed through one point (a dye trace); a timeline is a marked row of particles. In steady flow streamline, pathline and streakline coincide
  • → Flow is steady if nothing changes in time at a point (\(\partial/\partial t = 0\)) and uniform if nothing changes in space along the flow direction
  • → Rate of flow: volume flow \(Q = \int_A v_n\,dA = \bar{U}A\), average velocity \(\bar{U} = Q/A\), mass flow \(\dot{m} = \rho Q = \rho\bar{U}A\) — only the velocity normal to the area carries flow

When to use what:

  • → Velocity at fixed points / find a stagnation point? — Eulerian field \(\vec{V} = u\,\vec{i} + v\,\vec{j} + w\,\vec{k}\), stagnation at \(\vec{V} = 0\)
  • → Discharge from a velocity profile? — \(Q = \int_A v_n\,dA\) (integrate the normal component over the area)
  • → One representative speed for a profile? — \(\bar{U} = Q/A\) (laminar pipe: \(\bar{U} = V_{\max}/2\))
  • → Mass per unit time? — \(\dot{m} = \rho Q = \rho\bar{U}A\)
  • → Is the flow steady or uniform? — steady: \(\partial/\partial t = 0\) at a point; uniform: no change along the flow

⚠ Traps:

  1. Lagrangian \(\neq\) Eulerian — a pathline (one particle over time) is not a streamline (the whole field at one instant); they coincide only in steady flow
  2. Only the velocity component normal to \(dA\) carries flow — tangential velocity contributes nothing to \(Q\)
  3. Average velocity is not the maximum — for a laminar (parabolic) pipe profile \(\bar{U} = V_{\max}/2\) and \(Q = \tfrac12 V_{\max}\pi R^2\)
  4. Steady is about time, uniform is about space — a nozzle flow can be steady yet non-uniform
  5. \(\dot{m}\) carries the density: incompressible flow conserves \(Q\), but \(\dot{m} = \rho Q\) is what general (compressible) continuity conserves
  6. A streakline (dye injected at a fixed point) is not a pathline unless the flow is steady

Ch 5 — Reynolds Transport Theorem

Ch 5 — Reynolds Transport Theorem

Key ideas:

  • → The RTT rewrites any system law (mass, momentum, energy) in control-volume form: storage inside the CV + net flux through the CS
  • → For linear momentum: set \(B = m\vec{V}\), \(\beta = \vec{V}\) ⇒ \(\sum \vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\text{CV}} \vec{V}\rho\,d\forall + \int_{\text{CS}} \vec{V}\rho(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\,dA\)
  • → Steady ideal flow collapses to a discrete-port sum: \(\sum \vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = \sum \vec{V}\rho(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})A\)
  • → \(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n} < 0\) at inlets, \(> 0\) at outlets — the dot product handles signs automatically
  • → LHS from a free-body diagram: pressure + weight + normal + shear forces on the CV
  • → Moving CV at constant \(\vec{V}_b\): replace all velocities with \(\vec{V}_{f/b} = \vec{V}_{\text{flow}} - \vec{V}_b\)
  • → For angular momentum: set \(\beta = \vec{r} \times \vec{V}\) ⇒ \(\sum \vec{M}_{\text{ext}} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\text{CV}} (\vec{r}\times\vec{V})\rho\,d\forall + \int_{\text{CS}} (\vec{r}\times\vec{V})\rho(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\,dA\)
  • → Steady ideal angular momentum: \(\sum \vec{M}_{\text{ext}} = \sum(\vec{r}\times\vec{V})\dot{m}\) — only ports offset from the moment point contribute

When to use what:

  • → Force on a duct/nozzle/vane/gate? — draw CV, write \(\sum F = \sum V\rho(V\!\cdot\!n)A\)
  • → Multiple inlets/outlets? — use the discrete-port form with one term per port
  • → Simple one-in/one-out? — \(\sum F = v_{\text{out}}\dot{m}_{\text{out}} - v_{\text{in}}\dot{m}_{\text{in}}\)
  • → CV moves at constant speed? — use \(V_{f/b} = V_{\text{flow}} - V_b\) everywhere
  • → Sluice gate / open channel? — Bernoulli + continuity + momentum (three-tool combo)
  • → Need the moment/torque at a support? — angular momentum: \(\sum M = \sum(\vec{r}\times\vec{V})\dot{m}\)
  • → Tipping/overturning? — moment balance: weight stabilizes, momentum flux destabilizes

⚠ Traps:

  1. Two V’s in every flux term: the first carries momentum direction, the second (the dot product) carries the mass-flow sign — don’t confuse them
  2. Hydrostatic pressure forces on the CV faces still exist even in flowing problems — include them in the FBD (e.g. sluice gate \(F_A\), \(F_B\))
  3. The simplified \(\sum F = v_{\text{out}}\dot{m} - v_{\text{in}}\dot{m}\) is error-prone with angled outlets — use the full form for safety
  4. Moving-CV problems: \(V_{f/b}\) replaces V everywhere in the momentum equation, including inside the mass-flow dot product
  5. In ideal flow along a deflecting surface, speed stays the same (Bernoulli with same pressure) — only direction changes
  6. Angular momentum: if the inlet velocity passes through the moment point, its r×V is zero — only offset ports contribute moments
  7. Moment arm d is the perpendicular distance from the moment point to the line of action of the velocity, not the distance to the port center

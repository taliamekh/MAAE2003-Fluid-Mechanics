5.2 — Reynolds Transport Theorem: Linear Momentum

Newton’s 2nd law for a CV • Momentum equation • Steady-flow form • FBD approach • Moving CV • Four worked examples

Why Linear Momentum?

In Chapter 2 we computed forces on surfaces due to stationary fluids — hydrostatics. But what about a fire hose hitting a wall, a jet deflected by a vane, or water flowing under a sluice gate? These involve moving fluids that change velocity, and velocity changes require forces. The momentum equation lets us compute those forces without ever resolving the messy internal flow details — we only need what crosses the control surface.

★ Key Insight

The momentum equation is the most powerful problem-solving tool in this course. It replaces page-long pressure integrations with a simple bookkeeping exercise: draw a control volume, identify what flows in and out, and the forces fall out directly.

From Newton’s Second Law to the RTT

Recall from §5.1 that the RTT rewrites the rate of change of any extensive system property \(B\) in terms of a control volume. For linear momentum we set:

Momentum as the Extensive Property
\[ B = m\vec{V} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \beta = \frac{dB}{dm} = \vec{V} \]
\(B = m\vec{V}\) — total linear momentum of the system (kg·m/s) \(\beta = \vec{V}\) — velocity = momentum per unit mass (m/s)

Newton’s second law for a system (fixed collection of matter) says:

Newton’s Second Law (System Form)
\[ \sum \vec{F}_{\text{external}} = \frac{d(m\vec{V})}{dt}\bigg|_{\text{system}} \]
\(\sum \vec{F}_{\text{ext}}\) — vector sum of all external forces on the system \(d(m\vec{V})/dt\) — rate of change of the system’s momentum

This is valid only for a system — a fixed mass. To apply it to a control volume (where mass flows in and out), we substitute \(\beta = \vec{V}\) into the general RTT from §5.1:

The Linear Momentum Equation

Linear Momentum Equation (General Form)
\[ \boxed{\sum \vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\text{CV}} \vec{V}\,\rho\;d\forall \;+\; \int_{\text{CS}} \vec{V}\,\rho\,(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\;dA} \]
\(\sum \vec{F}_{\text{ext}}\) — sum of all external forces acting on the contents of the CV \(\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\int_{\text{CV}} \vec{V}\,\rho\;d\forall\) — local (storage) term: rate of change of momentum stored inside the CV \(\displaystyle\int_{\text{CS}} \vec{V}\,\rho\,(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\;dA\) — convective (flux) term: net momentum carried out through the CS \(\hat{n}\) — outward unit normal on the control surface
★ Three-Term Interpretation

Read the equation left to right: “Forces on the CV = rate the momentum inside the CV is growing + rate momentum is being carried out.” The LHS is forces (draw a free-body diagram). The first RHS term is zero for steady flow. The second RHS term is the momentum flux.

Steady-Flow Simplification

For steady flow (\(\partial/\partial t = 0\) everywhere), the storage term vanishes. Nothing inside the CV is changing with time — the same pattern repeats each instant:

Steady-Flow Linear Momentum
\[ \sum \vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = \int_{\text{CS}} \vec{V}\,\rho\,(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\;dA \]
Steady flow ⇒ storage term = 0 Only the momentum flux through the CS remains

If the flow is also incompressible and inviscid (ideal), the velocity is uniform across each inlet and outlet. The integral collapses to a sum over discrete ports:

Steady Ideal Flow — Discrete Form
\[ \sum \vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = \sum_{\text{ports}} \vec{V}\,\rho\,(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\,A \]
Each port contributes one term: the velocity vector \(\vec{V}\) times the mass flow rate \(\rho(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})A\) At an inlet: \(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n} < 0\) (flow opposes outward normal) ⇒ negative contribution At an outlet: \(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n} > 0\) (flow aligns with outward normal) ⇒ positive contribution

Tackling the Right-Hand Side (Momentum Flux)

Resolve the momentum equation into scalar x- and y-components. For each component, the flux term becomes:

x-Component Example
\[ \overset{+}{\rightarrow}\;\sum F_x = \sum V_x\;\rho\,(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\,A = (V_{\text{in}})_x\underbrace{(-\rho V_{\text{in}} A_{\text{in}})}_{\dot{m}_{\text{in}}} + (V_{\text{out}})_x\underbrace{(\rho V_{\text{out}} A_{\text{out}})}_{\dot{m}_{\text{out}}} \]
\(V_x\) — the x-component of the velocity at each port (carries the sign from geometry) \(\rho V A\) — the mass flow rate at each port (always positive as a magnitude) The sign of \(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n}\) handles inlet vs outlet automatically: negative at inlets, positive at outlets
★ Pay Attention to Signs

There are two velocity appearances in each term: \(\vec{V}\) outside (gives the direction of momentum) and \(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n}\) inside (gives the sign of mass flow). The first carries component signs from geometry. The second is negative at inlets (flow opposes the outward normal) and positive at outlets.

Tackling the Left-Hand Side (Free-Body Diagram)

The left-hand side is the vector sum of all external forces on the control volume. Start every problem by drawing a free-body diagram (FBD) of the CV:

External Forces on a CV
\[ \sum \vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = \vec{F}_{\text{pressure}} + \vec{F}_{\text{weight}} + \vec{F}_{\text{normal}} + \vec{F}_{\text{shear}} \]
\(\vec{F}_{\text{pressure}}\) — pressure forces at each inlet/outlet (push fluid into the CV) \(\vec{F}_{\text{weight}}\) — gravity (= \(W\) downward, often negligible for horizontal gas flows) \(\vec{F}_{\text{normal}}\) — support reactions from walls, flanges, bolts \(\vec{F}_{\text{shear}}\) — friction along walls (zero for inviscid/ideal flow assumptions)

Resolve these into x- and y-components and handle each direction independently. For many problems (jets hitting surfaces, nozzle reactions), pressure forces at open inlets/outlets to atmosphere are zero (gage pressure = 0), and for ideal flow shear forces are zero — leaving only the reaction force you’re solving for.

Free-body diagram of a curved duct CV: pressure forces at inlet/outlet, weight, normal reaction, and shear.
★ Why Momentum Is So Powerful

The momentum equation computes forces without resolving the details inside the CV. You could calculate the same result by integrating \(p \cdot dA\) and \(\tau \cdot dA\) around every surface inside the duct — but that would be enormously tedious. The momentum approach needs only the conditions at the inlets and outlets.

Oversimplified Form (One Inlet, One Outlet)

For the special case of steady, uniform, non-moving CV with one inlet and one outlet, the general equation collapses to:

Simplified Momentum (Single Inlet/Outlet)
\[ \sum \vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = \vec{v}_{\text{out}}\,\dot{m}_{\text{out}} - \vec{v}_{\text{in}}\,\dot{m}_{\text{in}} \]
\(\vec{v}_{\text{out}}\) — velocity vector at the outlet \(\vec{v}_{\text{in}}\) — velocity vector at the inlet \(\dot{m}_{\text{out}} = \rho V_{\text{out}} A_{\text{out}}\), \(\dot{m}_{\text{in}} = \rho V_{\text{in}} A_{\text{in}}\) — mass flow rates
★ Exam Warning

This simplified form works for simple one-inlet/one-outlet problems, but it is easy to make sign errors when there are multiple outlets, angled flows, or moving CVs. Dr. Bano recommends using the full formulas with the \(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n}\) sign convention for anything beyond the simplest cases. The full form handles signs automatically.

Problem-Solving Strategy

Momentum Problem Checklist

Step 1: Draw the CV and CS. Mark all inlets and outlets. Choose a coordinate system.

Step 2: Draw the FBD of the CV. Include pressure forces, weight, reaction forces, and shear (if viscous).

Step 3: Write continuity (\(\dot{m}_{\text{in}} = \dot{m}_{\text{out}}\)) to relate velocities and areas.

Step 4: Write the momentum equation in x and y components. Evaluate the \(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n}\) dot product at each port (negative at inlets, positive at outlets).

Step 5: Substitute known values and solve for the unknown forces.

Example 1: Fire Hose Hitting a Fixed Surface

Example — Fire Hose Splitting Flow

Water is discharged from a 1 in diameter nozzle at 180 gal/min. The flow strikes a fixed surface such that ¾ flows along path B (straight down, −y), while ¼ flows along path C (at 60° from the positive x-axis). The nozzle enters at 45° below the positive x-axis. Determine the x- and y-components of the resultant force on the surface. Assume ideal steady flow. \(\gamma_{\text{water}} = 62.4\;\text{lbf/ft}^3\).

CV around the deflecting surface: inlet A (45° below +x), outlet B (straight down), outlet C (60° above +x).
Solution

Step 1 — Flow rates and velocities.

Convert the discharge:

\[ Q_A = \frac{180\;\text{gal}}{\text{min}} \times \frac{1\;\text{min}}{60\;\text{s}} \times \frac{1\;\text{ft}^3}{7.48\;\text{gal}} = 0.4011\;\text{ft}^3/\text{s} \]

Nozzle area and velocity at A (diameter = 1 in = 1/12 ft):

\[ A_A = \pi\!\left(\frac{0.5}{12}\right)^{\!2} = 5.454 \times 10^{-3}\;\text{ft}^2 \] \[ V_A = \frac{Q_A}{A_A} = \frac{0.4011}{5.454 \times 10^{-3}} = 73.53\;\text{ft/s} \]

Flow splits: \(Q_B = \tfrac{3}{4}Q_A = 0.3008\;\text{ft}^3/\text{s}\), \(Q_C = \tfrac{1}{4}Q_A = 0.1003\;\text{ft}^3/\text{s}\).

For ideal flow (no friction losses), Bernoulli along each streamline from A → B and A → C with atmospheric pressure everywhere gives \(V_B = V_C = V_A = 73.53\;\text{ft/s}\).

Density: \(\rho = \gamma/g = 62.4/32.2 = 1.938\;\text{slug/ft}^3\).

Step 2 — Momentum equation, x-component.

Select CV around the surface. For steady ideal flow:

\[ \overset{+}{\rightarrow}\;\sum F_x = \sum V_x\;\rho\,(\vec{V}\cdot\hat{n})\,A \]

Evaluate each port. The inlet flow direction is at \(-45°\) (below the +x axis):

\[ -F_x = \underbrace{Q_B V_B \cos(-90°)}_{=\;0} + Q_C V_C \cos(60°) - Q_A V_A \cos(-45°) \] \[ -F_x = 0 + (0.1003)(73.53)\cos 60° - (0.4011)(73.53)\cos 45° \] \[ -F_x = 3.69 - 20.86 = -17.17 \]

Wait — let me redo this more carefully using the \(\rho\) factor. Each flux term is \(V_x \cdot \rho \cdot Q\):

\[ -F_x = \rho\bigl[Q_B V_B \cos(-90°) + Q_C V_C \cos(60°) - Q_A V_A \cos(45°)\bigr] \] \[ = 1.938\bigl[0 + (0.1003)(73.53)(0.5) - (0.4011)(73.53)(0.7071)\bigr] \] \[ = 1.938\bigl[3.688 - 20.86\bigr] = 1.938(-17.17) = -33.3\;\text{lb} \] \[ \boxed{F_x = 33.3\;\text{lb} \;\leftarrow} \]

Step 3 — y-component.

\[ F_y = \rho\bigl[Q_B V_B \sin(-90°) + Q_C V_C \sin(60°) - Q_A V_A \sin(-45°)\bigr] \] \[ = 1.938\bigl[(0.3008)(73.53)(-1) + (0.1003)(73.53)(0.8660) - (0.4011)(73.53)(-0.7071)\bigr] \] \[ = 1.938\bigl[-22.12 + 6.389 + 20.86\bigr] = 1.938(5.12) = 9.92\;\text{lb} \] \[ \boxed{F_y = 9.92\;\text{lb} \;\uparrow} \]

Resultant:

\[ |\vec{F}| = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2} = \sqrt{33.3^2 + 9.92^2} = \boxed{34.7\;\text{lb}} \]

Moving Control Volume at Constant Velocity

When the CV moves at a constant velocity \(\vec{V}_b\) (e.g. the CV is attached to a truck, boat, or vane), the momentum equation still holds but all velocities must be relative to the CV:

Steady Ideal Flow — Moving CV
\[ \sum \vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = \sum \vec{V}_{f/b}\;\rho\,(\vec{V}_{f/b}\cdot\hat{n})\,A \]
\(\vec{V}_{f/b} = \vec{V}_{\text{flow}} - \vec{V}_b\) — velocity of the fluid relative to the body/CV \(\vec{V}_{\text{flow}}\) — absolute velocity of the fluid (in the lab frame) \(\vec{V}_b\) — velocity of the body/CV (constant)
★ Relative Velocity Rule

Replace every \(\vec{V}\) in the momentum equation with \(\vec{V}_{f/b}\). The forces remain in the absolute frame (they don’t change), but the momentum fluxes are computed with the relative velocity because the CV’s mass flow depends on how fast the fluid enters/leaves relative to the moving surface.

Example 2: Force on a Windshield (Moving CV)

Example — Hose Hitting a Moving Truck

A hose with nozzle diameter \(D = 50\;\text{mm}\) discharges water at \(Q = 8\;\text{L/s}\). The stream hits the windshield of a truck moving at \(5\;\text{m/s}\) toward the hose. The deflected stream leaves at \(40°\) above the horizontal. Determine the force on the windshield. Assume steady ideal flow; \(\rho = 1000\;\text{kg/m}^3\).

Solution

Step 1 — Absolute velocity of the jet.

\[ V = \frac{Q}{\pi D^2/4} = \frac{8 \times 10^{-3}}{\pi(0.05)^2/4} = 4.074\;\text{m/s} \]

Step 2 — Relative velocity. Attach the CV to the truck (moving left at 5 m/s). The jet moves right at 4.074 m/s. In the truck’s frame:

\[ V_{f/A} = V_{\text{flow}} - V_b = 4.074 - (-5) = 9.074\;\text{m/s} \]

(The truck moves at \(-5\) m/s in our sign convention since it heads toward the hose.)

For ideal flow with equal inlet and outlet areas, \(V_{f/B} = V_{f/A} = 9.074\;\text{m/s}\).

Step 3 — x-momentum (moving CV).

\[ \sum F_x = V_{f/B}\cos 40°\;(\rho V_{f/B} A_B) - V_{f/A}\;(\rho V_{f/A} A_A) \]

With \(A_A = A_B = \pi(0.025)^2 = 1.9635 \times 10^{-3}\;\text{m}^2\):

\[ \sum F_x = (9.074\cos 40°)(1000)(9.074)(1.9635\times10^{-3}) - (9.074)(1000)(9.074)(1.9635\times10^{-3}) \] \[ = \rho\,V_{f}^{2}\,A\,(\cos 40° - 1) = (1000)(9.074)^2(1.9635\times10^{-3})(0.766 - 1) \] \[ = 161.7 \times (-0.234) = -37.83\;\text{N} \] \[ \boxed{F_x = 37.83\;\text{N}\;\leftarrow} \]

Step 4 — y-momentum.

\[ \sum F_y = V_{f/B}\sin 40°\;(\rho V_{f/B} A_B) = \rho\,V_{f}^{2}\,A\,\sin 40° \] \[ = (1000)(9.074)^2(1.9635\times10^{-3})(0.6428) = 103.9\;\text{N} \] \[ \boxed{F_y = 103.9\;\text{N}\;\uparrow} \]

Resultant:

\[ |\vec{F}| = \sqrt{37.83^2 + 103.9^2} = \boxed{111\;\text{N}} \]

Example 3: Concrete Flowing Into a Cart

Example — Cable Tension and Scale Reading

Concrete flows into a cart sitting on a scale. The concrete stream has density \(\rho = 1700\;\text{kg/m}^3\), cross-sectional area \(A = 0.12\;\text{m}^2\), and speed \(v_1 = 3\;\text{m/s}\), entering at angle \(\alpha = 60°\) below the horizontal. The cart plus concrete weighs \(W = 3600\;\text{N}\). Determine the tension in the cable and the weight recorded by the scale. Assume steady flow.

CV around the cart: concrete enters at angle α = 60°. FBD shows cable tension T, weight W, and normal force N from the scale.
Solution

The CV encloses the cart and concrete. There is one inlet (the stream) and no outlet (concrete accumulates, but we assume steady flow at this instant).

x-momentum:

\[ \sum F_x = \sum_{\text{CS}} v_x\;\rho\;\vec{v}\cdot\vec{A} \]

The only CS flux is at the inlet. The stream enters at angle \(\alpha = 60°\) below horizontal, so \(v_{1x} = v_1\cos\alpha\), and the dot product \(\vec{v}\cdot\hat{n}\) is \(-v_1\) (flow into the CV):

\[ T = -v_{1x} \cdot \rho \cdot v_1 \cdot A_1 = -v_1\cos\alpha \cdot \rho v_1 A_1 = -\rho A_1 v_1^2 \cos\alpha \] \[ T = -(1700)(0.12)(3)^2\cos 60° = -(1700)(0.12)(9)(0.5) = -918\;\text{N} \]

The negative sign means T acts opposite to our assumed direction — the cable is in tension (pulls the cart to the right, resisting the horizontal momentum of the incoming stream).

\[ \boxed{T = 918\;\text{N (tension)}} \]

y-momentum:

\[ N - W = +v_{1y}\;\rho\;v_1\;A_1 \]

The stream enters with a downward vertical component \(v_{1y} = -v_1\sin\alpha\), so the flux brings downward momentum into the CV:

\[ N - W = -(-v_1\sin\alpha)(\rho v_1 A_1) = +\rho A_1 v_1^2 \sin\alpha \]

Wait — let me be careful. The dot product at the inlet is \(\vec{v}\cdot\hat{n} = -v_1\) (inflow), so the y-flux is \(v_{1y}(-\rho v_1 A_1)\). With \(v_{1y} = -v_1 \sin\alpha\) (downward):

\[ N - W = (-v_1\sin\alpha)(-\rho v_1 A_1) = \rho A_1 v_1^2 \sin\alpha \] \[ N = W + \rho A_1 v_1^2 \sin\alpha = 3600 + (1700)(0.12)(9)\sin 60° = 3600 + 1590 = 5190\;\text{N} \] \[ \boxed{N = 5190\;\text{N} \;\text{(scale reads 5190 N)}} \]

The scale reads more than the weight because it also supports the downward momentum of the incoming concrete stream.

Example 4: Force on a Sluice Gate

Example — Sluice Gate with Bernoulli + Momentum

When sluice gate G is open, water flows out from under the gate at a depth of 2 ft. The gate is 5 ft wide. The channel behind the gate maintains a constant depth of 10 ft. Determine the horizontal force that must be applied to the gate to keep it in place. Assume ideal steady flow. \(\gamma = 62.4\;\text{lbf/ft}^3\).

Sluice gate: upstream depth 10 ft, downstream depth 2 ft, gate 5 ft wide. Bernoulli between ① and ② gives velocities; momentum gives the gate force.
Solution

This problem combines Bernoulli (to find velocities) with the momentum equation (to find the gate force). It also uses hydrostatics for the pressure forces on the CV faces.

Step 1 — Bernoulli between ① and ②.

Both free surfaces are at atmospheric pressure (\(p_1 = p_2 = 0\) gage). Take the datum at the channel bed:

\[ \frac{p_1}{\gamma} + \frac{V_A^2}{2g} + z_1 = \frac{p_2}{\gamma} + \frac{V_B^2}{2g} + z_2 \] \[ 0 + \frac{V_A^2}{2(32.2)} + 10 = 0 + \frac{V_B^2}{2(32.2)} + 2 \] \[ V_B^2 - V_A^2 = 2(32.2)(8) = 515.2\;\text{(ft/s)}^2 \]

Step 2 — Continuity.

Width \(w = 5\;\text{ft}\). Areas: \(A_A = 10 \times 5 = 50\;\text{ft}^2\), \(A_B = 2 \times 5 = 10\;\text{ft}^2\).

\[ \rho A_A V_A = \rho A_B V_B \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 50\,V_A = 10\,V_B \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_B = 5\,V_A \]

Step 3 — Solve for velocities.

Substitute \(V_B = 5V_A\) into Bernoulli:

\[ (5V_A)^2 - V_A^2 = 515.2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 24\,V_A^2 = 515.2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_A = 4.633\;\text{ft/s} \] \[ V_B = 5(4.633) = 23.17\;\text{ft/s} \]

Step 4 — Free-body diagram and momentum.

The CV encloses the water between section A and section B. External forces in the x-direction:

• \(F_A\) = hydrostatic force on upstream face = \(\frac{1}{2}\gamma h_A^2 w = \frac{1}{2}(62.4)(10)^2(5) = 15{,}600\;\text{lb}\) (pushing right)

• \(F_B\) = hydrostatic force on downstream face = \(\frac{1}{2}\gamma h_B^2 w = \frac{1}{2}(62.4)(2)^2(5) = 624\;\text{lb}\) (pushing left)

• \(F_G\) = force from the gate (pushing left, unknown)

x-momentum:

\[ \overset{+}{\rightarrow}\;\sum F_x = V_B(\rho V_B A_B) - V_A(\rho V_A A_A) \] \[ F_A - F_G - F_B = \rho(V_B^2 A_B - V_A^2 A_A) \]

With \(\rho = 62.4/32.2 = 1.938\;\text{slug/ft}^3\):

\[ \rho V_B^2 A_B = 1.938(23.17)^2(10) = 10{,}404\;\text{lb} \] \[ \rho V_A^2 A_A = 1.938(4.633)^2(50) = 2{,}080\;\text{lb} \] \[ 15{,}600 - F_G - 624 = 10{,}404 - 2{,}080 = 8{,}324 \] \[ F_G = 15{,}600 - 624 - 8{,}324 = \boxed{6{,}652\;\text{lb}} \]

(The small difference from the slide value of 6656 lb is due to rounding in intermediate steps.)

★ Sluice Gate Pattern

Sluice gate problems always combine three tools: (1) Bernoulli to relate velocities, (2) continuity to get velocities from depths, (3) momentum to find the gate force. The pressure forces on the CV faces come from hydrostatics (triangular pressure distributions). Watch for this pattern on the exam.

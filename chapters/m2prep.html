💡 Insider Tips

  • Every long-answer starts with a CV sketch. Draw the control volume, label all inlets/outlets, show pressure forces and the reaction force you're solving for. Partial marks come from this.
  • Bernoulli + Continuity are always paired. You'll never use Bernoulli alone — continuity gives you the velocity relationship, then Bernoulli connects pressures/elevations.
  • Hydrostatic pressure on a vertical surface = ρg·yc·A. The centroid depth yc is at half the water height. This substitution appears in every momentum problem involving open-channel flow.
  • Sign convention matters. Pick a positive direction, define FR as the force on the fluid (not the gate), then flip at the end. Getting this backwards is the #1 error.
  • Mass conservation ≠ volume conservation. Q1c proves this — the air and water have different densities, so equal volume flow rates give very different mass flow rates (ratio ≈ ρair/ρwater ≈ 1.2/1000).
  • Manometer reading: trace from one end to the other. Go step by step: +ρgh going down, −ρgh going up, switching ρ at each fluid interface. Don't try to shortcut it.

⚠️ Common Traps

  1. Forgetting the hydrostatic pressure term in momentum. The momentum equation gives \(\sum F = \dot{m}\Delta V\). The forces include both pressure forces on the CV surfaces and the reaction force. The pressure force on a vertical water surface is \(\frac{1}{2}\rho g w y^2\), not just \(p \cdot A\).
  2. Getting the sign of FR backwards. Define \(F_{Rx}\) as the force exerted on the fluid by the gate. After solving, if the result is positive, the actual force on the gate points in the opposite direction. State clearly what your sign means.
  3. Wrong manometer reading. Mixing up which direction adds or subtracts pressure. Rule: going DOWN in a fluid → pressure INCREASES (+ρgh). Going UP → pressure DECREASES (−ρgh). Switch ρ at each interface.
  4. Continuity ratio for circular pipes. \(V_1/V_2 = (D_2/D_1)^2\), NOT \(D_2/D_1\). The area scales with diameter squared.
  5. Assuming mass flow = volume flow. Q1c tests this directly. \(\dot{m} = \rho Q\), and since \(\rho_{\text{air}} \neq \rho_{\text{water}}\), equal volume flow rates give very different mass flow rates.
